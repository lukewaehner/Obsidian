# Direct vs Indirect Costs

Cost classification based on **traceability** to a cost object.

## Definitions

### Direct Costs
**Costs that can be easily, conveniently, and cost-effectively traced to a specific cost object.**

**Characteristics**:
- Clear, observable connection to the product/service
- Worth the effort to trace directly
- Usually variable with production volume

**Common Examples**:
- Direct Materials: Wood in a chair, flour in bread
- Direct Labor: Assembly worker wages, chef preparing meals

### Indirect Costs
**Costs that cannot be easily, conveniently, or cost-effectively traced to a specific cost object.**

**Characteristics**:
- Shared across multiple products/services
- Too costly or impractical to trace directly
- Often called **common costs**
- Require allocation methods

**Common Examples**:
- Factory rent (benefits all products)
- Factory supervisor salary (oversees all production)
- Factory utilities (powers all equipment)
- Manufacturing supplies (glue, oil, cleaning supplies)

## Cost Object Dependency

**Critical Insight**: Whether a cost is direct or indirect depends on the **cost object** being analyzed.

### Example: Regional Supervisor Salary

**Cost Object: Individual Store**
- Classification: **Indirect Cost**
- Reason: Supervisor oversees multiple stores
- Allocation needed to assign to specific store

**Cost Object: Region**
- Classification: **Direct Cost**
- Reason: Salary directly relates to managing that region
- No allocation needed

### Example: Shipping Costs

**Cost Object: Customer Order**
- Classification: **Direct Cost**
- Reason: Specific shipment for that order

**Cost Object: Product Line**
- Classification: **Indirect Cost** (if shipped with other products)
- Reason: Shipment includes multiple product lines

## Context Matters

The same cost can shift classification based on:

1. **Level of Analysis**
   - Department level vs company level
   - Product level vs product line level
   - Individual unit vs batch

2. **Information Systems**
   - Better tracking systems → more direct costs
   - Poor tracking → more indirect costs

3. **Cost-Benefit Trade-off**
   - Is tracing worth the effort?
   - Does precision improve decisions?

## Manufacturing Context

### Direct Manufacturing Costs
```
Direct Materials (DM)
+
Direct Labor (DL)
─────────────────────
= Prime Costs
```

### Indirect Manufacturing Costs
```
Manufacturing Overhead (MOH):
  - Factory rent
  - Factory utilities
  - Depreciation on equipment
  - Indirect materials (glue, screws)
  - Indirect labor (supervisors, janitors)
  - Factory insurance
```

## Traceability Spectrum

```
Easy to Trace                           Hard to Trace
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Direct Materials → Direct Labor → Manufacturing Overhead
```

**Direct Materials**: Most traceable
- Can physically observe material in product
- Easy to measure quantity used
- Material cost per unit is straightforward

**Direct Labor**: Moderately traceable
- Time cards track hours per product
- May require job tracking systems
- Increasingly harder in automated environments

**Manufacturing Overhead**: Least traceable
- Benefits multiple products simultaneously
- No clear per-unit consumption
- Requires allocation based on cost drivers

## Allocation Implications

### Direct Costs
- **No allocation needed**
- Assign directly to cost object
- High accuracy

### Indirect Costs
- **Allocation required**
- Use cost drivers (allocation bases)
- Lower accuracy, but necessary
- See: [[Overhead Application]], [[Activity-Based Costing]]

## Decision Context

### When Direct vs Indirect Matters

**Product Costing**:
- Direct costs → accurate per-unit costs
- Indirect costs → require allocation assumptions

**Pricing Decisions**:
- Must cover direct costs at minimum
- Indirect costs allocated based on volume or activity

**Make vs Buy**:
- Direct costs are clearly avoidable
- Indirect costs may or may not be avoidable

**Performance Evaluation**:
- Managers responsible for direct costs
- Indirect cost allocation may be unfair

## Example: Chair Manufacturing

**Cost Object: Single Chair**

| Cost Item | Classification | Reasoning |
|-----------|---------------|-----------|
| Wood | Direct Material | Traceable to specific chair |
| Assembly worker wages | Direct Labor | Time spent on this chair |
| Factory rent | Indirect (MOH) | Benefits all chairs made |
| Glue | Indirect (MOH) | Too small to trace per chair |
| Production supervisor | Indirect (MOH) | Oversees all production |
| Sandpaper | Indirect (MOH) | Shared across many chairs |

## Common Pitfalls

### Over-Allocation Complexity
- Making everything direct costs creates overhead
- Tracking costs money
- Benefit must exceed tracking cost

### Under-Allocation Simplicity
- Treating too many costs as indirect
- Leads to inaccurate product costs
- Poor pricing decisions

### Ignoring Cost Object
- Forgetting that classification depends on cost object
- Assuming a cost is always direct or always indirect
