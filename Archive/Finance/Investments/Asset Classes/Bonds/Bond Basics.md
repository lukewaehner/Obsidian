A **bond** is a debt security - essentially an IOU from the issuer to the bondholder. Understanding the basic structure and components of bonds is fundamental to fixed-income investing.

## What is a Bond?

### Definition
A **straight bond** obligates the issuer to pay the bondholder:

1. **Principal** (face value, par value) - typically $1,000
   - Paid at maturity
   - The "loan amount"

2. **Coupons** - periodic interest payments
   - Usually semi-annual (twice per year)
   - Fixed amount based on coupon rate

## Basic Bond Structure

```
Example: $1,000 Par Value, 6% Coupon, 10-Year Bond

Timeline:
Year 0: Pay $950 (purchase price)
Year 0.5: Receive $30 coupon ($1,000 × 6% ÷ 2)
Year 1.0: Receive $30 coupon
Year 1.5: Receive $30 coupon
...
Year 10: Receive $30 coupon + $1,000 par value
```

Total cash flows = 20 coupon payments + 1 principal payment

## Key Bond Components

### Face Value (Par Value)
- Typically $1,000 for corporate bonds
- Amount paid at maturity
- Basis for coupon calculation
- Not necessarily the purchase price!

```
Par value = $1,000
Coupon rate = 6%
Annual coupon = $1,000 × 6% = $60
Semi-annual coupon = $60 ÷ 2 = $30
```

### Coupon Rate
The annual interest rate stated on the bond:

```
Coupon Rate = Annual Coupon Payment / Par Value

Example:
Par value = $1,000
Annual coupon = $80
Coupon rate = $80 / $1,000 = 8%
```

**Important**: Coupon rate is FIXED at issuance and never changes!

### Maturity Date
When the bond expires and par value is repaid:
- **Short-term**: < 5 years
- **Intermediate-term**: 5-10 years
- **Long-term**: > 10 years

```
Example:
Issue date: January 1, 2020
Maturity: 10 years
Maturity date: January 1, 2030
```

### Coupon Payment Schedule
Most bonds pay semi-annually:

```
$1,000 par, 6% coupon bond:
- Annual payment: $60
- Semi-annual payment: $30
- Payment dates: e.g., June 1 and December 1 each year
```

## Bond Cash Flows

### Typical Bond Timeline

```
Time:     0      0.5yr    1yr    1.5yr   ...    10yr
          |       |       |       |             |
Out:   -$950
In:              $30     $30     $30      ...  $30+$1,000
```

### Total Return Components

**Income**: Coupon payments
**Capital gain/loss**: Difference between purchase price and sale/maturity price

```
Example:
Buy at $950
Receive $30 × 20 = $600 in coupons
Receive $1,000 at maturity

Total return = $600 + ($1,000 - $950) = $650
Return % = $650 / $950 = 68.4% over 10 years
```

## Types of Bonds by Coupon Structure

### Fixed-Rate Bonds
- Most common type
- Coupon rate stays constant
- Predictable income stream

### Zero-Coupon Bonds
- No periodic coupons
- Sell at discount to par
- Single payment at maturity

See: [[Zero-Coupon Bonds]]

### Floating-Rate Bonds
- Coupon rate adjusts periodically
- Tied to reference rate (e.g., SOFR)
- Less price volatility

### Step-Up Bonds
- Coupon rate increases over time
- Predefined schedule

## Bond Issuers

### U.S. Treasury
**Most secure bonds (backed by U.S. government)**:

- **T-Bills**: Maturity < 1 year
  - Zero-coupon
  - Minimum: $100
  
- **T-Notes**: Maturity 1-10 years
  - Pay semi-annual coupons
  - Most liquid bond market
  
- **T-Bonds**: Maturity > 10 years
  - Pay semi-annual coupons
  - Up to 30-year maturity

### Corporate Bonds
Issued by companies to raise capital:
- Higher yields than Treasuries (credit risk premium)
- Credit quality varies widely
- May have call provisions
- Taxable interest income

```
Example: Boeing 3.25% bond due 2035
- Par value: $1,000
- Coupon: 3.25% ($32.50/year)
- Rating: A (investment grade)
- Purpose: Finance aircraft production
```

### Municipal Bonds
Issued by state and local governments:
- Often tax-exempt (federal, sometimes state)
- Lower yields than corporate bonds
- Two types:
  - **General obligation**: Backed by taxing power
  - **Revenue bonds**: Backed by specific project revenues

### Federal Agency Bonds
Government-sponsored enterprises:
- **Fannie Mae** (FNMA): Mortgage-backed
- **Freddie Mac** (FHLMC): Mortgage-backed
- **Ginnie Mae** (GNMA): Explicitly government-backed
- **Federal Home Loan Banks**
- **Farm Credit System**

Higher yield than Treasuries, lower than corporates

## Special Features

### Callable Bonds
Issuer can repurchase before maturity:
- Call price (usually above par)
- Call protection period
- Benefits issuer when rates fall

See: [[Callable Bonds]]

### Convertible Bonds
Can exchange for stock:
- Specified conversion ratio
- Combines debt and equity features
- Lower coupon than straight bonds

See: [[Convertible Bonds]]

### Putable Bonds
Bondholder can force early redemption:
- Put price (usually par)
- Benefits bondholder when rates rise
- Less common than callable bonds

## Bond Indenture

The legal contract specifying:
- Par value and coupon rate
- Maturity date and payment dates
- Covenants (restrictions on issuer)
- Security (collateral, if any)
- Call provisions
- Sinking fund provisions

## Secured vs Unsecured Bonds

### Secured Bonds
Backed by specific collateral:
- **Mortgage bonds**: Real estate collateral
- **Equipment trust certificates**: Equipment collateral
- Lower risk, lower yield

### Unsecured Bonds (Debentures)
Backed by issuer's general credit:
- No specific collateral
- Depend on issuer's ability to pay
- Higher risk, higher yield

### Subordinated Debentures
Lower priority in bankruptcy:
- Paid after senior debt
- Higher risk, higher yield
- Still senior to equity

## Seniority and Priority

In case of default, payment priority:

```
1. Secured debt (highest priority)
2. Senior unsecured debt
3. Subordinated debt
4. Preferred stock
5. Common stock (lowest priority)
```

## Sinking Fund Provisions

Issuer sets aside money periodically:
- Reduces default risk
- May retire bonds before maturity
- Can be mandatory or optional

```
Example:
$100M bond issue, 20-year maturity
Sinking fund: Retire $5M per year starting year 11
Result: Reduces outstanding debt over time
```

## Bond Pricing Terminology

### Trading at Par
```
Price = $1,000 (100% of par)
Market price = Face value
```

### Trading at Premium
```
Price > $1,000 (> 100% of par)
Example: $1,050 = 105% of par
```

### Trading at Discount
```
Price < $1,000 (< 100% of par)
Example: $950 = 95% of par
```

See: [[Premium, Par, and Discount Bonds]]

## Example: Complete Bond Description

```
Bond: HFH Inc. Corporate Bond
Par Value: $1,000
Coupon Rate: 8%
Maturity: 10 years
Payment: Semi-annual
Current Price: $1,100
Credit Rating: BBB (investment grade)
Callable: Yes, after 5 years at $1,050
Secured: No (debenture)

Cash Flows:
- $40 every 6 months (coupon)
- $1,000 at maturity (par)
- Possible early call at $1,050
```

## Understanding Bond Quotes

Corporate bonds quoted as percentage of par:

```
Quote: 102.5
Meaning: 102.5% of $1,000 = $1,025

Quote: 98.75
Meaning: 98.75% of $1,000 = $987.50
```

Treasury bonds quoted in 32nds:

```
Quote: 99-16 (meaning 99 and 16/32)
= 99.50% of par
= $995.00 per $1,000 face value
```

## Why Companies Issue Bonds

Advantages over equity:
- **No ownership dilution**: Don't give up control
- **Tax deductible interest**: Reduces effective cost
- **Lower cost**: Generally cheaper than equity
- **Flexibility**: Can issue various maturities and structures

```
Example:
Company needs $100M
Option 1: Issue stock - dilute ownership, no tax benefit
Option 2: Issue bonds - keep control, tax-deductible interest
```
