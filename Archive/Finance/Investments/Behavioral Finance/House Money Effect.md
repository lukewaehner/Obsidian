## House Money Effect

The tendency to become less risk-averse after experiencing prior gains, treating profits as "the house's money" rather than one's own.

---

## Psychology

After winning, people feel like they are "playing with house money" (a casino term), making them more willing to take risks they would otherwise avoid. They mentally separate their original capital from their gains, creating distinct mental accounts with different risk tolerances.

---

## Example

An investor starts with $50,000 and through successful trades grows the account to $80,000. The $30,000 gain feels like "bonus money." The investor then makes increasingly risky bets with this $30,000 portion, such as buying speculative options, reasoning that "even if I lose it all, I'm back to where I started." 

However, $80,000 is $80,000 regardless of its source. A rational investor would apply the same risk management to all capital. The house money effect creates artificial mental accounting that leads to excessive risk-taking with gains.

---

## Mental Account Pyramid

People often build mental account layers in a pyramid structure:

- Bottom layer: Safe assets protecting against worst-case scenarios (emergency fund)
- Middle layer: Moderate-risk investments for medium-term goals
- Top layer: "Play money" from prior gains used for speculation

Each layer has different risk tolerance, even though optimal portfolio theory suggests treating all wealth as a single pool.
