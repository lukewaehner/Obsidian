## Loss Aversion

The phenomenon where losses are felt more intensely than equivalent gains. A core component of [[Prospect Theory]].

---

## The Loss Aversion Ratio

Research shows that to accept a 50/50 gamble of losing $100, most people require a potential gain of roughly $200 to balance it out.

The typical loss aversion ratio is 1.5x to 2.5x, meaning losses feel about twice as painful as equivalent gains feel good.

---

## Example

An investment advisor offers two portfolios:

Portfolio A: Expected return of 8% with low volatility
Portfolio B: Expected return of 12% with high volatility (could lose 20% in a bad year)

Many investors choose Portfolio A despite its lower expected return. The potential 20% loss in Portfolio B looms larger psychologically than the extra 4% annual return. A purely rational investor focused on long-term wealth maximization might prefer Portfolio B, but loss aversion makes the pain of potential losses feel too severe.

---

## Impact on Investment Behavior

Loss aversion leads investors to:

- Demand higher risk premiums for volatile assets
- Prefer bonds and stable dividend stocks even when growth stocks offer better risk-adjusted returns
- Check their portfolios less frequently to avoid experiencing the pain of interim losses
