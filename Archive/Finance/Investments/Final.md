Practice:
- Long-term bonds are more sensitive to market interest rates because the returned face value on maturity will be heavily discounted the longer away it gets
- Bonds have greater price risk the lower the coupon and longer the maturity. Lower coupon rates receive more at the end and less early on.
- Higher YTM is influenced by 
	- rating (lower rating = **more risk** = higher YTM needed)
	- time to maturity (**more risk** against interest rate changes needs higher YTM)
	- Convertibility (**not being convertible** means the bond is **riskier**, since if it looks bad you can convert to stock and get out of it early)
- Investors trading frequently is overconfidence since its shown that the lower the activity usually the higher the return
- inferring patterns from small samples is representativeness bias
- Information processing theory on forecasting errors means investors give more weight to recent experiences than prior beliefs and information.
- Semi-strong markets means that all available information is already priced in, so identifying mis-priced securities is a waste of time
- EMH means that stocks prior data does not reflect future data
- Efficient markets have all data priced in, but the risk premiums still exist, so spreading across asset classes matters, but the specific assets with the classes does not.
- Slope of SML is Market Risk Premium
- European Options can only be exercised on the expiration date
- Dirty Pricing = Coupon * Days since last coupon / days in period + clean price {quoted% * 1000}
- Caluclate a bond price with financial calculator:
	- N = Number of coupon payments
	- I/Y = YTM / Interest Rate (adjusted to coupon delivery structure)
	- PV = Price (Given or unknown)
	- PMT = Coupon = Par value * Coupon Rate / Coupons per annum
	- FV = Face Value (Given or Unknown)
- Rate of return under CAPM = $R_f + \beta(E(R_M)-R_f)$, note that $(E(R_M)-R_f)$ is representative of the market risk premium. Sometimes this is given
- CAPM Expected Return of Portfolio = $\sum E(R_i)\times{w_i}$
