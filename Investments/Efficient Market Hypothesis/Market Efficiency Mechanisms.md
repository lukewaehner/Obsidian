
## Market Efficiency Mechanisms

Three economic forces that drive markets toward efficiency.

---

### 1. Rational Investors

**Mechanism**: Investors use information rationally and systematically.

**Key Points**:
- Investors do not systematically overvalue or undervalue assets
- Information is processed correctly and reflected in prices
- Buy and sell decisions are based on fundamental value

**Implication**:
If every investor always makes perfectly rational investment decisions, it would be hard to earn excess returns because:
- All opportunities would be instantly recognized
- Prices would immediately adjust to fair value
- No mispricings would persist

**Reality Check**:
- Behavioral finance shows investors are NOT always rational
- Cognitive biases exist (overconfidence, anchoring, herding)
- However, rationality doesn't need to be universal for efficiency

---

### 2. Independent Deviations from Rationality

**Mechanism**: Irrational investors cancel each other out.

**Key Points**:
- Suppose many investors are irrational
- Some are overly optimistic, some overly pessimistic
- **The net effect is that these investors cancel each other out**
- Irrationality becomes just noise that is diversified away

**Critical Requirement**:
Irrational investors must have **different beliefs** and behave differently:
- Random errors in both directions
- No systematic bias in one direction
- Independent mistakes

**Example**:
```
Stock A true value: $50

Investor Group 1: Thinks it's worth $60 (too optimistic)
Investor Group 2: Thinks it's worth $40 (too pessimistic)

Net effect: Groups trade with each other
Price settles near $50
Market remains efficient
```

**When This Breaks Down**:
If irrationality is **systematic** (all in same direction):
- Bubbles can form (everyone too optimistic)
- Crashes can occur (everyone too pessimistic)
- Market becomes inefficient

---

### 3. Arbitrageurs Exist

**Mechanism**: Rational traders exploit and eliminate mispricings.

**Key Points**:
- Suppose collective irrationality does NOT balance out
- But there are well-capitalized, intelligent, rational investors
- These **arbitrageurs** identify mispricings
- They buy undervalued assets, sell overvalued assets
- Their trading pushes prices back toward fair value

**Requirements for Arbitrage to Work**:
1. **Capital**: Sufficient resources to take positions
2. **Intelligence**: Ability to identify mispricings
3. **Rationality**: Discipline to act on analysis
4. **Dominance**: Rational traders must dominate irrational

**Arbitrage Process**:
```
1. Mispricing occurs (stock undervalued at $45, fair value $50)
2. Arbitrageurs recognize opportunity
3. They buy the stock heavily
4. Buying pressure drives price up
5. Price returns to $50
6. Mispricing eliminated
```

**Limits to Arbitrage** (why this might not work):
- **Risk**: "Market can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent"
- **Capital constraints**: Limited resources to exploit mispricings
- **Short-selling constraints**: Hard to profit from overvaluation
- **Noise trader risk**: Irrational investors may push prices further away
- **Implementation costs**: Trading costs, taxes, borrowing costs

---

## Competition and Profit Motive

**The Ultimate Driver**: 
The driving force toward efficiency is **competition and the profit motive**.

**Why This Matters**:
- A small performance enhancement can be worth tremendous amounts of money
- This profit potential creates strong incentives to unearth relevant information
- Investors engage in:
  - **Alpha seeking**: Finding undervalued opportunities
  - **Valuation analysis**: Determining fair prices
  - **Research**: Searching for mispriced stocks

**Self-Correcting Nature**:
```
Inefficiency exists
    ↓
Profit opportunity created
    ↓
Smart investors exploit it
    ↓
Prices adjust
    ↓
Inefficiency eliminated
    ↓
Market becomes efficient
```

**Paradox**:
If markets are perfectly efficient, there's no incentive to gather information. But if no one gathers information, markets can't be efficient. Therefore, markets must be **mostly efficient** with just enough inefficiency to reward information gathering.

## Information and Costs

### Available vs New Information

**Available Information**:
- Already incorporated into prices
- Freely accessible or widely known
- No advantage from using it

**New Information**:
- Not yet in prices
- First to know can profit
- Creates temporary opportunities

### Information is Costly

**Costs Include**:
- Research expenses
- Data subscriptions
- Analyst time
- Computing resources
- Trading costs

**Incentive Structure**:
Investors have incentive to incur costs to acquire information **only if** they can make returns that exceed those costs.

**Equilibrium**:
Market reaches equilibrium where:
```
Expected Returns from Information = Cost of Acquiring Information
```

If returns exceed costs: More investors gather information → prices adjust faster → returns diminish

If costs exceed returns: Fewer investors gather information → opportunities remain → prices adjust slower

---

## Summary: Why Markets Are Efficient

Markets tend toward efficiency because:

1. **Rational investors** process information correctly
2. **Irrational investors** cancel each other out (if independent)
3. **Arbitrageurs** exploit and eliminate mispricings
4. **Competition** ensures profit opportunities are quickly exploited
5. **Information costs** are balanced by profit potential

**But**: Perfect efficiency is unlikely because:
- Some irrationality is systematic (behavioral biases)
- Arbitrage has limits (risk, costs, constraints)
- Information gathering must be profitable (can't be perfectly efficient)
