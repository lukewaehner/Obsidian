# Breaking the Buck

**Definition:** When a [[Money Market Mutual Funds (MMMFs)|Money Market Mutual Fund's]] [[Net Asset Value (NAV)]] falls below $1.00 per share.

## The Concept

### Normal Operations
- MMMFs traditionally maintain $1.00 NAV
- Designed to function like bank accounts
- Investors expect stable $1.00 value

### "Breaking the Buck"
```
NAV < $1.00
```
When this happens, the fund has "broken the buck."

## Why It Matters

### Psychological Impact
- Shatters perception of safety
- MMMFs marketed as cash equivalents
- Investors expect to get $1.00 back for every $1.00 invested

### Financial Impact
- Investors lose principal
- Even small losses (e.g., $0.97 NAV = 3% loss) are shocking
- Triggers loss of confidence in the fund

### Systemic Risk
- Can cause **runs on money market funds**
- Investors rush to redeem before NAV falls further
- Redemptions force fire sales of assets
- Can spread to other MMMFs (contagion)

## Causes

### Security Defaults
- Issuer of commercial paper defaults
- Corporate bonds held by fund lose value
- Credit crisis impacts holdings

### Market Disruptions
- Severe market stress
- Liquidity crises
- Interest rate shocks

### Poor Risk Management
- Excessive concentration in risky securities
- Chasing yield with lower-quality investments
- Inadequate diversification

## Historical Example: 2008 Financial Crisis

### Reserve Primary Fund
- September 16, 2008
- NAV fell to **$0.97 per share**
- Caused by losses on Lehman Brothers commercial paper
- First MMMF to break the buck in 14 years (since 1994)

### Consequences
- Massive redemptions across MMMF industry
- Government intervention required
- Treasury created temporary guarantee program
- Led to regulatory reforms

## Post-Crisis Changes

### Regulatory Response
1. **Floating NAVs** for institutional prime MMMFs
   - No longer locked at $1.00
   - NAV fluctuates based on market value
   
2. **Liquidity Fees and Redemption Gates**
   - Boards can impose fees on redemptions
   - Can temporarily halt redemptions in stress

3. **Enhanced Disclosure**
   - More frequent NAV reporting
   - Greater transparency on holdings

4. **Stricter Investment Rules**
   - Higher quality requirements
   - Better diversification mandates
   - Shorter maturity limits

### Current Landscape
- Retail MMMFs generally still target $1.00 NAV
- Institutional prime MMMFs use floating NAV
- Government MMMFs maintain stable NAV

## Investor Implications

### Risk Awareness
- MMMFs are **NOT risk-free**
- Not FDIC insured (unlike bank deposits)
- Small probability of loss exists
- Trade-off: Higher yield vs. slight risk

### Due Diligence
Check MMMF's:
- Quality of holdings
- Average maturity
- Expense ratio
- Historical stability
- Type of fund (government vs. prime)

### Alternatives
- FDIC-insured bank accounts (lower yield, zero risk up to limits)
- Treasury bills (direct ownership)
- Ultra-short bond funds (higher yield, more risk)

## Related Concepts

### Why $1.00 Target?
- Makes accounting simple
- Feels like a bank account
- Easier for investors to understand
- Simplifies transactions (no capital gains/losses)

### NAV Rounding
- MMMFs typically round NAV to $1.00
- Actual NAV might be $1.003 or $0.998
- Only when rounding can't maintain $1.00 does it "break"
