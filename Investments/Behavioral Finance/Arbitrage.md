## Arbitrage

The practice of exploiting price differences between markets or securities to earn risk-free profit. In theory, arbitrage eliminates mispricings and keeps markets efficient.

---

## How It Works

If an asset is underpriced in one market and overpriced in another, an arbitrageur can:

1. Buy the asset where it is cheap
2. Simultaneously sell where it is expensive
3. Pocket the difference as risk-free profit

This activity pushes prices toward equilibrium.

---

## Limits to Arbitrage

The key insight of [[Behavioral Finance]] is that arbitrage has limits:

- Arbitrageurs have limited capital and cannot bet against every mispricing
- Short-selling constraints make it difficult to profit from overpriced assets
- Noise trader risk: irrational investors may push prices further from fair value before correction
- Fundamental risk: a seemingly undervalued stock may actually have undiscovered problems
- Transaction costs and financing costs erode arbitrage profits

---

## Example

A stock trades at $100 on the NYSE and $102 on the London Stock Exchange. An arbitrageur could buy on NYSE and sell on LSE, capturing $2 per share risk-free. However, in practice:

- Transaction costs might be $0.50 per share each way ($1 total)
- Currency conversion fees reduce profit further
- The price gap may close before the trade executes
- Capital is tied up during settlement

These frictions mean small mispricings can persist.

---

## Why It Matters

If arbitrage were unlimited and costless, prices would always equal intrinsic value and [[Efficient Market Hypothesis|EMH]] would hold perfectly. Because arbitrage has limits, behavioral biases can create persistent market inefficiencies.
