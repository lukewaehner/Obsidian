## Gamblers Fallacy

The mistaken belief that deviations from long-run averages will be corrected in the short run. Also known as the Monte Carlo fallacy or the law of small numbers.

---

## The Error

People believe that the probability of an outcome changes after a series of similar outcomes, when in reality each event is independent. They expect short-term sequences to reflect long-term probabilities.

---

## Example

NVIDIA (NVDA) has risen for 10 consecutive trading days. An investor thinks "it has to go down soon" and shorts the stock. However, past returns do not shape future returns. Each day's price movement is largely independent. The stock could easily continue rising if positive fundamentals persist. The investor has confused statistical independence with some notion of "balance" that does not exist.

---

## Classic Example

At the Monte Carlo Casino in 1913, the roulette ball landed on black 26 times in a row. Gamblers lost millions betting on red, convinced that red was "due." Each spin remained a 50/50 chance regardless of prior outcomes.

---

## Contrast with Representativeness

While [[Representativeness Bias]] makes people see patterns that do not exist, Gamblers Fallacy makes people expect reversals that will not necessarily occur. Both stem from misunderstanding probability and sample sizes.
