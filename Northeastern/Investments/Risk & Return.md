## Dollar Returns

Return on an investment measured in dollars, accounting for all interim cash flows and capital gains or losses
- Does not require liquidation
- Does not take tax into consideration

Total Dollar Return on a Stock = Dividend Income + Capital Gain (or Loss)


Interim Cash Flows $\Rightarrow$ Dividends
Capital Gains / Losses $\Rightarrow$ Change in Stock Price (Increase or Decrease)

---

## Percent Returns

**Total percent return** is the return on an investment measured as a percentage of the original investment
The total percent return is the return for each dollar invested
$\text{Percent Return on a Stock} = \frac{\text{Dividend Income - Capital Gain(Or Loss)}}{Beginning Stock Price}$
or
Percent Return $= \frac{\text{Total Dollar Return on a Stock}}{\text{Beginning Stock Price (i.e., Beginning Investment)}}$

### Example: Calculating Total Dollar and Total Percent Returns
- Investment: $1,400
- Purchase price: $35/share → 40 shares
- Ending price: $49/share
- Dividend: $1.40/share

**Step 1. Total dollar return per share**
- Price gain = $49 − $35 = $14
- Dividend = $1.40
- Total = $15.40

**Step 2. Percent return per share**
- $15.40 ÷ $35 = 0.44 → **44%**

**Step 3. Total dollar return (all shares)**
- $15.40 × 40 = **$616**

**Step 4. Total percent return (on all shares)**
- $616 ÷ $1,400 = 0.44 → **44%**

### Exercise 1
- Shares: 200
- Purchase price: $30/share → $6,000 total
- Sale price: $31.50/share → $6,300 total
- No dividends

**Step 1. Holding period return (3 months)**
- Gain = $6,300 − $6,000 = $300
- Percent return = $300 ÷ $6,000 = 0.05 → **5%**

**Step 2. Annualized return**
- Formula: (1 + holding return)^(12 / months held) − 1
- (1.05)^(12/3) − 1 = (1.05)^4 − 1
- = 1.2155 − 1 = 0.2155 → **21.55% annualized**


- **Holding period:** start from the day you buy and end on the day you sell the stock
- **Effective Annual Return (EAR):** The return on an investment expressed on an "**annualized**" basis.

## Average Returns
- **Risk-free rate**: the rate of return on a **risk-less** investment.
- **Risk premium**: The extra return *on a risky asset over the risk-free rate*, that is, the reward for breaking risk.
- **The first lesson: There is a reward, on average, for bearing risk.**
- The risk premium earned by large-company stocks was 8.3% by 2019
	- Is 8.3% a good estimate of future risk premium?
	- The opinion of 226 financial economists

## Why Risk Premium?
- Modern investment theory focuses on this question
- We can examine this through the **dispersion, spread, of historical returns**
- Use two **statistical** concepts to study this dispersion, or variability: **variance and standard deviation**
- The second lesson: **the greater the potential reward, the greater the risk**

## Historical Average Returns
- A useful number to help summarize historical financial data is the simple, or arithmetic average
- If you add up the returns for large-company stocks from 1926 through 2015, you get about 1,067 percent.
- Because there are 90 returns, the average return is about 11.9%. How do we get this?
- If you make a guess about the size of the return for a year selected at random, the best guess is 11.9%
- The formula for average return is:
$$
	\text{Historical Average Return} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \text{Yearly Return}}{n}
$$
## Arithmetic Averages vs. Geometric Averages
- Arithmetic average tells you what you earned in one typical year
- Geometric average tells you what you actually earned per year on average, compounded annually, if you buy-and-hold
- When we talk about average returns, we generally are talking about arithmetic average returns
- For forecasting future returns:
	- Arithmetic is probably too high for long forecasts
	- Geometric is probably too low for short forecasts

## Risk, Variance and Standard Deviation
- **Variance** is a common measure of return dispersion. Sometimes, return dispersion is also called varaibility.
- **Standard deviation** is the square root of the variance
	- Sometimes the standard deviation is called volatility
	- Standard Deviation is handy because it is in the same "units" as the average
- **Normal distribution**: A symmetric, bell-shaped frequency distribution that can be described with only an average and a standard deviation

---

## Return Variability: Tools
- The formula for return variance is ("N" is the number of returns:)
$$
\text{VAR(R)} = \sigma^2 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N(R_i-\bar{R})^2}{N-1}
$$
- Sometimes it is useful to use the standard deviation, which is related to. variance like this:
$$
SD(R)=\sigma=\sqrt{VAR(R)}
$$
So, we would do something like this:
1. Find the average
$$
\frac{\sum{\text{Ret Year}_1 \rightarrow \text{Year}_N}}{N}
$$
2. Calculate the Variance
$$
Var = 
\frac{(R_{2009}-\bar{R})^2 +
(R_{2010}-\bar{R})^2 +
(R_{2011}-\bar{R})^2}{2011-2009}
$$
---
### Standard Deviations
- Mean: $0 \sigma$
- $\pm1\sigma$: Encompasses $68\%$ of data
- $\pm2\sigma$: Encompasses $95\%$ of data
- $\pm3\sigma$: Encompasses $\gt99\%$ of data

---
## Sharpe Ratio
The trade-off between return for taking the risk, and risk taken. Reward-to-volatility
$$
S_P=\frac{\text{Portfolio Risk Premium}}{\text{Standard Deviation of Excess Returns}}=\frac{E(r_p)-r_f}{\sigma_p}
$$
Where:
- $E(r_p)$ = Expected return of the portfolio
- $r_f$ = Risk Free rate of return
- $E(r_p) - r_f$ = Risk premium, the reward for taking risk
- $\sigma_p$ = Standard Deviation of portfolio excess return

## Risk Free Asset
Risk free return needs to be proxied:
- Treasury bonds (affected by inflation)
- Price-indexed government bonds
- Money market instruments effectively risk-free
- Risk of CDs and commercial paper is minuscule compared to most assets

---
- Risk-free rate represents compensation for waiting, or postponing consumptions
- This is often called the time value of money
- **If we are willing to bear risk, we can expect to earn a risk premium**
- **The more risk we take, the greater the expected risk premium**
