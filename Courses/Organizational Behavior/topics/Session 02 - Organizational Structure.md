### Opening examples: structure as a live business decision

Liao opens with firms that changed shape recently, not with a definition:

- **Alphabet (2015)** — Google reorganized itself *under* a holding company. Larry Page took
  Alphabet, Sundar Pichai took Google, and the moonshots (Calico, Google X, Nest, Fiber,
  Sidewalk Labs, Google Ventures, Google Capital) each got their own CEO alongside Google
  rather than inside it.
- **Google Brain + DeepMind (2023)** — two research organizations merged into Google DeepMind;
  Android, Chrome, Pixel, Fitbit, and Nest were pulled into a single Platform & Devices division.
- **GE** — "Dismantling an Icon." One conglomerate split into three listed companies: GE
  Aerospace, GE Vernova (energy), GE Healthcare.
- **Johnson & Johnson / Kenvue** — consumer health spun out so the parent could concentrate on
  MedTech and pharma innovation.

The pattern to notice: each of these is a *strategy* change expressed as a *structure* change.

### Why structure first?

Tushman & O'Reilly's congruence model frames the organization as **environment** and
**strategy** feeding an **organizational architecture** of four interdependent elements —
**talent**, **informal organization**, **formal organization**, and **critical tasks** — which
together produce **results** (performance and viability). **Leadership** acts on all four.

Structure is the *formal organization* box, and the course starts there for three reasons:

- It is one central area of the organization.
- It provides the pegs on which the informal organization — routines, networks, culture — hangs.
- Unlike the softer areas (culture, skills), structure **can be changed relatively quickly with
  significant effects**. Liao calls this the "up-and-down approach": you can move the boxes this
  quarter, you cannot move the culture this quarter.

### Functions of org structure

Bernstein & Nohria: structure exists to satisfy two things at once.

| Serves | Through |
| --- | --- |
| **Organizational needs** | Coordinated **capabilities**, coordinated **activities** |
| **Individual needs** | Coordinated **goals**, coordinated **boundaries** |

Capabilities and activities are the org's side of the bargain — what the firm can do and who
does it. Goals and boundaries are the individual's side — what I am aiming at and where my job
stops.

### Structure follows strategy

Miller et al.'s four strategy dimensions, with the metrics that reveal each one:

| Strategy | Indicators |
| --- | --- |
| **Differentiation** | *Innovation*: sales from new products, R&D as a percentage of sales, frequency of product changes. *Marketing*: product quality and image, marketing costs |
| **Focus** | Product line breadth, breadth of customer types, geographic coverage |
| **Cost leadership** | Relative directive costs per unit, newness of plant and equipment, product pricing, capacity utilization, vertical integration, R&D process |
| **Asset parsimony** | Fixed asset intensity, current asset intensity |

### The six design questions

**Organizational structure** — the framework by which job tasks are formally **divided,
grouped, and coordinated**. Six questions, six answers:

| # | Key question | Answered by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | To what degree are tasks subdivided into separate jobs? | Work specialization |
| 2 | On what basis will jobs be grouped together? | Departmentalization |
| 3 | To whom do individuals and groups report? | Chain of command |
| 4 | How many individuals can a manager efficiently and effectively direct? | Span of control |
| 5 | Where does decision-making authority lie? | Centralization / decentralization |
| 6 | To what degree will there be rules and regulations directing employees and managers? | Formalization |

#### 1. Work specialization

Degree to which activities in the organization are subdivided **into separate jobs**. The
lineage is Frederick Taylor's *Scientific Management* (1911) — analyze and synthesize workflows.

**Advantages**: efficient use of employees' skills; skill enhancement via repetition; selection
and training benefits; productivity and efficiency gains from purpose-built inventions and
machinery.

**Downside — human diseconomies**: the relation between specialization and productivity is an
**inverted U**, not a line. Past some point, boredom, fatigue, and turnover eat the gains
(the Ford line and Foxconn are the two illustrations).

The same inverted U shows up in the **experience–productivity** relationship (Staw 1980;
Staats & Gino 2012, transaction data from a mid-sized Japanese bank over 2.5 years): **skill**
rises monotonically with experience while **effort** falls, and the **joint effect** peaks in
the middle.

#### 2. Departmentalization

Basis by which jobs are grouped together for coordination. Five bases, with the examples Liao
uses:

| Basis | Groups by | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Functional** | Marketing / Finance / Production; Sales / Finance / HR / Engineering | BP, Netflix |
| **Product** | Cosmetics / Clothing / Appliances | — |
| **Customer** | Government / Industrial / Consumer | — |
| **Process** | Receiving / Sewing / Shipping | — |
| **Geographic** | North America / Asia-Pacific / Europe & Africa / South America | Unilever |
| **Market segment** | GenX / GenY / Millennials / Youth | Dell, American Express |

These **nest**. The divisional structure is a product-and-function hybrid: a CEO over corporate
managers, product divisions beneath (Washing Machine & Dryer, Lighting, Television & Stereo),
functional departments inside each division (Research, Marketing, Sales, Manufacturing,
Finance), and then possibly a *third* basis under that (Sales split South / West / East /
International).

**Functional departmentalization** specifically:

- *Characteristics* — task/knowledge driven, expandable to multiple organizational levels, the
  standard form in organizations.
- *Advantages* — efficiency gains, high specialization, reduced depletion of resources, large
  economies of scale in production.
- *Disadvantages* — **poor communication across functional areas**, and **preoccupation with
  departmental goals** rather than organizational ones. The fix Liao points at: create
  collective goals.

**The silo effect** is the failure mode:

- Slow communication and decision making, because action runs **up and down the hierarchy, not
  across** it.
- Preoccupation with departmental rather than organizational goals.
- **"Throwing it over the wall"** — doing your job without really involving other functions, so
  Marketing → Research → Design → Manufacturing → Sales each first sees the project once the
  previous group has finished with it.

#### 3. Chain of command

The unbroken line of authority extending from the top of the organization to the lowest level;
it clarifies who reports to whom.

The **process-based structure** is the alternative arrangement: CEO → executive committee →
**process owners**, each owning an end-to-end process (Product Development, Relationship
Management) that runs *at* the customer, with infrastructure and support processes underneath.
Authority follows the flow of work rather than the functional tree.

#### 4. Span of control

The number of employees reporting to a given manager. The arithmetic is the whole argument —
for 4,096 operatives:

| Span | Levels | Managers needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 4 | 7 | 1,365 (levels 1–6) |
| 8 | 5 | 585 (levels 1–4) |

| Narrow span | Wide span (**current trend**) |
| --- | --- |
| More time and attention per direct report | Less time and attention per direct report |
| Managers exert close control | Less coordination and control |
| Discourages autonomy | Increased autonomy, empowerment |
| Fosters silos | Faster communication and implementation |
| More managerial positions | Fewer managerial positions |

#### 5. Centralization and decentralization

**Centralization** — degree to which decision making is concentrated at a single point;
top-level managers make the decisions. **Decentralization** — degree to which lower-level
employees provide inputs or actually make decisions; usually paired with **wider spans of
control** (a flat structure).

| Centralize when | Decentralize when |
| --- | --- |
| Environment is stable | Environment is complex, uncertain |
| Low-level managers are not equipped decision makers | Low-level managers are capable and experienced |
| Low-level managers are disinterested in decisions | Low-level managers want a voice |
| Decisions are significant | Decisions are minor |
| The organization is in a crisis | The organization is open |
| The company is large | The company is dispersed |
| Effective implementation relies on managers maintaining control | Effective implementation relies on managerial flexibility |

#### 6. Formalization

Degree to which jobs within the organization are **standardized** — built on work specification
for task execution and control, and the mechanism by which a firm scales and expands.

*Characteristics*: explicit job descriptions; clearly defined procedures and routines.
*Appropriate for*: simple and routine jobs where **consistency** matters.

> [!question] Liao's in-class prompt
> In what organizations would consistency be of the utmost importance?

### Mechanistic vs. organic

The overarching category the six elements roll up into:

| The Mechanistic Model | The Organic Model |
| --- | --- |
| High specialization | Cross-functional teams |
| Rigid departmentalization | Cross-hierarchical teams |
| Clear chain of command | Free flow of information |
| Narrow spans of control | Wide spans of control |
| Centralization | Decentralization |
| High formalization | Low formalization |

### Common organization forms

- **The simple structure** (the start-up) — low departmentalization, wide span of control,
  little formalization, authority in a single person.
- **The bureaucracy** (the post office) — high routinization, fetishism around control.
- **Matrix** (the consulting firm) — dual reporting structure, typically along functional,
  product, and/or geographic lines. The illustration is a software org where a Business Analyst
  reports both to the Business Analyst Manager and to a Product Manager.
- **The virtual organization** (the movie studio).
- **The boundaryless organization** (teams at GE's boundaryless organization).

### Matching strategy and structure

> What organizational structure design should be implemented to support the specific strategies
> that the firm pursues?

| Structure | Why it fits | Strategy it supports |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Simple structure** | Offers high-quality, efficient service; reduces the liability of being small | Marketing differentiation, niche differentiation |
| **Bureaucracy** | Substantial scale economy opportunities; differentiation fits with production regularity | Cost leadership, marketing differentiation |
| **Organic structure** | Flexible, innovative; niche wide enough to use the innovative potential | Innovation differentiation, niche differentiation |
| **Divisionalized structure** | Divisions, profit centers, formal plans suitable for diversification; vertical integration | Cost leadership, marketing differentiation |

### New forms of organizational structure

The Manu Cornet cartoon of tech org charts — Amazon as a deep tree, Google as a tangle,
Facebook as a mesh, Microsoft as warring fiefdoms, Apple as a single red hub, Oracle as legal
dwarfing engineering — is the setup for two real cases.

**How Apple organized for innovation** (Podolny & Hansen 2020). Apple stayed **functional**
while growing roughly 40× in revenue. In 1998 the CEO had eight functions (Hardware, Software,
Marketing, Operations, Services & Support, Sales, Finance, Legal); by 2019 it had grown to
seventeen — Design, Hardware Engineering, Hardware Technologies, Software, Services, Machine
Learning & AI, Marketing, Marketing Comm., Operations, Sales, Retail, People, Finance, Legal,
Corporate Comm., Environment/Policy & Social, Corporate Dev. The point: it added functions
rather than converting to divisions, keeping decision rights with domain experts.

**Valve's matrix** — general management over functions (Sales, Finance, HR, Engineering) crossed
with products (Half-Life 2, Portal, Dota 2, Steam), so engineering personnel are assigned across
divisions.

**Holacracy** (Roberson 2015; Valve, Zappos) — the employee-centric form. Work enters, gets
**done in clear roles**, which surfaces **tensions** read as challenges and opportunities;
**tactical meetings** sync and triage ("working in" the business) while **governance meetings**
clarify and improve the structure itself ("working on" the business).

### Vacillation: the big moving ship

Boumgarden et al. 2012 — rather than settling on one point of the centralization spectrum, large
firms **oscillate** between exploration and exploitation.

- **HP** — centralize for efficiency (82–84, design/products/marketing), decentralize for
  innovation (88–94, responsiveness to the market), centralize again (95–98, integrating
  multiple computing systems for customer solutions).
- **USA Today** — the same swing, with efficiency and innovation trading places over time.

And the choice is not all-or-nothing across the firm: it is made **function by function**, and
the answer moves. On Liao's table, Marketing and R&D sat centralized and are drifting toward
decentralized, while Operations sat decentralized and is drifting back toward centralized; HR
and the rest stay decentralized.

### Restructuring and innovation

**Structural recombination** (Karim & Kaul 2015; Karim et al. 2016) — reorganizing the org
structure by merging business units, either by **absorbing one unit into another** or by
**forming a new business**.

*Causing factors*: industry/market/strategy change (industry growth or turbulence — dynamism in
sales), and managerial team change (TMT turnover, TMT size increase).

> [!question] Facilitation vs. disruption?
> How does structural recombination affect firm innovation?

Two competing views: recombination **facilitates** innovation by integrating knowledge resources
from different domains, or it **disrupts** new product innovation during the shake-up period.

Three moderators decide which one you get:

- **Knowledge coherence** — the relatedness and complementary nature of knowledge within the
  restructured units (measured as average relatedness of each patent to all other patents in the
  firm).
- **Path dependence** — dependence on established routines, processes, and mental models
  (measured as the share of self-citations in total citations).
- **Quality of existing knowledge** (measured as average citations received per patent).

The evidence — merging events across 71 firms over 20 years, outcome measured as citations
received — shows recombination **helps when knowledge coherence is high and hurts when it is
low**: the two lines diverge as the number of recombinations rises. Under low knowledge
quality, high path dependence is the worst combination and falls off with more recombinations.

## Key Takeaways

- Structure is the one piece of the organizational architecture you can move quickly, which is
  why it comes first — everything softer hangs off it.
- All six design elements are the *same* dial in different clothes: mechanistic on one end
  (specialized, siloed, narrow-span, centralized, formalized), organic on the other.
- Both specialization and experience relate to productivity as an **inverted U**. More is not
  better past the peak.
- Structure follows strategy, but the mapping is many-to-one — marketing differentiation is
  served by a simple structure, a bureaucracy, *and* a divisionalized structure depending on
  scale and diversification.
- Apple's counterexample matters: a firm can scale 40× and stay functional by **adding
  functions** rather than switching to divisions.
- Restructuring is not good or bad for innovation on its own. **Knowledge coherence** decides
  the sign.
- Large firms don't find the right point on the centralize/decentralize line — they
  **vacillate**, function by function, as the priority shifts between efficiency and innovation.
