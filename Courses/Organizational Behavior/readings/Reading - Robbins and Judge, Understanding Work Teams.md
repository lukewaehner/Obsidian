> [!abstract] One-line version
> A team is not just a group. It produces **synergy** through coordinated effort and shared
> accountability. Whether it works depends on its **context**, its **composition**, and its
> **processes**. And sometimes the right answer is not to use a team at all.

## Groups vs. Teams

| | Work group | Work team |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goal | Share information | Collective performance |
| Synergy | Neutral, sometimes negative | Positive |
| Accountability | Individual | Individual **and** mutual |
| Skills | Random and varied | Complementary |

A group's output is the sum of what each member does. A team's output should be **more** than
that sum. That gap is the whole reason to use a team.

## Four Types of Teams (plus one)

1. **Problem-solving teams.** A small set of people from the same department meet to suggest
   improvements but usually can't act on them alone (for example, quality circles).
2. **Self-managed teams.** Members take on the supervisor's duties: planning, scheduling, and
   assigning work. Results vary a lot depending on the norms and the conflict inside the team.
3. **Cross-functional teams.** Members at about the same level but from different areas, brought
   together for one task. They are powerful for complex work, but building trust takes longer.
4. **Virtual teams.** Members work across distance using technology. They lose social rapport
   and cues, so they need deliberate trust building and clear goals.
5. **Multiteam systems.** A set of teams that depend on each other to reach a higher-level goal.
   Coordination *between* teams becomes the leadership problem.

## The Team Effectiveness Model

The chapter's core framework has three categories of inputs that feed effectiveness.

### Context (what the organization supplies)

- **Adequate resources:** time, money, equipment, information
- **Leadership and structure:** who does what, with fair workloads and schedules
- **Climate of trust:** in each other and in the leader
- **Performance evaluation and rewards:** pay for team results, not only individual results

### Composition (who is on the team)

- **Member abilities:** technical, problem-solving, and interpersonal
- **Personality:** traits such as conscientiousness and agreeableness matter, especially in the
  lowest-scoring member
- **Allocation of roles:** fill the roles the team needs (see the team roles below)
- **Diversity:** varied perspectives, with a risk of fault lines and weaker cohesion
- **Cultural differences**
- **Size:** keep teams small. Large teams lose cohesion and invite social loafing
- **Member preferences:** people who don't want to work on teams can drag a team down

### Processes (how the team works)

- **Common plan and purpose:** a shared direction, plus **reflexivity** (stepping back to
  reflect on and adjust the plan)
- **Specific goals:** measurable and realistic
- **Team efficacy:** the team's belief that it can succeed
- **Team identity:** members feel attached to the team
- **Team cohesion:** members are emotionally linked and committed
- **Mental models:** a shared picture of how the work gets done
- **Conflict levels:** some *task* conflict can help, while *relationship* conflict almost
  always hurts
- **Social loafing:** counter it with individual accountability inside collective goals

### Team roles

The chapter presents a role wheel (Margerison & McCann) with roles such as creator, promoter,
assessor, organizer, producer, controller, maintainer, adviser, and a central **linker** who
coordinates. The point is that a strong team covers the roles. It does not need nine copies of
the same type of person.

## Turning Individuals into Team Players

- **Selection:** hire for interpersonal and teamwork skills along with technical skills
- **Training:** workshops on problem solving, communication, negotiation, and conflict
- **Rewards:** reward cooperation and contribution to the team, not only individual wins

## When Teams Aren't the Answer

Teams cost more time and coordination than individual work. Three tests before forming one:

1. Can the work be done **better by more than one person**?
2. Does the work give the group a **common purpose** that is more than the sum of individual
   goals?
3. Are the members **interdependent**? Does one person's work depend on another's?

If the answer to these is no, use a group or individuals.

## Crosswalk: Robbins & Judge vs. Haas & Mortensen

| Haas & Mortensen (Hackman) | Robbins & Judge equivalent |
| --- | --- |
| Compelling direction | Process: common plan and purpose, specific goals |
| Strong structure: number and mix | Composition: size, diversity, abilities, roles |
| Strong structure: task design | Context: leadership and structure. Also the "when to use teams" interdependence test |
| Strong structure: norms | Process: conflict levels, social loafing |
| Supportive context | Context: resources, trust, evaluation and rewards |
| Shared mindset: identity | Process: team identity, cohesion |
| Shared mindset: understanding | Process: mental models |

The two frameworks overlap a lot. Haas & Mortensen is the leader's design checklist, while
Robbins & Judge is the full list of variables.

## Key Takeaways

- Teams only pay off when there is real **synergy** and **interdependence**. Otherwise they are
  overhead.
- Effectiveness = context + composition + process. Most failures come from context and process,
  not personalities.
- Keep teams small, fill the roles, and keep conflict about the task and not about people.
- Reflexivity, meaning the team steps back to check its own plan, is what keeps a team adapting.
