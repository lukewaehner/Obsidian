# Reading — Chatman & Cha, "Leading by Leveraging Culture"

Reading for [[Session 04 - Organizational Culture]] · [[Organizational Behavior]]

> [!cite] Citation
> Chatman, Jennifer A., and Sandra Eunyoung Cha. "Leading by Leveraging Culture." *California
> Management Review*, vol. 45, no. 4, Summer 2003, pp. 20–34.
> [Primo](https://northeastern.primo.exlibrisgroup.com/discovery/fulldisplay?docid=cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_38518832&context=PC&vid=01NEU_INST:NUL)
> · PDF: `~/Documents/Northeastern/fall-2026/organizational-behavior/session-04/`

> [!abstract] One-line version
> Culture is not employee happiness — it is the informal control system that makes strategy
> *execute*, and it only works if it meets three tests (strategically relevant, strong,
> innovation-promoting) and is built with three levers (selection, socialization, reward); the
> sting in the tail is that a strong culture raises the penalty for a leader who appears to
> violate it.

> [!tip] This is the frame for the SMA case
> Chatman & Cha give you the vocabulary Session 04 is testing: **warring factions**, the **three
> levers**, and the **hypocrisy attribution dynamic**. MEPD is a warring-factions culture whose
> GM pulled one lever, ignored two, and then axed a beloved program during a downturn — which
> this article names as the precise trigger for cynicism. See
> [[Case - SMA, Micro-Electronic Products Division (A)]].

> [!warning] Three different sets of three — don't blur them
> The article's title phrase "The Three C's" is **clear, consistent, comprehensive** (how culture
> works), and it is *not* the three levers or the three criteria. Keep them straight:
> | Set | Members |
> | --- | --- |
> | Three **criteria** for culture as a leadership tool | strategically relevant · strong · emphasizes innovation |
> | Three **levers/tools** to build it | selection · socialization & training · rewards |
> | Three **C's** (what makes it "work") | clear · consistent · comprehensive |

## The Opening Disclaimer

They start by refusing the sales pitch. Prospective clients call and say "Come on down to our
organization and get us a better one," imagining "employees will be singing and dancing in their
cubicles."

> "Although this is a nice image, simply trying to make employees happy misses the power of
> leveraging culture. The problem is that organizational culture has become faddish; and, as
> such, it has been over-applied and under-specified."

And the claim is deliberately modest — worth quoting in class if anyone over-reaches:

> "We will not claim that by simply managing culture, leaders will be assured of organizational
> success, or that by neglecting culture, they will be doomed to failure. Leveraging culture is
> but one of a number of key leadership tools."

## Why Culture Is Powerful: Execution, Not Formulation

The hinge is Charan & Colvin's 1999 *Fortune* piece "Why CEOs Fail," whose answer was
"notoriously simple": CEOs failed when they could not fully execute their strategy. That cuts
against the industrial-economics view that firms win on well-formulated, hard-to-imitate
strategies.

> "Charan and Colvin's article suggested that firms whose strategies were merely reasonable but
> were executed fully could be the most successful. This shifts the focus from strategy
> formulation to strategy execution — **and culture is all about execution.**"

**Southwest**, their exhibit: a "transparent, almost simple" strategy — high volume, short
convenient flights, one fuel-efficient aircraft type, low costs, low fares. The operating numbers
that follow from culture, not from strategy:

- 15-minute turnaround vs. a competitor average of 35 minutes
- ~12 hours daily aircraft utilization vs. an industry average closer to 9
- the only U.S. airline profitable for 28 consecutive years

> "Southwest's success hinges not on how brilliant, unique, or opaque their strategy is, but on
> the alignment between their culture and strategy, on how clearly employees understand the
> culture and how intensely they feel about it."

**The definition to memorize:**

> "Culture is a system of shared values (defining what is important) and norms (defining
> appropriate attitudes and behaviors)."

Two mechanisms by which strong cultures raise performance:

1. **Energizing** — appealing to higher ideals, rallying people around unified goals; such ideals
   "are inherently engaging and fill voids in identity and meaning."
2. **Coordinating** — stated values and norms focus attention on priorities and guide behavior,
   and they do so "without impinging, as formal control systems do, on the autonomy necessary for
   excellent performance under changing conditions."

> **Criterion 1 — strategically relevant.** "A culture cannot be crafted until an organization has
> first developed its business strategy."

## Norms vs. Rules, and the Nordstrom Story

- **Norms**: "legitimate, socially shared standards against which the appropriateness of behavior
  can be evaluated." They are "the psychological bases of culture" and imply *social control* —
  positive or negative sanctions.
- **Rules**: "formal, codified directives."

**Hawthorne, cited against itself.** Roethlisberger and Dickson found group norms shaped behavior
more powerfully than money or physical environment — and the norm *capped* output. Employees who
worked too much were shunned just as much as those who worked too little. Chatman & Cha include
this deliberately: norms are powerful in whichever direction they point, and a strong culture is
not automatically a good one.

> "We assimilate because the consequences of violating strong norms — at best, embarrassment, and,
> at worst, exclusion or alienation from the social group — threaten our ability to survive in an
> interdependent world."

**The Nordstrom anecdote** (the first author's own shopping trip) is the article's best-told
example. Lance, a sales associate, fails to find her shoes. Howard, a *different* associate,
calls other stores, finds the shoes at **Macy's** — a primary Nordstrom competitor — and has
already arranged overnight delivery, with Nordstrom paying the shipping and Macy's billing the
customer.

Then the part she wasn't meant to hear. Howard, to Lance:

> "I can't believe you didn't work harder to find those shoes for her. **You really let us down.**"

> "Remember, Howard is not Lance's boss — they are peers — and yet, the norms encouraging customer
> service at Nordstrom are so strong that members are willing to sanction each other, regardless
> of status, for a failure to uphold those norms."

Peer sanction across status lines is the observable signature of a strong culture. It is exactly
what MEPD does not have.

### The irony of leading through culture

> "Relying on formal rules, policies, and procedures will not result in outstanding anything, be
> it customer service, innovation, or quality."

Rules are "only useful for addressing situations that are predictable and regular." But
outstanding service is judged on "situations that are nearly impossible to anticipate, unique to
a particular person, and difficult to solve."

> "**The irony of leadership through culture is that the less formal direction you give employees
> about how to execute strategy, the more ownership they take over their actions and the better
> they perform.**"

The instruction sets that follow from that: Nordstrom — "use your good judgment in all
situations." Southwest — "do what it takes to make the Customer happy."

> "Violations are considered in terms of letting their colleagues down rather than breaking rules."

And the payoff for the manager: if Howard polices himself *and* Lance, "their manager does not
have to spend time looking over their shoulders."

## Criterion 2 — Strong: The Agreement × Intensity Grid

Strength has two independent components: **agreement** about what's valued, and **intensity**
about it. The two off-diagonal cells are the useful ones.

| | **Low agreement** | **High agreement** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **High intensity** | **Warring factions** | **Strong culture** |
| **Low intensity** | Not a culture at all | **Vacuous culture** |

- **Warring factions** — high intensity, low agreement. Their example is high-tech: "marketing
  groups typically focus on customer-driven product features while engineering groups focus on
  elegant product designs." People care enormously about incompatible things.
- **Vacuous** — high agreement, low intensity, and they say it is **the more common failure**.
  Members agree on what matters "but they don't much care and, as such, are unwilling to go the
  extra mile... or to sanction others for a failure to uphold those norms." Its prevalence
  "probably reflects the faddish nature of organizational culture and the lip service such
  organizations pay to it."

**Timing matters.** A longitudinal study of 173 young high-technology companies found founders'
initial model of the employment relation "dramatically influenced their firms' later success," and
firms that switched models as they aged were *less* successful. Firms built on the **commitment
model** — strong culture, hiring on culture fit — completed IPOs sooner than those founded on
engineering or bureaucracy models. The implication is uncomfortable for turnaround situations:
culture is cheapest to set early and expensive to change later.

## Criterion 3 — Content: It Has to Emphasize Innovation

Of all the dimensions culture can vary on, **only one appears universally applicable** regardless
of size, industry, or age: innovation. The evidence is Kotter and Heskett's study of 207 large
firms over 11 years — a strong, strategically appropriate culture produced good long-run
performance **only if** it also contained norms promoting innovation and change.

> [!note] Kotter shows up here on purpose
> The same Kotter as Session 05. The bridge is real: Chatman & Cha's criterion 3 says culture must
> promote change; [[Reading - Kotter, Leading Change]] is the mechanics of actually running one.

**The best finding in the article.** Outside observers watched product development meetings where
one member pitched an idea, and rated the *pitcher* lowest in intelligence, time after time. Why?
Because the critics get the good lines — "Didn't you think of...?", "We already tried..." — while
the pitcher says "Um, I'm not sure" and "I don't know."

> "Not only are critical skills valued more than creative skills, but also **creativity and wisdom
> are inversely related in people's minds.** Expressing a creative idea is, therefore, risky —
> since a person suggesting one can end up being perceived as unintelligent."

> "Managers can bet on their employees having creative ideas in their head... **The question is:
> Are they willing to say their ideas out loud?**"

Three remedies:

1. **Structures that make pitching high-status.** Disney's "Gong Show," three times a year, where
   "everyone in the company — including secretaries, janitors, and mailroom staff — gets to pitch
   movie ideas to the top executives." IDEO's brainstorming as a "**status auction**" — "the more
   creative the idea, the higher the bid." Also the deliberately "weird" prescriptions: "ignore
   people who have solved the exact problem you face" and "find some happy people and get them to
   fight."
2. **Psychological safety.** "Leaders also promote innovation by creating a shared belief that
   team members are safe to take interpersonal risks." Safe employees "ask questions, seek
   feedback, experiment, reflect on results, and discuss errors or unexpected outcomes openly."
   Leaders set this by how they handle ideas and errors, which sets "shared perceptions of how
   consequential it is to make a mistake," feeding "a more lasting culture of fear or of openness."
3. **Speed.** **Charles Schwab**, late 1995: a research group built middleware and, knowing
   middleware is unsexy, bolted on a demo front end — a simple web-based trade. CIO Dawn Lepore
   happened to bring Charles Schwab himself, a self-described techno-buff, who "fell off my
   chair." A team was assembled within weeks, resourced, "protected from the larger bureaucracy,"
   reporting directly to President David Pottruck: "We needed a group that felt like they were
   nimble, unshackled from the larger bureaucracy." Commercial product in three months; **25,000
   subscribers within two weeks — their goal for the entire year**; 30% of the online market by
   1998, roughly equal to E*Trade, Fidelity, and Waterhouse combined.

   > "First, developing a culture that encourages employees to express creative ideas may cause
   > good ideas to crop up from unexpected places. Second, and more importantly, once managers
   > spot a good idea, norms that emphasize urgency and speed will ensure its implementation."

## The Three Levers

### Tool 1 — Recruit and select for *culture* fit, not just job fit

> "It makes sense to hire people who will fit the culture, possibly even trading off some
> immediate skills necessary for the specific entry job for better culture fit. **People can learn
> new skills; establishing culture fit is much harder.**"

The argument: if a person succeeds they will hold multiple jobs in the firm, "and these jobs are
linked by the organizational culture" — so hiring to the entry job optimizes the wrong thing.

**GE's stated criteria**, quoted for the intensity of the language rather than the content —
candidates who "stimulate and relish change and are not frightened or paralyzed by it, see change
as an opportunity, not a threat," and "have a passion for excellence, hating bureaucracy and all
the nonsense that comes with it."

**The similarity-attraction effect**, and the one-line rule that follows:

> "We are attracted to people who are similar to ourselves... **Be careful which people you send
> out to do your recruiting because you will get more of them back.**"

**Cisco**, hiring ~1,000 people a month in late-1990s Silicon Valley: culturally consistent
criteria (frugal, enthusiastic about the Internet, smart, **not obsessed with status**);
benchmarking and focus groups to tune the process; targeting *passive applicants* who weren't job
hunting; a fast website (500,000 hits/month **during work hours**) with a 5-minute initial
application; and a "friends@Cisco" hot key that got the candidate a call from a Cisco employee at
a comparable level within 24 hours — a peer, because similarity makes the cultural information
credible.

### Tool 2 — Socialization and training

Socialization and selection are **substitutable**: tight labor market → socialize more; loose
labor market → select harder. Two goals: employees acquire cultural knowledge, and they bond.

**E*Trade**: new employees stand on a chair at their first staff meeting and tell everyone
something embarrassing about themselves. Chatman & Cha defend it as "sound psychological logic" —
once you have already lost face, asking a basic question costs nothing; newcomers "are grateful
that their new colleagues accept them despite their faults"; and the shared initiation rite
"creates a bond... [and] by increasing their accountability to others, makes it more likely that
newcomers will work hard to uphold established organizational norms." CEO Christos Cotsakos also
took the executive team Formula One racing to make them "move faster" and to cooking school to
increase agility working together.

### Tool 3 — Rewards

> "**Culture is an organization's informal reward system and needs to be intricately connected to
> formal rewards.**"

**CompUSA** under CEO James Halpin — "a cross between a college fraternity and a military boot
camp." Regional sales managers at quarterly meetings are seated at the U-shaped table by store
sales, lowest sales nearest the front, because "they have to listen to everything we've got to
say." Name badges carry the store's "shrink number" (inventory loss). On the upside, when a young
employee earned $50,000 in commission in a month, Halpin flew to the store to hand him the cash in
front of customers and colleagues.

> "Though these specific rewards (and punishments) may be inappropriate for some organizations,
> the lesson is that rewards need to be **clear, consistent, and comprehensive** — the focus on
> generating revenues at CompUSA is simply impossible for employees to miss."

## The Pitfall: The Hypocrisy Attribution Dynamic

This is the most original section and the one most likely to be the exam question.

First, what employees actually read:

> "Employees attend vigilantly to leaders' behavior, even to the rather mundane aspects such as
> what they spend time on, put on their calendar, **ask and fail to ask**, follow up on, and
> celebrate... They convey much more to employees about priorities than do printed vision
> statements and formal policies."

Then the trap. Emphasizing cultural values raises the standard employees apply *to the leader*:

1. Values make everyone's behavior salient, so employees become "particularly attentive to
   possible violations, especially by leaders, who are highly visible based on their power over
   our fate at work."
2. The **actor-observer bias** kicks in — we explain our own behavior generously (good outcomes
   dispositional, bad outcomes situational) and others' unsympathetically (good outcomes
   situational, bad outcomes dispositional).
3. Some event eventually makes the leader look inconsistent. Employees conclude the leader is
   "personally failing to 'walk the talk.'"
4. "Organization members perceive hypocrisy and **replace their hard-won commitment with
   performance-threatening cynicism.**"
5. Worst of all: "because such negative interpersonal judgments are inherently threatening,
   employees say nothing publicly, **precluding a fair test of their conclusions and disabling
   organizational learning from the event.** The process cycles."

**The implication is counterintuitive and worth saying out loud in class: investing in culture
raises the downside risk.** A vacuous culture cannot be betrayed. A strong one can.

### Dreyer's Grand Ice Cream — the counterfactual to MEPD

June 1998, a $1bn company, everything at once: expansion investments took longer and raised the
cost structure; CEO Gary Rogers had been diagnosed with a brain tumor and had neurosurgery and
radiation that spring; butterfat hit a record $2.91/lb, costing $22m in gross profit while
competitors' discounting blocked a price increase; the "Better-For-You" segment they'd invested
in reversed; and Ben & Jerry's threatened to terminate its distribution contract (and did, in
August).

> "Rather than engaging in the kind of panicked cost-cutting common among organizations in tough
> situations like this, Dreyer's executive team intentionally handled this near-crisis period in a
> way that was **consistent with the culture in which they had long invested.**"

What they did:

- Executive committee members "were on planes, flying across the country, and by the end of that
  week they had met with **every one of their more than 4,000 employees.**"
- Cronk: "we know our limits and understand the law, but we tend to be very open with our
  employees, we communicate a lot." An account executive: "they reassured us by **calling it
  straight**... you looked at these [senior managers] and thought, you'd run through a wall for
  this guy."
- "1-800 calls" — pre-recorded Rogers speeches with "an honest tone that celebrated successes and
  disclosed shortcomings."
- **They kept funding Dreyer's Leadership University**, an extra million dollars, mid-crisis. The
  VP of sales: "when people heard that we were investing another million dollars into the
  [culture] and DLU it created a high degree of comfort and confidence that we're focused on
  what's really important and **that it's not just talk.**"

Results: Dreamery took 11.5% of superpremium within a year of its Sept 1999 launch; the
superpremium portfolio reached 31.3% volume share against Häagen-Dazs at 34.1% and Ben & Jerry's
at 33.4%; the stock went from 9.88 in September 1998 to over 36 by January 2001 and 71.23 on
2 January 2003 on the Nestlé merger agreement.

And the closing warning, which reads like it was written about Spichty:

> "To succeed, leaders must instill their employees with confidence and clarity about key cultural
> values... If they do not, **employees will provide their own explanations; and when leader
> behaviors are ambiguous or beloved structures are axed, the explanations that employees
> spontaneously generate are not likely to be charitable.**"

## Reading This Onto MEPD

| Article concept | MEPD |
| --- | --- |
| **Warring factions** (high intensity, low agreement) | The diagnosis. Everyone cares enormously; nobody agrees on strategy, priorities, or profitability criteria. Not vacuous — Gessler can't sleep before meetings |
| Peer sanction across status (Howard → Lance) | Absent. Disagreement goes to the hallway, never to the person. "People would end them by agreeing to disagree" |
| **Criterion 1** — strategically relevant | There is no agreed strategy to be relevant *to*. Falkniss: "We have not defined the IC business" |
| **Criterion 3** — innovation norms | The pitcher-rated-least-intelligent finding *is* the 28-day product development meeting. No psychological safety, so the focus divider dies and resurrects four times |
| Speed (Schwab: 3 months) | The sensor signal control product took **seven years** instead of three. Gessler blames a corporate environment that "is not a risk-taking one" |
| **Tool 1** — selection | SMA selects and promotes through manufacturing; Spichty, from R&D, is the anomaly and is distrusted for it |
| Similarity-attraction ("you get more of them back") | Marketing was staffed almost entirely by transfers **from Sales** — and is then blamed for not thinking like Marketing |
| **Tool 2** — socialization | Nothing. The one program that would have done it (OD Phase III, interfunctional coordination) was cancelled |
| **Tool 3** — rewards | The killer. Plants on gross margin, Sales on volume, with no shared measure. Chatman: culture "needs to be intricately connected to formal rewards" — MEPD's formal rewards are connected to *opposing* objectives |
| **Hypocrisy attribution dynamic** | Spichty names communication and coordination as priorities, kills the coordination program, and is read as "does not listen," "too soft," "people at the top do not create or confront conflict." Employees say nothing publicly — the hallway meetings *are* the disabled organizational learning |
| Dreyer's in crisis | The direct counterfactual. Same squeeze; Dreyer's over-invested in culture and communicated relentlessly, Spichty cut the culture program and deleted market planning |

## Key Takeaways

- **Culture is an execution technology, not a morale program.** The whole argument rests on Charan
  & Colvin: strategies fail in execution, and culture is the execution layer. Southwest's edge is
  a 15-minute turnaround, not a clever route map.
- **Norms beat rules precisely where rules can't reach** — the unanticipatable, one-off situation.
  So the less procedural direction you give, the better people perform, *provided* the norms and
  the strategic goals are clear.
- **Strength is agreement × intensity, and the two failure modes are different diseases.** Warring
  factions need a shared objective; vacuous cultures need somebody to care. Prescribing the wrong
  cure makes it worse.
- **Innovation is the one universally required content.** Kotter & Heskett: strategically
  appropriate culture only pays off long-run if it also promotes change.
- **Creativity is punished by default.** People read the idea-pitcher as the dumbest person in the
  room. Innovation norms exist to overpay for the pitch against that gradient — Disney's Gong
  Show, IDEO's status auction.
- **Three levers, and the third is the one organizations skip.** Selection and socialization are
  visible and fun; rewards are where culture either gets ratified or contradicted. A reward system
  that contradicts the espoused culture wins.
- **A strong culture is a liability as well as an asset.** The hypocrisy attribution dynamic means
  the more loudly you espouse values, the more expensive each apparent violation becomes — and the
  damage is silent, because nobody says it out loud.

## Open Questions

- The argument is circular at the edges: strong cultures cause performance, but Southwest, Cisco,
  Dreyer's, and Schwab are all selected *because* they performed. Where is the strong-culture firm
  that failed? Hawthorne is the only counter-example offered, and it is 70 years old.
- "Trade off immediate skills for culture fit" and "you get more of the recruiters back" sit
  badly together. Systematic hiring for fit plus similarity-attraction is a homogeneity engine —
  and criterion 3 wants innovation, which the same literature says needs difference. The article
  never reconciles them. (Worth raising: Session 08 is Team Composition and Diversity.)
- The 173-firm study says culture is set early and switching models hurts. If true, what is the
  realistic ceiling on Spichty's ambitions in a 150-year-old company where the division is 5% of
  corporate sales?
- The hypocrisy dynamic is described as near-inevitable — "an event inevitably occurs." If so, is
  the honest advice to *not* espouse values loudly unless you can guarantee consistency? Dreyer's
  is offered as the escape, but Dreyer's had decades of accumulated credibility to spend.
- Whose lever is the reward system? Chatman & Cha write as if the leader controls it. In MEPD, the
  plant-as-profit-center model is corporate and spans all eight divisions. What does a division GM
  do when lever 3 is not his to pull?
