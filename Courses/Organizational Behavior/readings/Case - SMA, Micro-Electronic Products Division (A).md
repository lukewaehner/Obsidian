# Case — SMA: Micro-Electronic Products Division (A)

Case for [[Session 04 - Organizational Culture]] · [[Organizational Behavior]]

> [!cite] Citation
> Beer, Michael, and Michael Tushman. *SMA: Micro-Electronic Products Division (A)*. Harvard
> Business School Case 9-400-084, rev. 13 Dec. 2018. Harvard Business Publishing course packet.
> PDF: `~/Documents/Northeastern/fall-2026/organizational-behavior/session-04/`

> [!abstract] One-line version
> A Swiss components division loses three quarters of its operating margin in three years while
> sales stay flat, and the general manager asks the consultants to tell him whether the conflict
> between his functions is "serious" — when the conflict is not a symptom of bad chemistry but
> the exact output the division's measurement system is built to produce.

> [!tip] What this case is actually for
> It is the culture case, not the conflict case. Every group in MEPD behaves rationally given
> what it is measured on, and every group is certain the others are incompetent. Read it with
> Chatman & Cha's three levers — selection, socialization, reward — and ask which of the three
> Spichty touched. He reorganized structure and replaced people. He did not change a single
> measure, and he cancelled the only program aimed at norms.

## The Ask

Guido Spichty, VP and general manager of the Micro-Electronic Products Division, calls in SMA's
director of organization effectiveness:

> "Most of my key people believe that we are having conflicts because economic and business
> conditions are bad. They say that if business would only get better we will stop arguing with
> each other. Frankly, I am not sure if they are right. The conflicts might be due to the
> pressures we are under but more likely they indicate a more fundamental problem. Can you and
> your group determine if the conflict between groups is serious and if so what I might do
> about it?"

Note the framing. He offers two hypotheses — it's the recession, or it's something fundamental —
and asks which. The case is built so that the answer is neither: the conflict is *designed in*,
and the recession only removed the slack that used to hide it.

## The Numbers Say Margin, Not Volume

Exhibit 1 (SFr thousands) is the single most important page in the case, and it does not say
what Spichty's managers say it says.

| | 2016 | 2015 | 2014 | 2013 | 2012 | 2011 | 2010 | 2009 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sales | 311,126 | 308,771 | 343,734 | 327,775 | 259,372 | 295,619 | 281,495 | 164,703 |
| Operating margin | 20,375 | 20,182 | 53,981 | 65,697 | 38,810 | 75,420 | 70,538 | 38,979 |
| **Margin %** | **6.5%** | **6.5%** | **15.7%** | **20.0%** | 15.0% | **25.5%** | 25.1% | 23.7% |

- **Sales in 2016 are higher than 2012 and roughly flat against 2015.** The volume collapse the
  managers keep invoking is not in the data.
- **Operating margin fell from 25.5% in 2011 to 6.5% in 2016** — a 73% decline. That is the
  crisis, and it is a pricing and cost crisis, not a demand crisis.
- Corporate expects **10% annual growth and 25–30% gross margins**, "higher than the prevailing
  profitability levels among electronic component manufacturers." MEPD is being held to a
  standard the industry no longer supports.
- Meanwhile **SMA corporate is thriving**: net sales grew every year from SFr 2.61bn (2007) to
  SFr 6.20bn (2016), net income SFr 605m in 2016 (Exhibit 3). MEPD is ~5% of corporate sales and
  has "the lowest gross margins in the company." It is the sick division inside a healthy firm,
  which is why the internal comparison hurts so much.

> "Volume could always be increased by taking low-priced business, but this reduced
> profitability. Most people within MEPD looked to new products as a major source of both new
> volume and profits."

Everyone agrees new products are the answer. Nobody can ship one.

## Amman's Residue

Jacob Amman ran the division "almost single-handedly" until his death in 2014. Two years later
he is still the operating culture.

Rodolphe Lindt, product development manager for integrated circuits:

> "Jacob was very authoritarian with me and others. As a result, those working for Amman who
> were most successful were political and manipulative. People around here did not extend
> themselves very much to disagree with Amman. The way to disagree with him was in a
> manipulative way."

> "Amman had a significant impact on our organization with all of us reflecting him in our
> managerial styles. **We are all more authoritarian than before.** I am less willing to let my
> people make mistakes... The pressure and unrealistic standards were transmitted down to people
> throughout the organization. This results in our commitments often being unrealistic."

> "There is little group activity and decision making by the top team except where there is a
> specific problem. **It is not a natural group.** We are never together... There is no
> cohesiveness in the group reporting to Amman and there is none now."

That last clause — *"and there is none now"* — is the case's quietest and most damning line. The
top team was never a team, and changing its members did not make it one.

**The paradox worth raising in class:** Amman was authoritarian *and* an OB enthusiast. Adelheid
Ragatz, one of SMA's organizational psychologists, "claimed that Amman was better read in the
field than she was." He attended leadership development programs and went through 360-degree
feedback. Knowing the theory changed nothing about the norms he set. That is a direct challenge
to any "educate the leader" prescription.

## The Program Spichty Killed

In 2013 Amman started a division-wide OD program in four phases: (1) individual management
styles, (2) group effectiveness, (3) **interfunctional coordination**, (4) organization-wide
problems.

When Spichty took over he asked for a status report and was told the program "had had a positive impact on the division, but that the uncompleted Phase III, dealing with the improvement of **interfunctional coordination**, was particularly needed. In light of business difficulties and his relative newness to the division, Spichty decided to discontinue the program."

He cancelled, on cost grounds, the one intervention aimed at the exact problem he is now paying consultants to diagnose. Two years later he writes a memo naming "communication, coordination" as a priority. This is the hinge of the case — use it.

## Spichty: Structure Changed, Nothing Else

Forty years old, Diploma and Doctorate in physical chemistry (Zurich), joined SMA in 2002 as a chemist, **his entire career in R&D**, no line experience, appointed GM June 2014. In a company where "manufacturing... was widely regarded as the function through which one could advance to the top," his appointment "was considered unusual."

What he did:

1. Moved headquarters from Grenzach back to Bienne (at corporate's urging).
2. Consolidated product development under Guillaume Tell in Bienne; relocated the market
   development groups to Bienne while the development groups themselves stayed at the plants.
3. **Replaced all his key managers except Tell** — Clerval (manufacturing), Falkniss (marketing),
   Bern (controller), Sesemann (sales, the only internal promotion).
4. Separated marketing from sales: *"Marketing is a strategic function, as opposed to a
   day-to-day function."*
5. **Eliminated the market planning function** as part of 2015–16 cost-cutting and handed its
   responsibilities to the new marketing department.
6. Pushed hard on delivery and service, which measurably improved.

Every item is structure, staffing, or cost. Not one touches how anyone is measured or rewarded. And #5 deletes the capability that "worked with customers on emerging product needs" — in a division whose stated growth strategy is new products.

### The contradictory criticism

The managers' complaints about Spichty do not agree with each other, which is itself data:

> "He does not listen too well. His interruptions of others prevent him from hearing others' opinions and make it seem as if he really does not want criticism. What's more, he has been **too soft on me. He should be holding me to my goals.** I have not met some of these goals and he should be climbing all over me."

> "You get the same record back from him regardless of what you say. It is safe to be open with him and tell him what's on your mind but he does not listen."

> "Spichty is too gentlemanly, is not tough enough, has not demonstrated risk taking, and is encumbered by SMA's corporate philosophy and standards."

> "**Wave-makers are not wanted in the division and are being pushed out. People at the top do not create or confront conflict.**"

He is simultaneously too dominant and too soft; safe to be open with and not listening. The
consistent thread is not a personality trait — it is that disagreement goes in and nothing comes
out. He absorbs conflict instead of resolving it.

## Four Functions, Four Scoreboards

This is the analytical core. Each group is measured on something different, and every accusation
maps cleanly onto a measure.

| Function                           | Measured on                                                                                        | Therefore wants                         | Accuses others of                                                                                   |
| ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Manufacturing** (Clerval)        | Plant gross margin, delivery, variances — plants are **profit centers** compared across all of SMA | High prices, long runs, no special runs | Sales of giving margin away; Marketing of fantasy forecasts; PD of unmanufacturable designs         |
| **Sales** (Sesemann)               | **Sales volume**, no commission                                                                    | Any order, fast delivery                | Marketing of bad information; plants of "putrid" service                                            |
| **Marketing** (Falkniss / Gessler) | Forecasts, market share, new-product pipeline                                                      | Product-line extensions, premium prices | Manufacturing of sniping and risk-aversion; PD of missed dates                                      |
| **Product Development** (Tell)     | Project completion                                                                                 | Clear written specs, schedule control   | Marketing of vague specs; Sales of withholding call reports; Technical Staffs of ignoring deadlines |

Henry Clerval, the manufacturing manager, states the mechanism himself and then fails to draw
the conclusion:

> "There is a feeling of mutual distrust between Sales and Manufacturing because Manufacturing believes Sales is not putting enough of a price on the products. **This is a typical problem that results when two groups have different goals.**"

A production superintendent, on the same seam:

> "There is a breakdown in common agreement when it comes to pricing. Sales will sell for
> anything and the plant won't buy it unless 40% margin is involved."

### The disappearing carrot

Manufacturing's name for Marketing's forecasting: Marketing "would come to the plant and project
a market of millions of Swiss francs for a new IC or chip (the carrot)," Manufacturing would run
samples and invest, "only to find out six months or a year later that Marketing was now
projecting much smaller sales and profits."

The plants' response is rational — they now distrust every projection, which is precisely what
makes the next new product late.

### Marketing is a one-year-old department staffed by salespeople

The function everyone blames for forecasting was "established as a separate department just a
year earlier." Most of its people "transferred from the Sales Department." Gessler was a district
sales manager. "Most marketing specialists were recent technical or business graduates with one
or two years of sales experience."

Spichty created a strategic function and staffed it with the least experienced people available,
then deleted the market planning group that held the customer-facing knowledge. Manufacturing
concluding that "Marketing lacked the ability to forecast market trends accurately and was
generally incompetent" is unkind, but it is not baseless — and the cause is a staffing decision,
not a character defect.

Uri Gessler on the corporate ceiling:

> "While corporate financial people will admit that we need a different set of criteria, they
> informally convey to us that we are doing a lousy job, and it makes us run conservatively. The
> corporate environment is not a risk-taking one... An innovative sensor signal control product
> took seven years in product development... their advantages were lost as the development
> process took seven years instead of the original three years projected for it."

Sesemann on Sales vs. Marketing:

> "Conflicts are not resolved based on facts. Instead there are accusations. I don't trust them
> [Marketing] and I don't trust that they have the capability to do their jobs."

And note *why* their numbers differ: "Sales based its forecasts on customer canvassing while
Marketing used analytical tools to develop its projection." Two legitimate methods, no agreed
tiebreaker, so the disagreement resolves as a character judgment.

## The Meeting Where Nothing Is Decided

Two full days every 28 days, ~20 people, chaired by Gessler with Spichty at the far end of the
table. It is a ritual, and the case describes its mechanics with unusual care:

> "If a function had not met its goals, as was often the case, new deadlines would be set. While
> problems encountered were always described, **the issue of slippage in goals and the underlying
> reasons for it were rarely discussed.** Differences in opinion usually proved very hard to
> resolve. **People would end them by agreeing to disagree** and moving on to the next item on
> the agenda. While tempers flared occasionally, open hostility or aggression was rarely
> expressed in the meetings. Afterward, however, people often met in pairs or small groups in the
> hallways, over coffee, or in individual offices to continue the debate."

Everything real happens in the hallway. And the person who knew most about the project under
discussion — Klara Lavenza, a marketing specialist — "was not present."

The consequence, visible in the product record:

- The **focus divider** for the television market was "killed and resurrected four times with
  different parts of the organization having differing knowledge of its status at given points in
  time." At one point salespeople were taking sample orders for a product Manufacturing and
  Marketing had already killed.
- The **personal identification card** coating change: Marketing got a commitment from division
  management, the plants "completely distrusted Marketing's judgment of the need for this
  change," and after "two years of dragging their feet, the conversion was nowhere near
  completion." A commitment from the top was simply not executed, and nothing happened as a
  result.

Gessler, who chairs it:

> "I never sleep well on the night before the meetings... We spend long hours in these meetings
> but people just don't seem to stick to their commitments to accomplish their objectives by a
> given date. Projects are slipping badly and we just can't seem to get them moving. In my
> opinion, we also have some projects that should be killed, but we can't seem to be able to do
> that, either. Frankly, if I had it to do over again, I would not take this job. After all, **how
> much marketing am I really doing?** I seem to spend most of my time in meetings getting others
> to do things."

Spichty started attending in 2016 at Marketing's request, "often became involved in the
discussion of a new product, particularly its technical aspects. Frequently he explained
technical points to others who did not understand them." The GM was brought in to break
deadlocks and is instead participating as the division's best physical chemist.

## Key Takeaways

- **The measurement system is the culture.** Plants on gross margin, Sales on volume, Marketing
  on forecasts, PD on project completion — with no shared measure above them. Every interpersonal
  accusation in this case is a scoreboard difference wearing a personality costume. Clerval names
  the mechanism and still blames Sales.
- **Spichty pulled the structural levers and none of the cultural ones.** New org chart, new
  people, new function boundaries; identical incentives, and the OD program deleted. Chatman &
  Cha's three levers are selection, socialization, and reward — he reshuffled selection and
  ignored the other two.
- **Conflict is not the problem; the absence of a resolution mechanism is.** Open hostility is
  rare. That is the pathology, not the cure. "Agreeing to disagree" plus hallway politics is
  exactly the manipulative-disagreement norm Lindt says Amman trained into everyone, still
  running two years after his death.
- **Culture survives its author.** Amman is dead, five of six direct reports are new, and the
  division still behaves like Amman runs it. Any answer of the form "replace the leader" is
  already disproven inside the case.
- **The recession story is false and everyone believes it.** Sales are flat; margin fell 73%. The
  shared explanation the managers offer Spichty is contradicted by Exhibit 1, and its function is
  to make the problem external and nobody's fault.
- **Cost-cutting ate the growth strategy.** Market planning was eliminated to save money in a
  division whose only agreed path out is new products. The seven-year sensor product is what the
  resulting cycle time looks like.

## Open Questions

- Spichty asks whether the conflict is "serious." If the honest answer is "the conflict is
  correct behavior given your metrics," what is the intervention — change the metrics (a
  corporate-level fight over the plant-as-profit-center model that runs across all eight
  divisions), or build a division-level forum with actual decision rights? Only the second is
  within his authority, which may be why he reaches for the first as an excuse.
- Is a division-level shared measure even possible when SMA compares plant gross margins across
  the whole corporation and plant managers' promotability depends on that comparison? The
  plant managers' career incentive points outside MEPD entirely.
- Was cancelling the OD program a genuine error, or the correct read that a norms program cannot
  survive a 73% margin collapse? If the latter, when *is* the moment to run Phase III?
- Spichty is criticized for being both too dominant and too soft. Is that one behavior — absorbing
  conflict without resolving it — or two different constituencies wanting incompatible things
  from him?
- Marketing is blamed for forecasts it was staffed a year ago to make, by people who deleted the
  market planning group. How much of "Marketing is incompetent" is a real capability gap that
  now has to be fixed on its own terms, regardless of who caused it?
- What does the (B) case do to this diagnosis? Worth not over-committing in class before seeing it.
