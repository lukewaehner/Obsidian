> [!abstract] One-line version
> Ten years watching 100+ companies try to remake themselves yields one structural claim —
> transformation is a sequence of eight phases that cannot be skipped or compressed, takes five
> to ten years, and fails through eight specific errors, of which the two most lethal are
> starting without enough urgency and declaring victory before the change reaches the culture.

## The Setup

> "Over the past decade, I have watched more than 100 companies try to remake themselves into
> significantly better competitors."

Named: Ford, Landmark Communications, General Motors, British Airways, Eastern Airlines,
Bristol-Myers Squibb. Under banners including "total quality management, reengineering,
rightsizing, restructuring, cultural change, and turnaround" — but with one basic goal, "to make
fundamental changes in how business is conducted in order to help cope with a new, more
challenging market environment."

The distribution is the first finding: "A few of these corporate change efforts have been very
successful. A few have been utter failures. **Most fall somewhere in between, with a distinct
tilt toward the lower end of the scale.**"

Two general lessons before the errors:

> "The change process goes through a series of phases that, in total, usually require a
> considerable length of time. **Skipping steps creates only the illusion of speed and never
> produces a satisfying result.**"

> "Critical mistakes in any of the phases can have a devastating impact, slowing momentum and
> negating hard-won gains."

## The Eight Errors, and the Eight Steps

The article is organized as errors; the sidebar states them positively as steps. They pair, but
the names don't match one-for-one, so hold both:

| # | Error (the article's spine) | Step (the sidebar's positive form) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Not establishing a great enough sense of urgency | Establishing a sense of urgency |
| 2 | Not creating a powerful enough guiding coalition | Forming a powerful guiding coalition |
| 3 | Lacking a vision | Creating a vision |
| 4 | Undercommunicating the vision by a factor of ten | Communicating the vision |
| 5 | Not removing obstacles to the new vision | Empowering others to act on the vision |
| 6 | Not systematically planning for, and creating, short-term wins | Planning for and creating short-term wins |
| 7 | Declaring victory too soon | **Consolidating improvements and producing still more change** |
| 8 | Not anchoring changes in the corporation's culture | Institutionalizing new approaches |

### Error 1 — Not establishing a great enough sense of urgency

> "**Well over 50% of the companies I have watched fail in this first phase.**"

Why: executives "underestimate how hard it can be to drive people out of their comfort zones";
"grossly overestimate how successful they have already been in increasing urgency"; lack patience
("Enough with the preliminaries; let's get on with it"); or are "paralyzed by the downside
possibilities" — defensive senior employees, dropping morale, events spinning out of control,
short-term results jeopardized, the stock sinking, and being blamed for creating a crisis.

The diagnosis underneath, which is the article's standing distinction:

> "A paralyzed senior management often comes from having **too many managers and not enough
> leaders.** Management's mandate is to minimize risk and to keep the current system operating.
> Change, by definition, requires creating a new system, which in turn always demands leadership."

Scope note worth keeping: "If the renewal target is the entire company, the CEO is key. **If
change is needed in a division, the division general manager is key.**"

**Bad results are "both a blessing and a curse."** Losing money catches attention but removes
maneuvering room; good results give resources but make the case harder.

Because of "an almost universal human tendency to shoot the bearer of bad news," executives often
use outsiders — Wall Street analysts, customers, consultants — to deliver the unwanted
information. The purpose, in one European CEO's words:

> "**To make the status quo seem more dangerous than launching into the unknown.**"

In a few successful cases a crisis was *manufactured*: one CEO "deliberately engineered the
largest accounting loss in the company's history"; one division president "commissioned
first-ever customer satisfaction surveys, knowing full well that the results would be terrible.
He then made these findings public."

**The threshold, and the number to remember:**

> "When is the urgency rate high enough? From what I have seen, the answer is when about **75% of
> a company's management** is honestly convinced that business as usual is totally unacceptable."

### Error 2 — Not creating a powerful enough guiding coalition

Goes well beyond "the head of the organization is an active supporter." The chairman/president/GM
"plus another five or 15 or 50 people" develop a shared commitment. It "never includes all of the
company's most senior executives because some people just won't buy in, at least not at first" —
but it is "always pretty powerful — in terms of titles, information and expertise, reputations,
and relationships."

Sizing: three to five people in year one; **20 to 50 in a big company before much progress is
possible in phase three and beyond.** Sometimes includes board members, a key customer's
representative, "or even a powerful union leader."

> "Because the guiding coalition includes members who are not part of senior management, it tends
> to operate outside of the normal hierarchy by definition. This can be awkward, but it is clearly
> necessary. **If the existing hierarchy were working well, there would be no need for a major
> transformation.**"

Two named failure modes, both relevant to MEPD:

- "Sometimes they have **no history of teamwork at the top** and therefore undervalue the
  importance of this type of coalition."
- "Sometimes they expect the team to be led by a **staff executive** from human resources,
  quality, or strategic planning instead of a key line manager. No matter how capable or dedicated
  the staff head, **groups without strong line leadership never achieve the power that is
  required.**"

Off-site retreats of two or three days are the usual vehicle, often a series over months.

### Error 3 — Lacking a vision

A vision "always goes beyond the numbers that are typically found in five-year plans" and
"says something that helps clarify the direction in which an organization needs to move." First
draft usually from one person, "a bit blurry," then three to twelve months of coalition work.

The European company example: the first pass held two-thirds of the final ideas — global reach,
preeminence in certain businesses — but the central idea of "getting out of low value-added
activities" came only after months of discussion.

> "Without a sensible vision, a transformation effort can easily dissolve into **a list of
> confusing and incompatible projects**... the reengineering project in the accounting department,
> the new 360-degree performance appraisal from the human resources department, the plant's
> quality program, the cultural change project in the sales force will not add up in a meaningful
> way."

The two failure pictures are vivid: a company that "gave out four-inch-thick notebooks" spelling
out "procedures, goals, methods, and deadlines" with no statement of where it was leading — most
employees "were either confused or alienated" and it "probably had just the opposite effect"; and
the executive whose vision came out as "a barely comprehensible 30-minute lecture" with the real
elements "buried — deeply."

> "**A useful rule of thumb: If you can't communicate the vision to someone in five minutes or
> less and get a reaction that signifies both understanding and interest, you are not yet done
> with this phase.**"

### Error 4 — Undercommunicating the vision by a factor of ten

Three patterns, with the arithmetic that gives the error its name:

1. A single meeting or single communication — "about **0.0001%** of the yearly intracompany
   communication" — and then surprise that few people understand.
2. Lots of speeches by the head of the organization — still only about **0.0005%** of total yearly
   communication.
3. Newsletters and speeches *plus* "some very visible senior executives still behave in ways that
   are antithetical to the vision. The net result is that **cynicism among the troops goes up,
   while belief in the communication goes down.**"

Transformation needs hundreds or thousands of people "willing to help, often to the point of
making short-term sacrifices," and they won't unless they believe useful change is possible.
Phase four is hardest when the sacrifices include job losses — "for this reason, successful
visions usually include new growth possibilities and the commitment to treat fairly anyone who is
laid off."

The method is to fold the vision into existing traffic: business-problem discussions,
performance appraisals, quarterly reviews, employee Q&As. "They turn boring, unread company
newsletters into lively articles about the vision. They take ritualistic, tedious quarterly
management meetings and turn them into exciting discussions of the transformation." The guiding
principle: "Use every possible channel, especially those that are being wasted on nonessential
information."

Then the part that matters most — the 60-year-old plant manager who "has spent precious little
time over 40 years thinking about customers" and changed anyway, helped by high urgency,
membership in the guiding coalition and the vision-creation team, the constant communication, and
peer and subordinate feedback.

> "Communication comes in both words and deeds, and the latter are often the most powerful form.
> **Nothing undermines change more than behavior by important individuals that is inconsistent
> with their words.**"

### Error 5 — Not removing obstacles to the new vision

"To some degree, a guiding coalition empowers others to take action simply by successfully
communicating the new direction. But communication is never sufficient by itself."

The elephant in the path is sometimes "in the person's head," but "in most cases, the blockers are
very real":

- **Structure** — "narrow job categories can seriously undermine efforts to increase productivity
  or make it very difficult even to think about customers."
- **Systems** — "sometimes compensation or performance-appraisal systems make people choose
  between the new vision and their own self-interest."
- **People** — "perhaps worst of all are bosses who refuse to change and who make demands that are
  inconsistent with the overall effort."

The blocker story: a company made good progress through phase four, then stalled "because the
officer in charge of the company's largest division was allowed to undermine most of the new
initiatives. He paid lip service to the process but did not change his behavior." Motives "were
complex" — disbelief, personal threat, fear he couldn't produce both change and the expected
operating profit. And crucially, the other officers "did virtually nothing to stop the one
blocker": no history of confronting problems like this, fear of the officer, a CEO worried about
losing a talented executive.

> "The net result was disastrous. Lower-level managers concluded that **senior management had lied
> to them** about their commitment to renewal, cynicism grew, and the whole effort collapsed."

> "In the first half of a transformation, no organization has the momentum, power, or time to get
> rid of all obstacles. **But the big ones must be confronted and removed.**"

### Error 6 — Not systematically planning for, and creating, short-term wins

> "**Most people won't go on the long march unless they see compelling evidence in 12 to 24
> months** that the journey is producing expected results."

The win has to be unambiguous — quality up on specific indices, the decline in net income
stopping, a successful product introduction, market share up, productivity or customer
satisfaction measurably better. "The result is not just a judgment call that can be discounted by
those opposing change."

> "**Creating short-term wins is different from hoping for short-term wins. The latter is passive,
> the former active.**"

The worked example — a U.S. manufacturer whose guiding coalition produced a visible new product
introduction about **20 months** in, having selected it at **six months** against four criteria:

1. it could be designed and launched in a relatively short period;
2. it could be handled by a small team of people devoted to the new vision;
3. it had upside potential;
4. the team "could operate outside the established departmental structure without practical
   problems."

"Little was left to chance, and the win boosted the credibility of the renewal process."

And a defence of deadline pressure: "when it becomes clear to people that major change will take
a long time, urgency levels can drop. Commitments to produce short-term wins help keep the urgency
level up and force detailed analytical thinking that can clarify or revise visions."

### Error 7 — Declaring victory too soon

> "While celebrating a win is fine, **declaring the war won can be catastrophic.** Until changes
> sink deeply into a company's culture, a process that can take **five to ten years**, new
> approaches are fragile and subject to regression."

Twelve reengineering efforts: in all but two, "victory was declared and the expensive consultants
were paid and thanked when the first major project was completed after two to three years. Within
two more years, the useful changes that had been introduced slowly disappeared. In two of the ten
cases, it's hard to find any trace of the reengineering work today."

Typically the root problems start earlier — insufficient urgency, a weak coalition, an unclear
vision — "**but it is the premature victory celebration that kills momentum.** And then the
powerful forces associated with tradition take over."

The mechanism is a pincer, and it is the sharpest observation in the article:

> "Ironically, it is often a combination of change initiators and change resistors that creates
> the premature victory celebration. In their enthusiasm over a clear sign of progress, the
> initiators go overboard. They are then joined by resistors, who are quick to spot any
> opportunity to stop change. After the celebration is over, **the resistors point to the victory
> as a sign that the war has been won and the troops should be sent home.** Weary troops allow
> themselves to be convinced that they won. **Once home, the foot soldiers are reluctant to climb
> back on the ships.**"

The alternative: use the credibility from short-term wins "to tackle even bigger problems... go
after systems and structures that are not consistent with the transformation vision and have not
been confronted before," and "pay great attention to who is promoted, who is hired, and how people
are developed."

**The seven-year quantification**, worth copying exactly — amount of change per year on a 1–10
scale: year 1 = 2, year 2 = 4, year 3 = 3, year 4 = 7, year 5 = 8, year 6 = 4, year 7 = 2.

> "The peak came in year five, **fully 36 months after the first set of visible wins.**"

### Error 8 — Not anchoring changes in the corporation's culture

> "Change sticks when it becomes '**the way we do things around here**,' when it seeps into the
> bloodstream of the corporate body. Until new behaviors are rooted in social norms and shared
> values, they are subject to degradation as soon as the pressure for change is removed."

Two factors:

1. **Show people the causal link explicitly.** "When people are left on their own to make the
   connections, they sometimes create very inaccurate links. For example, because results improved
   while charismatic Harry was boss, the troops link his mostly idiosyncratic style with those
   results instead of seeing how their own improved customer service and productivity were
   instrumental." One company "was relentless": time at every major management meeting spent on
   why performance was increasing, article after article in the company newspaper.
2. **Succession.** "If the requirements for promotion don't change, renewal rarely lasts. **One bad
   succession decision at the top of an organization can undermine a decade of hard work.**" Poor
   succession follows from boards not being "an integral part of the renewal effort." In three
   cases the change champion was the *retiring* executive and the successor was "not a resistor"
   but "not a change champion" either; two CEOs didn't resist their boards "because they felt the
   transformation could not be undone by their successors. **They were wrong.** Within two years,
   signs of renewal began to disappear."

### The closing caveat

> "In reality, even successful change efforts are messy and full of surprises. But just as a
> relatively simple vision is needed to guide people through a major change, so a vision of the
> change process can reduce the error rate."

## Kotter Against MEPD

Spichty's two years running MEPD are a near-complete set of the eight errors. This mapping is the
single most useful thing to walk into Friday with — and it doubles as practice for the In-The-Wild
implementation plan.

| # | Kotter's error | MEPD |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Urgency | **Fails, and inverted.** Managers believe the problem is the economy, so urgency is attributed *externally*. Kotter wants ~75% convinced business as usual is unacceptable; in MEPD roughly nobody is. Kotter's "blessing and a curse" is exactly Spichty's bind — bad results got attention and removed his room to spend |
| 2 | Guiding coalition | **Fails.** "It is not a natural group. We are never together." Five of six direct reports replaced in 2014–15 — no history of teamwork at the top, Kotter's named failure mode. And the OD program that preceded Spichty was run out of the organizational-psychology staff function, which Kotter says "never achieve[s] the power that is required" |
| 3 | Vision | **Fails.** Spichty's GLF memo is a list: growth, then operating margin ("a close second"), then morale, communication, coordination, and "the proper balance of long- and short-range efforts." Four-plus priorities and a ranking. It does not survive the five-minute test |
| 4 | Communication | **Fails on deeds.** The 28-day meetings are the channel and they're spent on project status. Spichty says coordination matters, attends to explain technical points, and cancelled the coordination program — "nothing undermines change more than behavior by important individuals that is inconsistent with their words" |
| 5 | Removing obstacles | **Fails, and this is the real one.** "Compensation or performance-appraisal systems make people choose between the new vision and their own self-interest" is a literal description of plant-gross-margin profit centers inside a division that needs cross-functional new products. And the blocker-boss story is the ID card coating: two years of foot-dragging against a division-management commitment, with no consequence |
| 6 | Short-term wins | **Fails.** The opposite happened — the focus divider "killed and resurrected four times," the card conversion "nowhere near completion" after two years. Kotter's four criteria for picking a first win (short launch, small devoted team, upside, **operates outside the established departmental structure**) read as a direct prescription |
| 7 | Declaring victory too soon | **Partial.** The delivery/service improvement is Spichty's one unambiguous win. Kotter says spend that credibility on the systems nobody has confronted. Spichty banked it and moved on |
| 8 | Anchoring in culture | **Fails.** Amman is two years dead and "we are all more authoritarian than before." Promotion in SMA still runs through manufacturing and plant gross margin — "if the requirements for promotion don't change, renewal rarely lasts." Spichty *is* the bad succession Kotter describes: not a resistor, but not a change champion |

> [!important] The bridge to Chatman & Cha
> Kotter's error 8 and [[Reading - Chatman and Cha, Leading by Leveraging Culture]] are the same
> claim from two directions. Kotter: change fails unless it reaches the culture last. Chatman &
> Cha: culture is what makes strategy execute in the first place, and it lives in selection,
> socialization, and **rewards**. Kotter's error 5 (appraisal systems forcing a choice against the
> vision) is Chatman's Tool 3 failing. Chatman even cites Kotter & Heskett for the finding that
> strong culture only pays off long-run when it promotes change.

## Key Takeaways

- **Sequence is the whole theory.** Eight phases, no skipping: "skipping steps creates only the
  illusion of speed." The errors are numbered because the order is the content.
- **Over half of all efforts die in phase one.** Urgency is not a mood, it is ~75% of management
  convinced the status quo is unacceptable — and manufacturing a crisis is a legitimate move.
- **Managers vs. leaders is the load-bearing distinction.** Management minimizes risk and keeps
  the current system running; change requires building a new one. Paralysis at the top is a
  symptom of the ratio.
- **The coalition must sit outside the hierarchy, and must be line-led.** If the hierarchy worked,
  you wouldn't need the transformation. A staff-led change team has no power.
- **Communication is under-done by a factor of ten, and deeds outrank words.** One visible
  executive behaving inconsistently converts the whole communication program into evidence of
  cynicism.
- **Short-term wins are engineered, not awaited** — chosen early against explicit criteria,
  delivered in 12–24 months, and unambiguous enough that opponents can't discount them.
- **Error 7 is the most counterintuitive:** the thing that kills transformations is often the
  celebration, and initiators and resistors build it together. Peak change came in year *five*.
- **Anchoring is a promotion problem.** If who gets promoted doesn't change, nothing sticks. One
  succession decision can undo a decade.

## Open Questions

- Kotter says the division GM is key when change is needed in a division — but error 5 tells
  Spichty to remove an obstacle (the plant-as-profit-center measurement) that is corporate policy
  across eight divisions. Does the model have anything to say to a leader whose biggest blocker is
  above him, other than "fight corporate"?
- The urgency threshold (~75%) and the timeline (5–10 years) are stated with precision but sourced
  to "from what I have seen." How much of this is measurement and how much is pattern-matching on
  100 cases with no control group of firms that did all eight and still failed?
- Errors 1 and 7 are in tension. Manufacturing a crisis raises urgency; but Kotter also says
  "commitments to produce short-term wins help keep the urgency level up." If urgency has to be
  sustained for five to ten years by repeated crisis and repeated wins, when does the organization
  actually operate?
- Error 8 says change must become "the way we do things around here." Chatman & Cha's 173-firm
  study says culture is set at founding and switching models later makes firms *less* successful.
  Read together, is a genuine culture change in a 150-year-old firm realistic, or is Kotter
  describing something rarer than his 100 cases imply?
- For the In-The-Wild report: the syllabus offers Kotter **or** Lewin. Kotter is eight steps and
  narrative; Lewin is unfreeze–change–refreeze and structural. Which is easier to make specific
  about "sequencing, milestones, and expected outcomes" without being "overly simplistic"?
