# Case — Cynthia Carroll at Anglo American (A)

Case for [[Session 06 - Case Application II]] (Tue Sep 29) · [[Organizational Behavior]]

> [!cite] Citation
> Mukunda, Gautam, Lisa Mazzanti, and Aldo Sesia. *Cynthia Carroll at Anglo American (A)*.
> Harvard Business School Case 9-414-019, rev. 18 Nov. 2021. Harvard Business Publishing course
> packet. PDF: `~/Documents/Northeastern/fall-2026/organizational-behavior/session-06/`

> [!abstract] One-line version
> Four months into the job, the first non-South African and first woman to run a 90-year-old
> mining company hears about the 29th death of the year. She has to decide whether to do what no
> mining company has done: shut down its most profitable, most dangerous mine. Her mandate is to
> cut costs and repair relations with the government, and the shutdown would cost about
> $8 million a day. The deaths come out of a culture, a structure, and a language that were all
> built for giving orders, not for listening.

> [!tip] What this case is actually for
> It follows the SMA culture case and Kotter's change reading, so it is the **change** case. SMA
> asked why a culture produces bad outcomes. This one asks how a new leader with no authority
> inside the subsidiary moves a culture that treats deaths as the price of doing business. Read
> the shutdown decision as a Kotter **urgency** move and ask whether it is enough: it creates
> urgency, but it does not by itself change the supervisors, the language, or the hierarchy
> that produce the deaths.

## The Decision

June 27, 2007. Carroll is flying back from the Rustenburg platinum mines when Ralph Havenstein,
CEO of the majority-owned subsidiary Anglo American Platinum, tells her there has been another
fatality. That makes **29 deaths so far in 2007**. Between 2002 and 2006 the company averaged
**46 deaths a year**.

> "The organization is out of control. I will not support operations that are killing people."

The case ends with her question: **should she shut down the mines**, something "no company had
ever done before"?

The (A) case stops at the decision. Argue from what it gives you, not from what happened later.

## Current Structure

Liao's first prep question.

**Group level: a holding company of independent fiefdoms.**

- Operations or exploration in **40 countries**. **162,000 employees**, two-thirds in South
  Africa. 90% of operations in developing countries.
- "Largely a collection of mining businesses that operated independently." On whether the mining
  heads ever met: "We don't."
- "The heads of the businesses acted like **tribal chiefs**. If they were asked to do something
  around safety or people development, etc., they would tell you to get lost."
- The history explains the structure. Apartheid-era sanctions and capital controls pushed the
  company into unrelated South African businesses. By the 1990s it controlled about 25% of the
  Johannesburg stock market. Trahar (CEO 2000–07) had started selling off non-core assets.

**The chain of command to the problem:**

```
Carroll (Group CEO, London)
  └─ Havenstein (CEO, Anglo American Platinum; majority-owned, separately run)
       └─ Head of mining
            └─ Rustenburg general manager (~30,000 workers, 9 shafts)
                 └─ middle managers → front-line supervisors → miners
```

Carroll is **several layers removed** from the rock face, and the unit she wants to shut down is a
subsidiary with its own CEO. Whether the call is hers alone is itself a structural question.

**Rustenburg by the numbers:**

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Share of Amplats | 20% of tons mined, **35% of steady-state operating profit** |
| Revenue | about **$8 million per day** |
| Depth | shafts 1.5 km down, then 3–4 km horizontal. The rock face can be 5.5 km from the surface |
| Working height | stopes about **1 m high**. All mined rock goes to the surface, so there is pressure to mine only the 1 m reef |
| Yield | about 3 g platinum per ton. One ring takes about 280 tons of ore |
| Labor intensity | about 20,000 people where an open pit needs about 2,000 |
| Deaths | **16 of the 29** group deaths, Jan–Jul 2007 |
| Workforce | about 70% illiterate, 11 official languages, migrants from Botswana and Zimbabwe |

## Key Challenges

**1. The deaths, and how people think about them.** Fall of rock is the main killer. The
conditions change daily because each blast exposes new rock. The workforce has written the
deaths off:

- An executive on "zero harm": "Cynthia, it will never happen in our lifetime."
- "It's not as if you could say publicly anything less than zero harm would be acceptable,
  regardless if it was achievable or not." Zero harm is a slogan everyone repeats and nobody
  believes.
- Whitcutt: "mining was a manly endeavor, like going to war."
- Moody-Stuart on the prevailing attitude: injured workers "took shortcuts, did not always follow
  the rules; they were stupid. That is just the way it is."

**2. A mandate that pulls the other way.** The board judges her on:

- **cost containment**: Citigroup says $560M–$1.1B could be cut
- **government relations**: "She offered a clean slate with the government"
- a **share price** that trails BHP Billiton and the industry

A shutdown works against all three: it forgoes revenue, it risks a fight with the government,
and it spooks investors. Her shareholder base is fragmented too. Old South African holders
want dividends, UK institutions hold it for the FTSE 100, and the state-owned Public Investment
Corporation is a new holder.

**3. Legitimacy.** She is four months in, the first non-South African and the first woman in
charge, an aluminum executive in a mining company, running a company closely tied to apartheid
and still "run almost entirely by white executives" over a mostly black workforce. Ramphele's
verdict, "She's tough enough," shows that her toughness was an open question.

**4. Where the resistance is.** Senior management has come on board. The resistance is in the
**middle**:

- Woicke: "Procedures were in place, but the attitude within the organization was not where it
  needed to be."
- Ndlovu: "It was our **front line supervisors** that were the most resistant to change."
- With platinum prices at record highs, supervisors are poached constantly. **High turnover**
  means any culture change has to be re-taught to each new supervisor.

## Underlying Organizational Design Issues

Liao's third question, and where the analysis should go deepest. The deaths are a symptom. The
root causes are designed into the organization.

- **A language built for one-way orders.** Fanagalo is about 2,000 words, "most of them
  commands," and was developed so white supervisors could direct African laborers. A black South
  African told Moody-Stuart: "fundamentally the language operates in the **imperative**. The
  dialogue is one way—supervisor to worker." A miner who sees a crumbling roof has no words to
  challenge the supervisor. The safety problem is also a communication problem.
- **Hierarchy that punishes questions.** Solidarity's McMaster: "Employees were not allowed to
  question management decisions. When a general manager came back from visiting the operations
  the discussion was mostly about financial and production targets. The human element was rarely
  if ever addressed." What gets measured is tons and profit, so that is what gets managed. This
  is SMA's lesson again.
- **Decentralization without shared standards.** Carroll: "there wasn't consistency, there
  weren't universal standards and there wasn't a clear objective of safety being number one."
  Tribal-chief autonomy means every business sets its own safety floor.
- **Incentives that reward risk.** The economics of the 1 m reef, a large fixed cost base ("when
  you stop production, the revenue loss flows right to the bottom line"), and record prices all
  push toward more output from the same shafts.
- **A racial and ethnic divide inside the chain of command.** White management over black
  miners. Moody-Stuart: "When you build **trust between the constituencies**, you fix safety."
  Trust is the missing element, and the structure makes it hard to build.

## The Shutdown Tradeoff

| For shutting Rustenburg | Against |
| --- | --- |
| A shock the whole system can't ignore: a credible signal that safety outranks output | About **$8M a day** lost, with a high fixed cost base, while the board wants cost cuts |
| 16 of 29 deaths happened there, so the shutdown is targeted where the problem is | **Idle mines get more dangerous.** Geology shifts without miners noticing, and "continuity of operations was considered essential" to safety |
| The law already allows it. Section 54 (Exhibit 6) lets inspectors halt a mine, so a voluntary halt runs ahead of the regulator | About 30,000 workers, many migrants with families to support. Who gets paid during the halt? |
| It fits her track record: 80% fewer lost-time accidents at Alcan, and "even if you are the only person with that view… that's what you do" | Anglo is "not markedly different" from its peers. Singling itself out could look like an admission of guilt |
| It creates time to retrain, re-audit, and reset standards before restarting | The government relationship she was hired to repair. A halt that hurts output and jobs could strain it |
| She frames it as the industry's responsibility, not only Anglo's | A shutdown alone treats the symptom. Supervisors, Fanagalo, and the incentives are all still there when the mine reopens |

## The Exhibits Worth Reading Closely

**Exhibit 8 undercuts the "we're improving" story.**

| | 2005 fatalities | 2006 fatalities | 2005 LTIFR | 2006 LTIFR |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rustenburg Section | 14 | 7 | 1.84 | **2.28** |
| Amplats managed | 24 | 18 | 2.06 | **2.52** |
| Anglo American managed | 46 | 44 | 0.94 | **1.16** |

(LTIFR is the lost-time injury frequency rate: injuries that cost at least one full shift, per
200,000 hours worked.)

**Fatalities fell while the injury rate rose at every level.** The board's "safety performance
had improved" is based on deaths, a small and noisy number. The broader injury measure got
worse. Then Rustenburg had **16 deaths by July 2007**, more than in all of 2005.

**Exhibit 7:** South African mining deaths fell from 290 to 200 between 2002 and 2006, and platinum
from 53 to 40. The industry improved, but was 20–25% short of the 2003 summit's target of 20% a
year. "A South African miner was dying every 44 hours."

**Exhibit 1:** revenue went from $17.2B to $25.0B and EPS from $1.87 to $3.73 between 2004 and 2006,
while the **dividend per share fell** from $1.08 to $0.70. The dividend-focused South African holders
are already getting less.

**Exhibit 2:** Platinum's operating profit nearly tripled in 2006, from $854M to $2,398M, and rose from
12.6% to 23.4% of the total. She would be halting a big part of the group's fastest-growing profit
source at the top of the market.

## Frameworks to Apply

- **[[Reading - Kotter, Leading Change]]:**
  - **Error 1, urgency.** Fatalism means nobody thinks business as usual is unacceptable. A
    shutdown manufactures that urgency.
  - **Error 2, guiding coalition.** Senior management is on board, but the coalition doesn't reach
    the supervisors, who are the actual blockers.
  - **Error 8, anchoring in culture.** Supervisor turnover means gains leak out unless selection,
    training, and promotion change.
  - Appendix B's implementation plan asks for Kotter explicitly.
- **[[Reading - Chatman and Cha, Leading by Leveraging Culture]]:** the three levers are selection,
  socialization, and reward. Supervisors are selected and rewarded for production, and new ones
  are socialized in Fanagalo and hierarchy. Which lever does a shutdown pull? Arguably none. It
  is a signal, not a mechanism.
- **Congruence model (Appendix B, internal analysis).** Work (deep, variable, dangerous) doesn't
  fit people (70% illiterate, multilingual, migrant), which doesn't fit the formal organization
  (production metrics, decentralized standards), which doesn't fit the informal organization
  (fatalism, machismo, one-way language). Safety fails where these meet.
- **PESTLE (Appendix B, external analysis):**
  - Political: the post-apartheid government and B-BBEE
  - Economic: record platinum prices and the supervisor labor market
  - Social: race, language, migrant labor, HIV/AIDS
  - Legal: the Mine Health and Safety Act, Section 54

## Ready for Class

Liao will probe for **at least one each**:

- **Observation:** Exhibit 8 shows deaths falling while the injury rate rose at Rustenburg, at
  Amplats, and group-wide. The organization was measuring the wrong thing and believed it was
  getting safer.
- **Question:** Is the decision even Carroll's to make? Rustenburg belongs to Anglo American
  Platinum, which has its own CEO in Havenstein. Does the group CEO overriding a subsidiary on
  operations prove her point about "tribal chiefs," or repeat the command-and-control habit she
  is trying to end?
- **Recommendation:** Shut **Rustenburg** (not every mine), but only as the first move in a change
  plan, not the whole plan:
  - **Short term:** halt Rustenburg; keep paying workers; bring in the unions and the Department
    of Minerals together; run a shaft-by-shaft safety audit with a published standard for
    reopening. Idle-mine hazards call for a small, trained crew to keep monitoring the rock.
  - **Medium term:** one group-wide safety standard that overrides local autonomy; retrain
    supervisors and hold them accountable, with safety in their pay and promotion; give workers
    the explicit right to stop work, in their own languages.
  - **Long term:** replace Fanagalo's command vocabulary with two-way communication; build
    trust between the constituencies (Moody-Stuart's point); make safety the first item every
    time a general manager reports back, ahead of tons.

**Counterargument to be ready for:** "It will cost too much and the mine is more dangerous idle."
Reply: halting under monitoring is safer than running on a 16-deaths-in-six-months trajectory.
And a sign that the company is serious is worth more than the lost days of output, because the
real cost is the supervisors' belief that "it will never happen in our lifetime."

> that's what you do." (Carroll, on Alcan)

## Key Takeaways

- **The deaths come out of the organization's design.** Command language, production metrics,
  autonomous business units, and a racial hierarchy together produce "shortcuts" and then blame
  the people who took them.
- **Change is blocked in the middle.** The top agrees and the workers are the ones dying. The
  resistance sits with the supervisors, and they turn over constantly.
- **Watch what is being measured.** Fatality counts said "improving" while the injury rate said
  "worse."
- **A shutdown creates urgency; it doesn't change the culture.** It works only if the rest of
  Kotter's steps follow.

## Open Questions

- Anglo was "not markedly different" from its peers. If every company is equally bad, is this a
  firm problem or an industry problem? Carroll frames it as the industry's responsibility, which
  suggests one CEO's shutdown can't solve it.
- Does the shutdown reproduce the command-and-control habit it is meant to break? One leader
  ordering all work to stop is still one-way dialogue from the top.
- For the group case report, if the team ever returns to this case, the Appendix B rubric wants
  "root causes," not symptoms. Is the root cause the culture, or the incentive structure that
  keeps the culture going?
