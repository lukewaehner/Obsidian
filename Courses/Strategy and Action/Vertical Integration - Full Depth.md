## 1. Core Idea

- Corporate strategy decides _where_ to compete (scope); business strategy decides _how_. Scope has three dimensions: vertical, product, geographic.
- The firm's boundary is set by **relative cost**: internalize when the transaction costs of using markets exceed the administrative costs of organizing internally.
- Transaction costs push activity _in_; administrative costs push it _out_. Neither is zero, and the comparison is always relative — this is the whole analytical engine of the chapter.
- Transaction-specific investment plus uncertainty is the main force toward integration. Scale mismatch, capability development, strategic dissimilarity, weak incentives, inflexibility, and compounded risk are the forces against it.
- The answer is always "it depends" — and it depends on _firm-specific_ resources and strategy, not just industry conditions. Two firms in the same industry can rationally integrate to opposite degrees.

## 2. Key Terms

### 2.1 Transaction costs (priority block)

| Term                           | Definition                                                                                                            |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Market mechanism**           | Coordination by independent decisions guided by prices. Adam Smith's _invisible hand_ — needs no conscious planning   |
| **Administrative mechanism**   | Coordination by managerial decision imposed through hierarchy. Chandler's _visible hand_ — depends on active planning |
| **Firm**                       | An organization of individuals bound by employment contracts to a central contracting authority                       |
| **Transaction costs**          | The costs of using the market: search, negotiation and contract drafting, monitoring, and enforcement                 |
| **Administrative costs**       | The costs of organizing the same activity inside a hierarchy                                                          |
| **Relative cost (Coase)**      | The rule determining firm boundaries — internalize when market transaction costs exceed internal administrative costs |
| **Interaction costs**          | Alternative term (Hagel & Singer) for the time and money spent whenever parties exchange goods, services or ideas     |
| **External transaction costs** | Costs of dealing outside the firm; largely a function of the operating environment (slides)                           |
| **Internal transaction costs** | Costs of organizing inside — recruiting, retaining, setting up operations; tend to rise with firm size (slides)       |

### 2.2 Administrative costs of internalization (priority block)

| Term                              | Definition                                                                                                                                    |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Minimum efficient scale (MES)** | The output level at which an activity reaches full scale efficiency. If internal need falls well below it, in-house production is inefficient |
| **Distinctive capabilities**      | Superior competence in an activity; specialists serving many customers develop these faster than captive internal units                       |
| **Strategic dissimilarity**       | The degree to which two vertically linked businesses require different management systems, planning, control, HR and top-management skills    |
| **High-powered incentives**       | Sharp profit-based motivation created by a market interface between buyer and seller                                                          |
| **Low-powered incentives**        | Muted motivation of an internal supplier–customer relationship governed by management systems; consequences are slow and undramatic           |
| **Shared service organization**   | An internal supplier of corporate services forced to compete with external providers, to restore incentive intensity                          |
| **Competitive effects of VI**     | The risk that independent suppliers and customers become less willing to deal with a firm now perceived as a competitor                       |
| **Compounding risk**              | Exposure created when a problem at one owned stage threatens production and profit at every other stage                                       |

### 2.3 Supporting terms (condensed material)

| Term                                          | Definition                                                                                                                            |
| --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Product / geographical / vertical scope**   | The three dimensions of corporate scope: range of products, spread of locations, number of value-chain stages                         |
| **Vertical integration**                      | Ownership of vertically related activities; measured by value added ÷ sales revenue                                                   |
| **Backward / forward integration**            | Taking ownership upstream (own inputs) / downstream (activities previously done by customers)                                         |
| **Full / partial integration**                | All of one stage's output transferred internally / stages not internally self-sufficient                                              |
| **Technical economies**                       | Cost savings from physically integrating processes at one site. Justify **co-location**, not common ownership                         |
| **Transaction-specific investment**           | An asset built for one counterparty, with little value elsewhere. The culprit that converts a competitive market into a dependent one |
| **Bilateral monopoly**                        | One buyer, one seller — no equilibrium price; outcome depends purely on relative bargaining power                                     |
| **Opportunism / strategic misrepresentation** | Exploiting a counterparty's dependence / distorting information to improve bargaining position                                        |
| **Hold-up**                                   | Extracting better terms after the fact because the other party's assets are worthless outside the relationship                        |
| **Incomplete contracts**                      | Contracts that cannot anticipate every future circumstance; why better drafting doesn't solve opportunism                             |
| **Information asymmetry**                     | One party (usually the seller) holds private information the other lacks (slides)                                                     |
| **Principal–agent problem**                   | Owners want value creation; managers may pursue their own interests. A cost of organizing inside firms                                |
| **Spot / long-term contract**                 | One-off market purchase / a specified series of transactions setting terms and responsibilities                                       |
| **Vendor partnership / relational contract**  | Trust-based supplier relationship, sometimes with no written contract at all                                                          |
| **Franchising**                               | Contract letting a franchisee produce and market under the franchiser's trademark and system in a defined area                        |
| **Taper integration**                         | Deliberately mixing in-house supply with outside suppliers/distributors (slides)                                                      |
| **Virtual / hollow corporation**              | A firm whose main function is coordinating a supplier network / the failure state where it loses the ability to adapt                 |
| **Architectural vs. component capabilities**  | Ability to design and coordinate the whole system vs. deep expertise in one part                                                      |
| **Economies of scale / scope**                | Unit-cost reduction from volume of the same thing / cost advantage from applying one resource across several products                 |

## 3. PRIORITY — Transaction Costs and the Scope of the Firm (p. 342)

### 3.1 The setup: two ways to organize economic activity

A capitalist economy is usually called a "market economy," but it actually contains **two** organizing mechanisms:

|                                                  | **Market mechanism**                   | **Administrative mechanism**                   |
| ------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| How decisions are coordinated                    | Independent decisions guided by prices | Managerial decisions imposed through hierarchy |
| Who makes production, supply and input decisions | Each firm/individual separately        | Managers                                       |
| Label                                            | Adam Smith's **invisible hand**        | Chandler's **visible hand**                    |
| Requires conscious planning?                     | No                                     | Yes — depends on active planning               |

### 3.2 Firms are not necessary for complex production

Grant's basement-remodel illustration is the load-bearing example on this page, because it establishes that coordination does **not** require a firm.

- He contracted a self-employed builder, who in turn subcontracted a plumber, an electrician, a joiner, a drywall installer and a painter.
- Several people's activity was coordinated to complete one complex job.
- But none of them were linked by **employment relations** — they were linked by **market contracts**, each with a stated price for a stated scope of work.

**Why this matters:** if markets can coordinate complex, multi-party production, then the existence of firms needs an explanation. "Firms exist because coordination is needed" is not an answer. The real question is what determines which activities get done inside a firm versus between parties coordinated by contracts.

### 3.3 Coase's answer: relative cost

Ronald Coase's answer is **relative cost**. The critical premise is:

> **Markets are not costless.**

Making a purchase or sale involves four cost categories:

| Cost type                             | What it covers                                              | Where you'd see it                                         |
| ------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Search costs**                      | Finding and evaluating a suitable counterparty              | Identifying which contract manufacturers can hit your spec |
| **Negotiating and contracting costs** | Bargaining over terms and drawing up the contract           | Legal fees, months of back-and-forth on a supply agreement |
| **Monitoring costs**                  | Verifying the other party is actually fulfilling their side | Inspecting quality, auditing delivery performance          |
| **Enforcement costs**                 | Arbitration or litigation if a dispute arises               | Suing over a breached supply contract                      |

All four are types of **transaction costs**. (Note 4 in the chapter records that _interaction costs_ has also been used for the same idea — the time and money expended whenever people and companies exchange goods, services or ideas.)

### 3.4 The decision rule

> **If the transaction costs of organizing across markets are greater than the administrative costs of organizing within firms, coordination of productive activity will be internalized within firms.**

Written as a comparison:

- Transaction costs (market) **>** Administrative costs (firm) → **internalize / vertically integrate**
- Transaction costs (market) **<** Administrative costs (firm) → **use the market / buy**

The supplemental slides state the same rule as the **make-or-buy** decision, with one addition worth carrying: **internal costs tend to rise with firm size** (firm-dependent), while **external costs are largely a function of the environment** the firm operates in (environment-dependent).

### 3.5 The three scope questions this rule answers (Figure 13.1)

Figure 13.1 contrasts a _single integrated firm_ (administrative interfaces between units) with _several specialized firms_ (market interfaces between separate companies). The same relative-cost test is applied along all three dimensions:

| Dimension              | The question posed       | Options compared                                                                                                                       |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Vertical scope**     | Which is more efficient? | Three independent companies — one producing steel, one rolling it into sheet, one producing cans — vs. all three stages in one company |
| **Geographical scope** | Which is more efficient? | Three independent companies making cans in the US, UK and Italy vs. one multinational owning canmaking plants in all three             |
| **Product scope**      | Which is more efficient? | Metal cans, plastic packaging and domestic appliances made by three separate companies vs. merged into one                             |

**The point of the figure:** vertical, geographic and product scope are three applications of _one_ analytical question — administrative interface or market interface? This is why the chapter says the same underlying concepts (economies of scope, transaction costs, costs of corporate complexity) apply to all three.

### 3.6 Exam traps on this page

| Trap                                                           | Correction                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| -------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Treating transaction costs as the price paid for the good      | Transaction costs are the costs of _arranging and policing_ the exchange — search, negotiation, monitoring, enforcement — not the purchase price or the production cost                                                   |
| Concluding "markets have costs, so integrate"                  | Both sides have costs. The test is _relative_. Firms are not free either                                                                                                                                                  |
| Treating the four cost types as interchangeable                | Each behaves differently. The internet slashed **search** and payment costs while leaving monitoring and enforcement roughly intact — which is why it moved the boundary toward markets in some industries and not others |
| Saying firms exist because complex activity needs coordination | The basement example disproves this directly. Markets coordinate complex activity fine; firms exist where doing so through contracts is _more expensive_                                                                  |
| Confusing transaction costs with administrative costs          | Transaction = cost of using the market. Administrative = cost of running it internally. Mixing them collapses the comparison the entire chapter is built on                                                               |

## 4. PRIORITY — Administrative Costs of Internalization (pp. 348–353)

**The framing sentence for this whole block:** the existence of transaction costs in intermediate markets does **not** mean vertical integration is efficient. Integration avoids the costs of using the market, but internalizing the transaction creates costs of administration. Whether internal administration is efficient depends on the six factors below.

Grant reaches this section immediately after establishing (p. 348) that specific investments make market contracts inefficient, and that contracts can't be written to fix it because **the future is uncertain** — when a steel producer and a sheet roller agree to build an integrated plant, it is impossible to anticipate every circumstance over the plant's 30-year life, so **contracts are inevitably incomplete**. Section 4 is the counterweight to all of that.

### 4.1 Differences in optimal scale between stages (p. 348)

**Mechanism:** even where transaction costs are real, the transaction costs avoided by integrating may be trivial compared with the inefficiency of producing below minimum efficient scale.

**The FedEx case — the numbers matter:**

- Suppose FedEx needs delivery vans designed and built to its specific requirements. To the extent the van manufacturer must make transaction-specific investments, FedEx has an incentive to avoid the resulting transaction costs by building its own vehicles.
- Would that be efficient? Almost certainly not.
- **FedEx purchases over 40,000 trucks and vans a year. The MES of an assembly plant is 200,000.** (For scale: Ford produced two million commercial vehicles in 2005.)
- The transaction costs avoided are trivial next to the manufacturing inefficiency incurred.

**The brewing parallel (p. 349):** specialist brewers such as Anchor Brewing of San Francisco or Adnams of Suffolk are not backward integrated into cans and bottles, unlike Anheuser-Busch or SAB-Miller. Dedicated canmaking plants _do_ involve specific investments and _do_ create opportunism problems that integration would avoid — but small brewers simply lack the scale for efficiency in can manufacture.

**Direction:** greater dissimilarity in optimal scale between two stages → **greater advantage of market contracts**.

**Trap:** this is not "small firms shouldn't integrate." It is about the _gap_ between the firm's own requirement and the MES of the adjacent activity. A firm can be enormous and still fail this test in a particular stage (Amazon clears MES for chip design, not for fabrication).

### 4.2 Developing distinctive capabilities (p. 349)

**Mechanism:** a company specialized in a few activities can develop distinctive capabilities in them. A specialist serving many different customers gets learning and innovation that a captive internal department does not.

**Evidence for outsourcing:**

- Even large technology-based companies — **Xerox, Kodak, Philips** — cannot maintain IT capabilities matching IT services specialists such as **EDS, IBM and Accenture**.
- The specialists' ability to work with many different customers stimulates learning and innovation.
- If **General Motors'** IT department only serves GM's in-house needs, that does not encourage rapid development of its IT capabilities.

**The critical qualifier — this argument reverses when capabilities are linked:**

- The above assumes capabilities in different vertical activities are **independent of one another**.
- Where one capability _builds on_ capabilities in adjacent activities, vertical integration may help develop distinctive capabilities.
- **IBM's** half-century of success in mainframes owes much to its technological leadership in semiconductors and software.
- **Wal-Mart's** retailing efficiency depends critically on specialized IT and logistics from its in-house departments.

**Direction:** greater need for continual investment in upgrading and extending capabilities within individual activities → **greater advantage of outsourcing** — _unless_ the capabilities are interdependent, in which case integration helps.

**Trap:** this is the most commonly half-remembered item in the section. Stating only the outsourcing side (specialists learn faster) misses that Grant immediately supplies the counter-case. A complete answer names the independence assumption and then names IBM or Wal-Mart.

### 4.3 Managing strategically different businesses (p. 349)

**Mechanism:** scale differences and capability development are part of a wider problem — managing vertically related businesses that are _strategically very different_.

- A major disadvantage to **FedEx** of owning a truck manufacturer is that the management systems and organizational capabilities needed for truck manufacturing differ greatly from those needed for express delivery.
- This may explain the general lack of vertical integration between **manufacturing and retailing**. Integrated design-manufacture-retail companies such as **Zara and Gucci are comparatively rare**; most of the world's leading retailers — **Wal-Mart, Gap, Carrefour** — do not manufacture.
- The differences are not only in organizational capability. They extend to **strategic planning systems, approaches to control, human resource management, and top management styles and skills**.

**De-integration driven by strategic dissimilarity:**

| Firm                     | Action                                              | Reasoning                                                                   |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Marriott**             | Split into Marriott International and Host Marriott | _Owning_ hotels is a strategically different business from _operating_ them |
| **Whitbread plc**        | Divested its breweries                              | Specialized in pubs, restaurants and hotels                                 |
| **Scottish & Newcastle** | Sold off most pubs and hotels                       | Became a specialist brewer                                                  |

**Direction:** greater strategic similarity between two stages (similar key success factors, common resources/capabilities) → **greater advantage of VI over outsourcing**. Greater dissimilarity → outsource.

### 4.4 The incentive problem (pp. 349–350)

**Mechanism:** vertical integration changes the incentives between vertically related businesses.

|                                 | **Market interface**                                          | **Under vertical integration**                                                                                                                                           |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Governance                      | Profit incentives                                             | Corporate management systems                                                                                                                                             |
| Buyer's motivation              | Secure the best possible deal                                 | —                                                                                                                                                                        |
| Seller's motivation             | Pursue efficiency and service to attract and retain the buyer | —                                                                                                                                                                        |
| Incentive strength              | **High-powered incentives**                                   | **Low-powered incentives**                                                                                                                                               |
| Consequence of poor performance | Lose the customer                                             | If Shell's tanker fleet is inefficient and unreliable, employees lose bonuses and the head of shipping may be fired — but these consequences are **slow and undramatic** |

**The partial remedy — shared service organizations (p. 350):** one approach to creating stronger performance incentives inside integrated companies is to **open internal divisions to external competition**. Many large corporations have created shared service organizations where internal suppliers of corporate services (IT, training, engineering) compete with external suppliers of the same services to serve internal operating divisions.

**Direction:** greater need for entrepreneurial flexibility and drive in the separate vertical activities → greater advantage of the high-powered incentives market contracts provide → **greater administrative disadvantage of VI**.

**Trap:** "performance incentives don't exist inside firms" is wrong. They exist; they are just _low-powered_ — slower and less dramatic. The distinction is intensity, not presence.

### 4.5 Competitive effects of vertical integration (p. 350)

**Mechanism — two distinct claims, and the second is the important one:**

**(a) Monopoly extension — the classic cases, but rare.**

- Monopolistic companies have used VI to extend monopoly positions from one stage to another.
- **Standard Oil** used its power in transportation and refining to foreclose markets to independent oil producers.
- **Alcoa** used its monopoly in aluminum production to squeeze independent fabricators of aluminum products to the advantage of its own fabrication subsidiaries.
- **Such cases are rare.** As economists have shown, once a company monopolizes one vertical chain of an industry, **there is no further monopoly profit to be extracted** by extending that position to adjacent vertical stages.

**(b) Deterring counterparties — the greater concern.**

- Vertical integration may make independent suppliers and customers **less willing to do business** with the integrated company, because it is now perceived as a competitor rather than as a supplier or customer.
- After **Disney acquired ABC**, other studios (e.g. **Dreamworks**) became less interested in collaborating with ABC on new TV programming.

**Trap:** answering "vertical integration extends monopoly power" as the main competitive effect gets the emphasis backwards. Grant says the foreclosure cases are rare and economically limited; the realistic cost is the loss of willing trading partners.

### 4.6 Flexibility (p. 350)

**Mechanism:** flexibility cuts **both ways**, and the direction depends on _what kind_ of flexibility is required.

| Type of flexibility required                                                                             | Which structure wins     | Evidence                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Rapid responsiveness to uncertain demand**                                                             | **Market transactions**  | The lack of VI in construction reflects the need to adjust to cyclical demand patterns and to each project's different requirements                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| **Responding quickly to new product opportunities requiring new combinations of technical capabilities** | **Market transactions**  | Some of the most successful recent electronic products — **Apple's iPod, Microsoft's Xbox, Dell's notebook range** — were produced by contract manufacturers. Extensive outsourcing is a key feature of fast-cycle product development throughout electronics                                                                                                                                                     |
| **System-wide flexibility — simultaneous adjustment throughout the vertical chain**                      | **Vertical integration** | **American Apparel**: tightly coordinated integration from its Los Angeles design and manufacturing base to its 160 retail stores allows a super-fast design-to-distribution cycle; VI is also central to its brand identity (Figure 13.4). **Zara**: cut cycle times and maximized market responsiveness through a vertically integrated strategy challenging the industry's dominant contract-manufacture model |

**Trap:** "vertical integration reduces flexibility" is only half the chapter. It reduces _responsiveness to demand uncertainty_ and _technical recombination_, but it increases _system-wide coordination speed_. Naming which flexibility is in play is what separates a correct answer from a memorized one.

### 4.7 Compounding risk (pp. 350–353)

**Mechanism:** to the extent VI ties a company to its internal suppliers, it compounds risk — problems at any one stage of production threaten production and profitability at **all** other stages.

- When union workers at a **General Motors brake plant went on strike in 1998, GM's 24 US assembly plants were quickly brought to a halt.**
- When technology or customer preferences are changing quickly, it is especially likely that poor decisions at one stage have knock-on effects throughout the firm.

**Direction:** the heavier the investment requirements and the greater the independent risks at each stage, the riskier VI is.

### 4.8 Strategy Capsule 13.2 — Making vertical integration work: Zara (pp. 351–352)

This sits inside the priority page range and is the chapter's one sustained counter-example: a case where the administrative costs of integration are _outweighed_ by the coordination benefits.

**Performance:**

- Zara is the main division and brand of the Spanish company **Inditex**, contributing **68% of Inditex's sales**.
- Between 2000 and 2006, Inditex achieved **30% annual sales growth, an 11% net margin, and a 29% return on average equity — well ahead of Gap, H&M and Mango**.
- By end-2006, Zara operated **over 1,400 stores in 40 countries**.

**The speed advantage:**

- Most fashion retailers face a **six-month lag** between completing a design and deliveries reaching stores. Zara can move a new design from drawing board to retail store in **as little as three weeks**.

**How the integrated system produces it:**

| Stage         | Arrangement                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| ------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Design        | Inditex HQ in La Coruña. **Over 40,000 garments designed annually, about one-quarter entering production.** Designers work alongside "market specialists" monitoring sales and trends by region and "buyers" handling procurement and production planning; the three groups jointly select what goes into production                          |
| Fabric        | **Comditex**, a wholly owned Inditex subsidiary, supplies **40% of fabric requirements** for own production. Most fabric is supplied **undyed** — postponing dyeing allows colors to change at short notice                                                                                                                                   |
| Manufacturing | **Close to half** of products made within Zara's local network of own factories and subcontractors who do all sewing; the rest outsourced to third parties                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| Distribution  | Finished goods ironed, labeled with local-currency prices, bagged or hung ready for display, transferred by monorail to the La Coruña distribution center. Stores order **twice a week** and receive shipments **twice a week**. Orders dispatched within **eight hours**, delivered within **24 hours in Europe, 48 in the US, 72 in Japan** |
| Retail        | Zara owns and manages **almost all** its stores — enabling standardized layout and window displays plus close communication with headquarters                                                                                                                                                                                                 |

**The feedback loop:** at each season's start only small numbers of each item are produced and placed in a few lead stores. Zara adjusts production according to market response. Products typically spend **no more than two weeks** in a store. Market specialists provide feedback used both to adjust production levels and to modify design or color on existing items. Designers begin work some **nine months** before a season but adjust continuously.

**Demand-side consequence:** compressed product cycles changed customer behavior — Zara customers visit stores more frequently than is typical and decide faster, knowing garments move quickly and are unlikely to be restocked.

**Why this matters for the priority section:** Zara pays every administrative cost in §4.1–4.7 — scale penalties, strategic dissimilarity between manufacturing and retailing, low-powered internal incentives, compounded risk — and integration still wins, because the coordination benefit (three weeks vs. six months) is worth more than all of them _in this industry, for this strategy_. That is the setup for §4.9.

### 4.9 Assessing the pros and cons (p. 353)

**The conclusion of the priority block:**

- Is vertical integration beneficial? As with most questions of strategy — **it all depends**. There are costs and benefits to both VI and market contracts. The value of the analysis is being able to determine which factors decide the relative advantage of each.
- **Even within the same industry, different companies succeed with very different degrees of vertical integration:**

| Industry                 | More integrated | Less integrated       |
| ------------------------ | --------------- | --------------------- |
| Low-end fashion clothing | **Zara**        | Hennes & Mauritz, Gap |
| Designer clothing        | **Armani**      | Donna Karan           |

- **The key issue:** even when external circumstances are identical, different companies have different resources and capabilities and pursue different strategies — so they will make different vertical integration decisions.

**This is the single most testable sentence in the priority range.** Any question that implies one correct level of integration for an industry is testing whether you know that industry conditions underdetermine the answer.

## 5. Condensed Reference — Everything Else

### 5.1 Scope and corporate strategy (pp. 339–341)

- **Corporate strategy = _where_ a firm competes; business strategy = _how_.** Corporate strategy decides scope along three dimensions.
- **Product scope:** specialized firms (Coca-Cola, SAB-Miller, Gap, Swiss Re) vs. diversified (GE, Samsung, Bertelsmann).
- **Geographical scope:** Clyde's (12 restaurants in DC) vs. Popeye's (US-wide) vs. McDonald's (121 countries).
- **Vertical scope:** Disney (produces films, distributes to cinemas and via ABC/Disney Channel, uses characters in retail and parks) vs. Nike (design and marketing only; outsources manufacturing, distribution, retailing).
- Common concepts across all three dimensions: economies of scope in resources and capabilities, transaction costs, costs of corporate complexity.

### 5.2 The shifting boundary between firms and markets (pp. 342–343)

| Period              | Direction                                          | Cause                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 19th c.–mid-20th c. | Firms absorb market transactions                   | Administrative costs fell: **technology** (telegraph, telephone, computer expanded managers' capacity) and **management techniques** (double-entry bookkeeping, scientific management, rapid method development)                            |
| Late 1960s          | Prediction of a "corporate economy"                | Galbraith (1969) predicted firms' planning advantages would concentrate capitalism in a few giants                                                                                                                                          |
| 1980s–1990s on      | Sharp reversal — **downsizing** and **refocusing** | Turbulence: oil shocks (1973, 1979), end of fixed exchange rates (1972), the integrated circuit, international competition. Under instability, administrative costs rise as the need for flexibility outruns traditional management systems |

Figure 13.2 shows the 500 biggest US companies' share of total private-sector employment falling from just over 20% in the early 1980s to roughly 15–16% by the mid-1990s, staying near that level through 2004.

### 5.3 Defining vertical integration and technical economies (pp. 344–346)

- **VI = ownership of vertically related activities.** Greater ownership and control over successive value-chain stages = greater degree of integration. **Measured by value added ÷ sales revenue** — highly integrated firms (e.g. major oil companies controlling everything from exploration to gasoline retailing) have low bought-in goods relative to sales.
- **Backward** = own your inputs. **Forward** = own activities previously done by customers.
- **Full integration:** all of stage one's output goes to stage two, no third-party sales or purchases. **Partial integration:** stages not internally self-sufficient — "crude-rich" firms like Statoil produce more oil than they refine and sell crude; "crude-poor" firms like ExxonMobil buy crude to keep refineries supplied.
- **Technical economies:** cost savings from physically integrating processes — most steel sheet is made by integrated producers whose plants first produce steel, then roll hot steel into sheet, cutting transport and energy costs. Similar in pulp and paper, and in linking oil refining with petrochemicals.
- **The crucial limit:** technical economies explain the need for **co-location of plants**. They do _not_ explain why **common ownership** is necessary. Answering that requires transaction costs.

### 5.4 Sources of transaction costs in vertical exchanges (pp. 346–348)

**The steel cans value chain:** iron ore mining → steel production → steel strip production → can-making → canning of food/drink/oil.

| Interface           | Governance                                                                | Why                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Steel strip → cans  | Market contracts (Crown Holdings, Ball Corporation buy strip on contract) | Low transaction costs: many buyers and sellers, information readily available, low switching costs. Same for other commodities — few jewelry firms own gold mines, few flour millers own wheat farms |
| Steel → steel strip | Vertical integration                                                      | Technical economies from hot-rolling steel as it is poured force both parties into integrated facilities → **bilateral monopoly**                                                                    |

**The causal chain (memorize this):** technical economies → co-located, purpose-built plants → **transaction-specific investments** → each plant worthless without the partner's → **bilateral monopoly** → no equilibrium price, only bargaining power → **opportunism and strategic misrepresentation** → **hold-up** → market contracting inefficient → integrate.

**Empirical support (p. 348):** among automakers, specialized components are more likely made in-house than commodity items like tires and spark plugs; the same holds in aerospace for company-specific components. In semiconductors, integrated design-plus-fabrication producers (Intel, ST Microelectronics) perform relatively better the more technically complex the circuit — because complexity raises the need for designer–fabricator collaboration.

**Why not just write a better contract?** Uncertainty. Over a plant's 30-year life it is impossible to anticipate all circumstances. **Contracts are inevitably incomplete.**

### 5.5 Table 13.1 — VI vs. outsourcing criteria (p. 354)

| Question                                             | Points toward                                            |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Few firms in the adjacent activity?                  | **VI**                                                   |
| Transaction-specific investments needed?             | **VI**                                                   |
| Large information asymmetries between stages?        | **VI**                                                   |
| Intermediate transactions taxed or regulated?        | **VI**                                                   |
| High uncertainty over the relationship's life?       | **VI**                                                   |
| Stages similar in optimal scale?                     | Dissimilar → **market contracts**                        |
| Stages strategically similar?                        | Similar → **VI**                                         |
| High need for continual capability investment?       | **Outsourcing**                                          |
| High need for entrepreneurial flexibility and drive? | **Outsourcing**                                          |
| Highly uncertain market demand?                      | **Outsourcing**                                          |
| Does VI compound risk across the chain?              | Heavier investment + bigger per-stage risks → VI riskier |

### 5.6 Designing vertical relationships (pp. 353–357)

- Relationships are classified on two axes: **degree of commitment** (resources committed) and **formalization** (how documented).
- The continuum (Figure 13.5), roughly low → high commitment: spot sales/purchases → long-term contracts → agency agreements → franchises → informal supplier/customer relationships → supplier/customer partnerships → joint ventures → vertical integration.

| Form                    | Strength                                                                                                                                                                  | Weakness                                                                                                                                          |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Spot contracts**      | Work under competitive conditions — many buyers and sellers, standard product, no specific investment                                                                     | No protection once specific investment enters                                                                                                     |
| **Long-term contracts** | Avoid opportunism; provide security to make specific investments                                                                                                          | Cannot anticipate all circumstances; risk being too restrictive _or_ so loose they invite opportunism. Acute in IT outsourcing deals of 10+ years |
| **Vendor partnerships** | Security for specific investments, flexibility, incentives against opportunism; may be purely **relational contracts** with nothing written                               | Depend on trust; mutual dependence creates vulnerability                                                                                          |
| **Franchising**         | Combines corporate brand, marketing and systems with local entrepreneurship; gets specific-asset coordination _plus_ high-powered incentives across dissimilar businesses | Franchisee bears most of the risk — its capital, and a flat royalty on sales                                                                      |

**Named examples:** Toyota and Nissan directly produced about 20–23% of their cars' value in the late 1980s vs. Ford ~50% and GM ~70%, yet achieved close supplier collaboration (vendor partnerships). Franchising systems: McDonald's, Century 21, Hilton, Seven-Eleven. Same-industry divergence: Starbucks owns and manages its outlets while most food and beverage chains franchise; Capital One keeps IT in-house as a source of advantage while most banks outsource to IBM and EDS.

**Two design variables beyond make-or-buy:**

1. **Allocation of risk.** Franchisees bear most risk. Oil exploration outsourcing (Chevron, ExxonMobil, Eni with Schlumberger, Halliburton) moved from fixed-price to risk-sharing agreements where the driller often takes project equity.
2. **Incentive structures.** Under-specified contracts invite substandard performance (ready-mixed concrete without stated cement/sand/gravel proportions), but completeness is costly (the $400 Navy toilet seats). Often the best incentive is the promise of future business — fixed-term operating contracts with performance reviews in privatized public services; Toyota and Marks & Spencer vendor partnerships.

### 5.7 Recent trends and the summary (pp. 357–358)

- Growing diversity of **hybrid** relationships reconciling market flexibility with integration's collaboration. Collaborative networks are not new — northern Italy's industrial districts (textiles, packaging equipment, motorcycles). Japanese supplier practice drove a shift from arm's-length purchasing to long-term collaboration with fewer suppliers, single-supplier arrangements, certification and quality programs.
- **Equity stakes deter opportunism:** Commonwealth Bank of Australia took a stake in EDS Australia; pharma firms take stakes in biotechs doing their R&D.
- **Counter-trend:** the internet cut search and payment costs, reviving arm's-length contracting via B2B hubs (Covisint, Elemica, Rock and Dirt).
- **Outsourcing widened** from components to whole chunks of the chain — Hon Hai Precision makes Apple iPods, Nokia phones and Sony's PlayStation.
- **The virtual corporation** risk: outsourcing so much the firm becomes a **hollow corporation**, losing the ability to evolve. The test is whether it retains **architectural capabilities** to manage partners' **component capabilities**. Aero-engine makers outsource key components but keep R&D in those technologies — evidence the two are complements.
- **Chapter conclusion:** the most critical long-run consideration is organizational capability. A firm should restrict itself to activities where its capabilities beat those of firms that specialize in them. The hardest cases involve linkages — a contract manufacturer may build the product more cheaply, but what happens to new product development capability without in-house manufacturing?

### 5.8 Diversification (slides — condensed)

**Rumelt's four types** — classified by % revenue from the primary business and competency sharing across units:

| Revenue from primary | Type                     | Competency sharing                      | Examples                            |
| -------------------- | ------------------------ | --------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| >95%                 | Single business          | One business leverages its competencies | Birkenstock, Coca-Cola, Facebook    |
| 70–95%               | Dominant business        | Dominant + minor businesses share       | Harley-Davidson, Nestlé, UPS        |
| <70%                 | Related-constrained      | All businesses generally share          | ExxonMobil, J&J, Nike               |
| <70%                 | Related-linked           | Some businesses share                   | Amazon, Disney, GE                  |
| <70%                 | Unrelated (conglomerate) | Few or none shared                      | Samsung, Berkshire Hathaway, Yamaha |

**Diversification–performance relationship:** inverted U — rises from single → dominant → **peaks at related** → falls into unrelated.

**Build-Borrow-or-Buy (Capron & Mitchell)** — sequential filter from a strategic resource gap:

1. Internal resources **relevant** (similar to what's needed _and_ superior to competitors')? High → **Build**.
2. Target resources **tradable** by contract? High → **Borrow** (licensing, franchising).
3. Need to be **close** to the partner? Low → **Borrow** with equity (equity alliance, JV).
4. Can you **integrate** the target? High → **Buy** (acquisition). Low → revisit options or reformulate strategy.

Acquisition is the last resort: justified only by low relevance, low tradability, high closeness need. Post-integration failures are common (Microsoft–Nokia).

**Why firms grow:** profits and shareholder returns (failure invites hostile takeover), lower costs via scale, market power, risk reduction through diversification, management motivation.

### 5.9 Global strategy (slides — condensed)

**The central trade-off:** pressure for **cost reductions** vs. pressure for **local responsiveness**.

|                        | Low local responsiveness                                                                                                                                      | High local responsiveness                                                                                                    |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **High cost pressure** | **Global-standardization** — scale and location economies, global division of labor, price competition, minimal adaptation. _Infosys, Lenovo, Siemens Energy_ | **Transnational** — "think globally, act locally." Hard: duplication, complexity, cost. _ABB, Bertelsmann, P&G_              |
| **Low cost pressure**  | **International** — same offering at home and abroad, leveraging home-based competencies. _Harley-Davidson, Rolex, Starbucks_                                 | **Multidomestic** — products perceived as local; costly duplication of functions per country. _Bridgestone, Nestlé, Philips_ |

- **CAGE distance framework:** **C**ultural, **A**dministrative/political, **G**eographic, **E**conomic — the distances creating cost and risk, guiding which countries to enter.
- **Entry modes** by rising investment and control: exporting → long-term contracts (licensing, franchising) → equity alliances → joint ventures → wholly owned subsidiary (acquisition or greenfield).
- **Advantages of expansion:** new markets, lower-cost inputs, new competencies. **Disadvantages:** liability of foreignness, loss of reputation, loss of intellectual property.
- **Porter's Diamond** of national competitive advantage: factor conditions, demand conditions, related and supporting industries/complementors, competitive intensity in the focal industry. Explains persistent clustering (US biotech/software, Germany engineering, Italy fashion, France wine) against the death-of-distance hypothesis.
- **Ghemawat's "10% surprise":** the internationally-crossing share of most activity is far lower than globalization rhetoric implies.

### 1. Amazon — Retail Ad Service

**Source:** trade/news report on Amazon's Retail Ad Service beta (undated; beta announced early 2025). Original reporting credited to Adweek; AWS parallel credited to CNBC.

**What happened**

- Amazon is opening the ad tech behind its own ad business (~$50B, third in US advertising behind Google and Meta) for other retailers to run on their own sites — contextually targeted ads on third parties' product/search/browsing pages, using models trained on two decades of Amazon shopping signals.
- Adopting retailers are plugged into Amazon's existing advertiser base. Amazon captures retail-media profit off its own property *and* gains visibility into rivals' data (flagged as likely FTC interest).
- Explicitly the AWS pattern: infrastructure built for Amazon's own marketplace, later sold as a service to outside firms.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept                                                    | How it applies                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| ---------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Economies of scope in resources/capabilities**           | The ad-tech stack and the trained models are a single resource being applied across a second market at near-zero marginal cost — the textbook justification for widening scope                                                                       |
| **Shared service organization (Grant, incentive problem)** | Grant's remedy for low-powered internal incentives is exposing internal suppliers to external competition. Amazon goes a step further: the internal supplier is turned into a standalone external business, which forces it to meet market standards |
| **The firm boundary moving outward**                       | Most course examples are about pulling activity *in*. This is the mirror image — an activity that was inside the boundary for internal reasons gets converted into a market offering, expanding scope without vertical integration into retailers    |
| **Information asymmetry / data as a resource**             | Access to rivals' shopping data is the strategic prize *and* the regulatory exposure                                                                                                                                                                 |
| **Competitive effects of integration**                     | Adopting retailers hand a competitor visibility into their own demand — the same reluctance mechanism as Dreamworks pulling back from ABC after the Disney acquisition                                                                               |

**Trap to watch:** this is *not* vertical integration — Amazon isn't acquiring retailers or a value-chain stage. Calling it forward/backward integration is the "wrong concept, right-sounding language" error; the correct label is scope expansion via economies of scope / capability externalization.

### 2. Apple Weighs Using Anthropic or OpenAI to Power Siri

**Source:** Mark Gurman, Bloomberg, June 30, 2025.

**What happened**

- Apple is in talks with Anthropic and OpenAI to license LLMs for a rebuilt Siri, sidelining its in-house Apple Foundation Models — though an internal "LLM Siri" project on in-house models continues in parallel. No final decision.
- It asked both firms for custom versions trained to run on Apple's own Private Cloud Compute servers (Apple silicon), arguing that keeping inference on Apple-controlled hardware better protects privacy. After multiple testing rounds, Anthropic was judged the best fit — but talks stalled on price, with Anthropic reportedly seeking a multibillion-dollar annual fee escalating sharply year over year, pushing Apple toward OpenAI or others.
- Build/borrow/buy are all visible at once: Apple explored acquiring Perplexity and Thinking Machines Lab; killed Swift Assist (in-house coding models) in favor of third-party models in Xcode; but keeps on-device models in-house. It has committed a multibillion-dollar 2026 budget to run its own cloud models.
- Talent fallout: the ~100-person foundation models team reacted badly; senior researcher Tom Gunter left; the MLX team threatened to leave and was retained with counteroffers. Meta floated $10–40M packages for its Superintelligence Labs.
- Precedent: Samsung brands features as Galaxy AI while many run on Gemini; Amazon uses Anthropic to help power Alexa+.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Make-or-buy on a core input** | The classic decision, applied to the input executives consider most strategically important |
| **Build-Borrow-or-Buy** | All three branches at once: *build* (Foundation Models, on-device), *borrow* (licensing Claude/ChatGPT — long-term contract, high tradability), *buy* (Perplexity, Thinking Machines explored). Framework predicts borrow: resource is tradable, AI-lab integration capability doubtful |
| **Relevance test** | Executives judged their own models inferior — internal resources fail the "superior to competitors'" half of the test, pushing away from build |
| **Developing distinctive capabilities (Grant)** | The Xerox/Kodak/Philips vs. EDS/IBM/Accenture argument: specialists serving many customers learn faster than a captive internal group |
| **Transaction-specific investment** | The custom model trained for Apple's Private Cloud Compute is specific to Apple — a vendor-side specific investment, hence a long-term contract rather than spot purchasing |
| **Bilateral bargaining / no equilibrium price** | Few credible suppliers, one very large buyer, a bespoke product — no market price, so the deal turns on bargaining power, and stalled there. Apple's counter-leverage: OpenAI and Gemini as alternatives |
| **Hold-up risk on Apple's side** | The escalating annual fee is the anticipated extraction; Apple's intent to reclaim ownership "if its own technology improves" hedges against it |
| **Core competence erosion (Prahalad & Hamel)** | The team's fear that outsourcing Siri portends outsourcing other features is the hollow-corporation risk from the inside; talent departures accelerate it — capability degrades as soon as the decision is signaled |
| **Architectural vs. component capabilities** | Retain architectural capability (device integration, on-device models, privacy infra) while buying the component (frontier model) — Grant's aero-engine finding (outsource component, keep R&D) applied |

**Trap to watch:** "Apple should build because AI is strategically important" is incomplete — strategic importance argues for *control*, not *ownership*, and Table 13.1 (greater need for continual capability investment → greater advantage of outsourcing) cuts the other way. The defensible answer names both forces and the architecture compromise.

### 3. Johnson Controls / Tyco Merger

**Source:** Bob Tita and Dana Mattioli, *The Wall Street Journal*, Jan. 25, 2016.

**What happened**

- A $14B merger creating a commercial-building systems provider with $30B+ in annual sales — Johnson Controls' HVAC plus Tyco's security and fire-suppression lines. Johnson Controls holders take ~56%; the firm is renamed Johnson Controls PLC but keeps Tyco's Irish domicile, making it an **inversion**.
- Both had shrunk first: Tyco fell from ~$41B in sales as a conglomerate (electronics, surgical equipment) to ~$10B; Johnson Controls shed automotive-parts units that had been two-thirds of revenue, pushing toward higher-margin businesses. Rationale: activist-investor dissatisfaction with sprawling conglomerates, plus belief in combining same/adjacent-industry businesses for clout.
- Synergies: ≥$150M/yr tax savings and ≥$500M cost savings over three years; effective tax rate 18–19% (Tyco had paid 12% vs. Johnson Controls' 29%). Staged handoff: Molinaroli leads 18 months, then Oliver becomes CEO.
- Terms: 11% premium, below the 20% average for prior-year "merger of equals" deals; heavily stock-structured. Comparables: Pfizer–Allergan ($155B, split contemplated), Dow–DuPont (>$120B, to split into three).

**Framework mapping**

| Concept                                                     | How it applies                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| ----------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Rumelt's diversification types**                          | Tyco runs unrelated (conglomerate) → dominant/related; Johnson Controls exiting auto parts does the same. The merged firm is closer to **related-constrained** — HVAC, security, and fire suppression share the building-systems customer, channel, and service competencies |
| **Diversification–performance inverted U**                  | The curve traversed deliberately: both retreated from the low-performing unrelated end toward the peak at related diversification, then combined *within* that band                                                                                                          |
| **Economies of scope**                                      | A complete equipment suite for building owners plus data services (power consumption, predictive maintenance) — one customer relationship and one data platform across more product lines                                                                                    |
| **Principal–agent problem / activist investors**            | Activists are the corrective mechanism; "failure to create shareholder value invites a hostile takeover" is the same discipline in another form                                                                                                                              |
| **Table 13.1 — taxes/regulations as a cost of contracting** | Tax and regulatory treatment of transactions shapes firm boundaries; the inversion is that criterion driving the *legal* boundary rather than the operational one                                                                                                            |
| **CAGE — administrative/political distance**                | The Irish domicile is a pure administrative-distance play — note the board must meet in Ireland for major decisions, a real governance cost                                                                                                                                  |
| **Build-Borrow-or-Buy — Buy branch**                        | Full acquisition where closeness needs are high (integrated suite) and resources aren't separately tradable; the staged CEO handoff mitigates integration risk                                                                                                               |

**Trap to watch:** "bigger" and "more diversified" are not the same — the article's framing is bigger *but more focused*. Reading this as increasing diversification inverts the case.

### 4. Meta / Scale AI

**Source:** Cory Weinberg, *The Information*, June 10, 2025.

**What happened**

- Meta took a 49% stake in data-labeling firm Scale AI for $14.8B, structured so cash goes to Scale's existing shareholders (Accel, Index, Founders Fund, Greenoaks) rather than the company. Scale valued at $28B, up from $13.8B the prior year. Meta had the cash to buy outright.
- Scale CEO Alexandr Wang moves to a senior Meta role running a new "superintelligence" lab, with other senior Scale staff; Jason Droege was in talks to become Scale CEO.
- The sub-50% structure is attributed to regulatory caution — Meta had just come off a trial arguing its Instagram/WhatsApp buys were anticompetitive. Motivation: Zuckerberg's dissatisfaction with Llama 4's reception and aggressive AI-researcher recruiting.
- Customer conflict: Scale's customers include Alphabet (one of its largest), Microsoft, Amazon, Nvidia, OpenAI. Meta is itself a customer and plans to increase labeling spend.
- Financials: ~$870M revenue last year, >$2B expected this year, ~$150M EBITDA loss; had missed targets set with investors. Structural headwind: some large AI developers are bringing labeling in-house.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept                                          | How it applies                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Equity alliance on the make-or-buy continuum** | Partial ownership, greater commitment than a contract, "try before you buy," short of parent–subsidiary control. Analogue: Coca-Cola/Monster 18.5% stake                                                                              |
| **Why not acquire?**                             | Acquisition is the last resort — but here the binding constraint is regulatory, not integration capability (a variant worth naming)                                                                                                   |
| **Quasi-backward integration**                   | Data labeling is an upstream input to Meta's training; Meta moves up its value chain without full ownership                                                                                                                           |
| **Competitive effects (Disney–ABC mechanism)**   | Scale's customers (Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon, Nvidia, OpenAI) now buy a critical input from a firm nearly half-owned by a direct competitor. Grant predicts they become less willing to deal — the open question the article raises |
| **Customers backward-integrating**               | "Some big AI developers are bringing this work in house" is the same threat from the other side — buyers internalizing Scale's stage weakens its standalone position and makes the alliance more attractive to it                     |
| **Acqui-hire / resource relevance**              | The resource acquired is arguably Wang and the team, not the labeling operation; human capital's low tradability pushes past a contract toward equity                                                                                 |
| **Compounding risk**                             | Removing the founder from a company that had missed targets, with its customer base primed to defect, concentrates risk in the asset Meta just paid for                                                                               |

**Trap to watch:** don't call this an acquisition — 49% is deliberately below control and the cash goes to shareholders, not the balance sheet. And regulatory avoidance isn't the *only* motive: the talent transfer and guaranteed supply stand on their own.

### 5. Nvidia Muscles Into GPU Cloud Market

**Source:** Anissa Gardizy, *The Information*, June 11, 2025.

**What happened**

- Nvidia launched DGX Cloud Lepton, a marketplace letting developers rent GPU servers directly from Nvidia, aggregating inventory across a dozen-plus cloud providers (CoreWeave, Nebius) plus its own; AWS and Microsoft to be the first large clouds to join.
- Providers face a bind: the service gives Nvidia direct customer relationships (competitive), but abstaining risks losing business to rivals who join — better to <cite index="0-0">"play with [Nvidia] in the sandbox than not at all."</cite> **Parasail declined**, CEO Mike Henry unwilling to lose control of his customers, though he understood why others joined (expensive GPUs force revenue maximization).
- Customers rent through an **Nvidia Cloud Account** (mirroring AWS/Azure) — whoever owns the account owns the relationship. Nvidia offers European VC portfolio companies up to $100K in credits.
- Scale: Nvidia's AI cloud/software on pace for ~$2B/yr (≈ what CoreWeave, >$75B market cap, generated renting Nvidia chips); Nvidia says it could someday reach $150B, rivaling AWS. Lepton is built on tech from Lepton, a GPU cloud provider Nvidia bought for several hundred million; the 2023 DGX Cloud ran inside other clouds' data centers.
- Countervailing: AWS and other big clouds are developing their own AI chips. The DOJ has been investigating Nvidia's dominance. Nvidia's position: Lepton connects partners with customers and does not compete.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Forward vertical integration** | A component supplier moving downstream into its customers' stage — the mirror of the Walmart case |
| **Mutual encroachment** | Clouds backward-integrate into chip design while Nvidia forward-integrates into cloud; each move is partly defensive — integration decisions as strategic responses, not just cost calculations |
| **Competitive effects of VI** | Customers become less willing to deal with a supplier now seen as a rival. Parasail's refusal is the pure case; the dozen who joined show why the mechanism often fails to bite — defection is individually costly when rivals participate |
| **Supplier bargaining power** | Why resistance collapses: providers depend on Nvidia for the input that *is* their business, so declining risks more than lost marketplace revenue — Five Forces supplier power enabling vertical encroachment |
| **Taper integration** | Nvidia sells its own inventory *and* aggregates partners' — partly integrated, partly reliant on outside firms. Also the 2023 DGX Cloud, which ran on other companies' data centers |
| **Buy, then build** | The Lepton acquisition supplied the technology — Build-Borrow-or-Buy with low tradability of a working marketplace platform |
| **Foreclosure and antitrust** | Rare foreclosure cases (Standard Oil, Alcoa) don't mean regulators ignore them — DOJ interest here is leveraging chip dominance into an adjacent stage |
| **Switching costs / customer ownership** | The Nvidia Cloud Account is the mechanism — whoever owns the account owns the relationship; Henry's objection is stated in those terms |

**Trap to watch:** Nvidia's stated position — merely connecting partners to customers — is a claim to evaluate, not repeat; the account structure and direct customer relationships are evidence against it. But don't overstate: participants are voluntary and gain a demand channel, so this isn't Standard Oil–style foreclosure.

### 6. Walmart, Dean Foods — Grocers Move Into Milk Bottling

**Source:** *The Wall Street Journal* (undated in the provided excerpt; events run through 2020, after Dean's November 2019 bankruptcy filing).

**What happened**

- Kroger, Walmart, and Albertsons built their own milk-bottling plants for store-brand milk, threatening national processors in a ~$40B US milk industry. Dean Foods (recently the largest US processor) and Borden both filed for bankruptcy, blaming grocers' focus on cheap milk used as a loss leader.
- Dean supplied Walmart for decades under continuous cost pressure — a Louisville plant re-engineered bottles from 60g to 57g (~half a cent/gallon), processed up to 1.2M gal/week with 70% going to 130 Walmart stores, and prioritized Walmart during disruptions. When prices fell in 2015, Walmart wanted cuts; CEO Gregg Tanner refused, consolidating regional brands under the national **DairyPure** label and holding branded pricing — but Walmart's buyers believed shoppers no longer distinguished store brands from premium milk costing up to twice as much.
- Upstream supply was consolidating: by 2017 the **eight largest dairy co-ops marketed 54% of US milk**, making direct sourcing plus in-house processing feasible. March 2016: Walmart announced a Fort Wayne plant to supply 600+ Walmart/Sam's Club stores; Dean estimated losing ~100M of the 2.6B gallons it sold annually. Successor CEO Ralph Scozzafava argued bottling was a poor use of Walmart's resources; Walmart proceeded.
- Cascade: mid-2017 DairyPure volume −7.5%; Dean closed plants across seven states, rerouting created logjams. Early 2018 Food Lion (1,000+ stores) ended its Dean contract (~50M gal/yr) and switched to **Kroger, which processes ~90% of the milk it sells**. Feb 2019 board explored a sale; Nov 2019 Dean filed bankruptcy; Feb 2020 Dairy Farmers of America agreed to buy it — a DFA exec noting grocers with plants still need outside supply.
- Background demand pressure: per-capita US milk consumption down ~40% over four decades (substitution to water, juice, almond/oat alternatives, declining cereal); ~3,300 dairy herds lost in 2019.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Backward vertical integration** | Walmart, a downstream retailer, takes ownership of the upstream bottling stage — the definitional case |
| **Transaction-specific investment and hold-up** | The Louisville plant sent 70% of output to 130 Walmart stores and prioritized Walmart — a specific investment in all but name, and the hold-up ran in Walmart's favor: sustained price pressure Dean couldn't refuse until it did, then lost the business |
| **Bilateral dependence, asymmetric power** | Bilateral monopoly has no equilibrium price and turns on bargaining power. The asymmetric version: one side has alternatives (many processors, plus the option to integrate), the other has concentrated exposure |
| **Table 13.1 — number of firms in the adjacent stage** | Upstream co-op consolidation (eight co-ops = 54% of US milk) made direct sourcing practical — note it was the *supply* stage consolidating that opened the move, not processing |
| **Optimal scale (FedEx test, passing this time)** | FedEx shouldn't build vans at 40,000 units vs. a 200,000 MES; Walmart clears the bar — one plant serves 600+ stores, milk a top-ten item. Scozzafava's "poor use of resources" argument is the FedEx logic applied — and it failed on the numbers |
| **Low differentiation as the enabling condition** | Buyers judged shoppers no longer distinguished store brands from premium milk; Dean's DairyPure branding bet lost. Where the intermediate good is a commodity, integration is easy and the supplier's only defense — brand — is weak |
| **Loss leader / complementary demand** | Milk draws traffic that buys other items, raising the strategic value of controlling its cost and availability above its own margin |
| **Compounding risk** | Dean's plant-closure cascade created rerouting logjams at surviving plants — a linked-stage failure propagating, the GM brake-strike pattern in slow motion |
| **Kroger as the completed case** | Processing ~90% of the milk it sells and selling to Food Lion — forward-integrated far enough to become a merchant supplier to rival retailers |
| **Five Forces overlay** | Buyer power (concentrated grocers, low switching costs, high volume/buyer), substitutes (water, juice, plant-based, declining cereal), and entry-by-customer all compound — Dean's failure isn't down to any single force |

**Trap to watch:** two independent causes of Dean's collapse — retailer backward integration *and* secular demand decline/substitution; citing only one is incomplete. Note also Dean's own error (holding branded prices into a market that stopped valuing the brand) is firm-level execution, distinct from industry structure.

# Vertical Integration — Stuckey & White

**Source:** John Stuckey & David White, "When and When _Not_ to Vertically Integrate," _Sloan Management Review_, Spring 1993, pp. 71–83. Authors were partners in McKinsey & Company's Sydney office.
**Scope of these notes:** pp. 71–76 in full depth (when to integrate, VMF, market power, life cycle). Pp. 77–83 covered in condensed form at the end — key concepts and frameworks only, not full detail.

---

## Core Idea

- Vertical integration is a **means of coordinating stages of an industry chain**, used when ordinary buying and selling between the stages (bilateral trading) doesn't work well.
- It is expensive, hard to reverse, and management's historical track record with it is poor — so the default answer is _don't_.
- There are exactly **four legitimate reasons** to integrate; vertical market failure is by far the most important one.
- Most bad integration decisions come from spurious justifications (reducing earnings cyclicality, "capturing more value," getting closer to customers) and from ignoring cheaper quasi-integration alternatives.
- Governing rule: **do not vertically integrate unless it is absolutely necessary to create or protect value.**

---

## Key Terms & Definitions

### Core Concepts

- **Vertical integration** — Bringing two or more stages of an industry chain under common ownership, as a substitute for trading between them.
- **Industry chain** — The sequence of production/distribution stages that turn raw inputs into a delivered end product (e.g., cement production → aggregate mining → concrete mixing → transport → construction).
- **Bilateral trading** — Ordinary arm's-length buying and selling between two adjacent stages. Integration is only warranted when this arrangement _isn't_ beneficial.
- **Forward integration** — Moving downstream, toward distribution and the final customer.
- **Backward integration** — Moving upstream, toward suppliers and raw inputs.
- **Quasi-integration** — Arrangements that get some benefits of integration without full ownership (long-term contracts, joint ventures, alliances, licenses, asset ownership, franchising). Introduced on p. 76 as one of the two things managers ignore when they overintegrate; detailed later in the article.

### The Integration Decision (Table 1 criteria)

- **Setup costs** — The one-time cost of entering the adjacent stage: capital (equipment, acquisitions), systems development, training.
- **Transaction costs** — Costs of trading with an outside party: information collection and processing, legal, sales and purchasing functions.
- **Risk (of trading)** — Exposure to unreasonable price changes, supply or outlet foreclosure, and being insulated from the market (e.g., missing technical changes and new products developed outside the firm).
- **Coordination effectiveness** — How well adjacent stages are synchronized: run lengths, inventory levels, capacity utilization, delivery performance, quality.
- **Core tradeoff** — Integration typically _lowers_ trading risk and transaction costs but _raises_ setup costs, and its promised coordination gains are frequently illusory. The criteria pull against each other.

### Vertical Market Failure (VMF)

- **Vertical market failure (VMF)** — A vertical market "fails" when transactions in it are too risky _and_ the contracts that would fix that risk are too costly or impossible to write and administer.
- **Three typical features of a failed vertical market** — (1) few buyers and sellers, (2) high asset specificity/durability/intensity, (3) frequent transactions. Any one alone doesn't prove VMF; all three together strongly indicate it.
- **Bilateral monopoly** — One buyer facing one seller. Each tries to leverage its monopoly position, producing costly haggling and attempted exploitation as conditions shift.
- **Bilateral oligopoly** — A few buyers facing a few sellers. Creates severe coordination problems because each player must share a fixed surplus with many others and may compete it away to customers.
- **Asset specificity** — The degree to which an asset's value depends on one particular trading relationship; its value drops sharply if redeployed elsewhere.
- **Site specificity** — Buyer and seller locate fixed assets physically adjacent to cut transport and inventory costs (e.g., mine next to refinery).
- **Technical specificity** — One or both parties invests in equipment usable only with the other party, with little alternative-use value.
- **Human capital specificity** — Employees develop skills tailored to one specific buyer or customer relationship.
- **Asset intensity** — How capital-heavy the assets are; magnifies the _cost_ of any disruption because of the cash and opportunity costs incurred while idle.
- **Asset durability** — How long-lived the assets are; extends the _time horizon_ over which lock-in risks apply.
- **Switching costs** — Cost to a buyer or seller of changing trading partners. High specificity + intensity + durability drives these up for both sides, which is what shrinks an apparently large market into locked pairs.
- **Pre-investment vs. postinvestment phase** — Before assets are committed, many parties bid competitively; after the commitment, the specific assets lock the two parties together into a bilateral monopoly. Postinvestment lock-in is the most frequent cause of VMF.
- **Transaction frequency** — How often the two parties transact. Frequent transactions mean more haggling and more chances for exploitation, raising costs — but only matters much when asset specificity is also high.
- **Uncertainty** — Inability to foresee future conditions (timing/severity of breakdowns, supply-demand swings), which makes complete contracts impossible to draft.
- **Bounded rationality** — Herbert Simon's concept: humans have limited capacity to solve complex problems, so they cannot write contracts covering all future contingencies. Oliver Williamson applied it to market failure.
- **Opportunism** — Williamson's concept: given the chance, parties will cheat or deceive in commercial dealings when they perceive it's in their long-term interest.
- **R&D / information market failure** — Markets for R&D and new-product ideas fail because the good being sold is information: the buyer can't value it before seeing it, and the seller won't reveal it before payment. Ripe for opportunism, so integration or strong property rights are often needed.

### Market Power

- **Market power (in a vertical context)** — When one stage of a chain can extract abnormally high returns from an adjacent stage; the dominated stage then has an incentive to integrate into the dominant one.
- **Economic surplus** — The return a business earns _in excess of its full costs of being in the business, including a fair return on capital_. This — not value added, not customer proximity — is what drives superior returns.
- **Value added** — Full cost plus surplus at a stage, less the cost of inputs bought from the preceding stage. Distinct from surplus: a stage can add enormous value and still earn a poor return.
- **Barriers to entry (via integration)** — When most incumbents are integrated, an entrant must enter _all_ stages at once, raising required capital and minimum efficient scale.
- **Minimum efficient scale** — The smallest output at which a competitor can operate at competitive cost; integration pushes this upward.
- **Thin market** — An intermediate-product market with too little independent trading volume to support a nonintegrated player (e.g., bauxite and alumina before the 1970s).
- **Price discrimination** — Charging different prices to segments with different price sensitivities. Only sustainable if the low-price segment can't resell into the high-price one.
- **Forward integration as an anti-resale device** — Buying into the price-sensitive downstream segment so the supplier sells to _itself_ there, eliminating the resale channel that would otherwise wreck price discrimination.
- **Exclusive dealership** — A distribution outlet contractually restricted to one manufacturer's product; an inherited network of these is a real entry barrier for newcomers.

### Industry Life Cycle

- **Market development (young-industry) integration** — Forward integrating to build demand for a new product where no distribution or end-use market yet exists. Treated as a special case of VMF.
- **Free rider (imitator)** — A competitor that captures the benefit of another firm's market-development spending without paying for it. If free riders can imitate, the market-developing integrator never recoups its investment.
- **Declining-industry gap filling** — Integrating to replace independents who are exiting a shrinking industry, before the survivors on that stage become concentrated enough to exploit you.

### When Not to Integrate (opening only, p. 76)

- **Spurious reasons** — Shallow, invalid justifications for integration: "reducing cyclicality," "assuring market access," "moving into the high value-added stage," "getting closer to our customers."
- **Reducing cyclicality/volatility in earnings** — The argument that combining stages smooths corporate earnings. Invalid because (a) contiguous stages' returns are positively correlated and driven by the same end-product demand, so portfolio risk barely falls, and (b) shareholders can diversify on their own; smoothing benefits managers, not shareholders.
- **Assuring supply or outlets** — The argument that captive supply/distribution eliminates foreclosure risk and unfair prices. Only valid when foreclosure or "unfair" prices are symptoms of actual VMF or structural market power.
- **Market foreclosure** — Being cut off from supply inputs or from access to customers because a trading partner refuses to deal.
- **Capturing more value** — The 1980s prescription to move into high value-added stages and get closer to final customers. Rejected: the correlation between a stage's profitability and its value added or customer proximity is weak and inconsistent.
- **Transfer price** — The internal price an integrated firm books between its own stages. Setting it away from the market price is self-delusion and produces suboptimal output and capacity decisions.

### Terms from pp. 77–83 (condensed coverage)

- **Long-term contract / joint venture / strategic alliance / technology license / asset ownership / franchising** — The six named quasi-integration mechanisms; all involve lower capital cost and greater flexibility than full ownership.
- **Asset ownership (as a mechanism)** — Retaining ownership of the critical specific assets in an adjacent stage while contracting out everything else about operating it. Often enough on its own to defeat opportunism tied to physical capital.
- **Specialized complementary assets** — Assets (skilled marketing, service teams, distribution) without which an innovation can't be commercialized. Their availability and ownership drive the innovator's license-vs.-integrate choice.
- **Market for corporate control** — The takeover/raider market, which pressures overintegrated firms to restructure.
- **Vertical disintegration** — The unwinding of integrated positions back toward trading; the dominant late-1980s/early-1990s trend.
- **Institutionalized piracy** — The authors' warning label for strategic alliances that turn out to be one-sided knowledge transfers.

## The Four Reasons to Integrate

Listed in the article's order; the authors state explicitly that these are **not equally good** and that reason 1 is the most important.

| #   | Reason                                                     | What has to be true                                                                              | Strength                                                                 |
| --- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1   | **The market fails** — trading is too risky and unreliable | Few buyers/sellers, high asset specificity, frequent transactions, contracts too costly to write | The most important reason; the authors' primary justification            |
| 2   | **Adjacent stage has more market power**                   | A neighboring stage earns abnormally high returns by exerting power over yours                   | Valid but often destroyed by acquisition premiums (see below)            |
| 3   | **Integration creates or exploits market power**           | Raises entry barriers, or enables price discrimination by blocking resale                        | Valid but expensive; barriers erode over time                            |
| 4   | **Industry life-cycle response**                           | Young industry needing market development, or declining industry where independents are exiting  | Weakest; the market-development rationale is "overrated" per the authors |

## Vertical Market Failure — The Mechanics

### The setup: why market structure matters

Ordinary supply and demand don't set price and volume deterministically when there are few players on each side. Instead, terms are set by the **balance of power** between buyer and seller — which is unpredictable and unstable. That instability is the raw material of trading risk.

### Figure 1 — Vertical Market Structures (sellers × buyers)

|                  | **One buyer**     | **Few buyers**    | **Many buyers**  |
| ---------------- | ----------------- | ----------------- | ---------------- |
| **One seller**   | High trading risk | High trading risk | Sellers dominate |
| **Few sellers**  | High trading risk | High trading risk | Sellers dominate |
| **Many sellers** | Buyers dominate   | Buyers dominate   | No one dominates |

- The high-trading-risk zone is the bilateral monopoly / bilateral oligopoly corner — this is where VMF lives.
- "Sellers dominate" and "buyers dominate" are _power asymmetry_ problems, not market failure problems; they map to reason 2 (defending against market power), not reason 1.

### Why bilateral oligopoly is uniquely hard

Three suppliers and three customers means each player must split the collective surplus with five others. Left alone, they compete it away and hand it to end customers. Horizontal monopoly at each stage would solve it but is illegal, so players **merge vertically**: six players become three, each now facing only two rivals for the surplus — a small enough group to behave rationally.

### Assets: why "many players" can be an illusion

A market can show dozens of buyers and sellers and still be a set of tightly bound bilateral oligopolies. High asset specificity, durability, and intensity raise switching costs so much that only a small slice of the apparent universe of counterparties is genuinely available to any given firm.

| Type of asset specificity     | Mechanism                                                            | Example from article                                                         |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Site specificity**          | Fixed assets co-located to minimize transport and inventory cost     | Coal mine and power station; bauxite mine and alumina refinery               |
| **Technical specificity**     | Equipment usable only by one counterparty, low alternative-use value | Refineries tailored to the chemical/physical properties of their own bauxite |
| **Human capital specificity** | Employee skills built around one specific relationship               | (Named as a category; no separate example given in this range)               |

**The pre/post-investment flip (key mechanism):** The bauxite market looks competitive _before_ investment — many miners and refiners bid for a greenfield opportunity. Once the mine and refinery are built, asset specificity locks that specific pair together economically. VMF is created by the investment itself, not by the initial market structure.

**Multiplier effects:** Bilateral oligopoly raises the _risk_ of disruption; capital intensity and high fixed costs raise the _cost_ of any disruption; durability extends the _time horizon_ over which both apply.

### Transaction frequency

High frequency promotes VMF _only in combination with_ bilateral oligopoly and high asset specificity — because frequency simply means haggling and exploitation opportunities recur more often. Low asset specificity neutralizes it (grocery shopping is frequent and requires no negotiation).

### Figure 2 — Transaction-Asset Matrix (the coordination-mechanism map)

|                       | **Low asset specificity / durability / intensity**                      | **High asset specificity / durability / intensity**                     |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Seldom transacted** | Detailed, standardized contracts (office lease, credit sale agreements) | Detailed, probably unique contract (major public construction projects) |
| **Often transacted**  | Standardized transactions (groceries)                                   | **Vertical integration** (bauxite, specialized auto components)         |

Read-off: integration is warranted in _one_ cell only. Everywhere else, some form of contract does the job.

### The three "soft" factors

- **Uncertainty** — makes contracts unwritable as circumstances change (machine shop: unpredictable timing, nature, and severity of breakdowns).
- **Bounded rationality** — even with good intentions, humans can't specify all future contingencies.
- **Opportunism** — parties will exploit gaps in contracts when it pays.

These three are universal but **vary in intensity by country, industry, and era** — which is the authors' explanation for why Japanese steel and auto manufacturers are less backward-integrated than Western counterparts, relying instead on a few contractors in stable, nonadversarial relationships. Suggested cause: opportunism is less prevalent in Japanese commercial culture.

## Defending Against Market Power (Reason 2)

**The logic:** If an adjacent stage exerts power over yours and earns abnormally high returns, that stage is attractive — and attracts entrants both from inside the chain and outside it.

**The two traps:**

| Trap                           | Mechanism                                                                                                  | Consequence                                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Paying away the surplus**    | Entry via acquisition means handing the seller the capitalized value of the surplus in the purchase price  | No value created; firms in weak stages routinely overpay for businesses in powerful stages                                                   |
| **Assuming insider advantage** | Managers believe being in the chain makes entry into the adjacent stage easier for them than for outsiders | False — key skills differ sharply between stages, so outsiders with analogous skills from _other_ industries are often the superior entrants |

**Additional caution:** even a successful entrant may dissipate the target stage's value — if one firm can scale the barriers, others can too.

**Decision test:** can the weak-stage player achieve integration _at a cost lower than the value of the benefits_? The authors' experience: usually not.

---

## Creating and Exploiting Market Power (Reason 3)

### Raising barriers to entry

When most competitors in an industry are vertically integrated, a nonintegrated entrant must enter every stage to compete. That raises required capital and minimum efficient scale — a structural entry barrier.

**Caveat:** this is an expensive ploy and not durable. If the economic surplus is large enough, inventive entrants eventually find a way in.

### Price discrimination

A supplier with market power selling a commodity to two segments with different price sensitivities wants to charge each differently — but the low-price segment will resell to the high-price one and collapse the scheme. Forward-integrating into the _low-price_ segment removes the reseller. Note the corollary: integration is targeted at segments with the **most price-sensitive demand**, and avoided where substitution threat is low.

---

## Responding to Industry Life Cycle (Reason 4)

### Young industry — forward integrate to develop the market

Framed as a special case of VMF (no downstream market exists yet to trade with).

**Three conditions for it to actually work:**

1. The downstream business has proprietary technology or a strong brand that blocks free-rider imitators.
2. You can capture the economic surplus for at least several years — otherwise there is no point developing the market.
3. The product has real advantages over current _and potential_ substitutes.

The authors' verdict: this rationale is **overrated** in practice.

### Declining industry — integrate to fill the gaps

As an industry declines, weaker independents exit, leaving the remaining suppliers or customers _more concentrated_ and therefore able to exploit the core players. Integration fills those gaps defensively.

## When Not to Integrate (p. 76 onward)

**Governing rule (stated in italics in the source):** do not vertically integrate unless absolutely necessary — the strategy is expensive, risky, and difficult to reverse.

Companies err toward **excessive** integration for two reasons:

1. Decisions are based on **spurious reasons**.
2. Managers **fail to consider quasi-integration** strategies that can beat full integration on both benefits and cost.

### Spurious reason 1 — Reducing cyclicality/volatility in earnings

A restatement of the old "internal portfolio diversification benefits shareholders" argument. Invalid twice over:

- Returns in contiguous stages are **positively correlated** — they share the same drivers, notably end-product demand — so combining them barely reduces portfolio risk (example: zinc mining and zinc smelting).
- Even if returns were negatively correlated, shareholders can diversify their own portfolios to shed unsystematic risk. Smoothing corporate earnings creates value **for managers, not shareholders**.

### Spurious reason 2 — Assuring supply or outlets

The claim: owning captive supply or distribution eliminates foreclosure risk and unfair prices and buffers short-run supply/demand imbalances in intermediate markets.

The authors' qualification: this is justified only where foreclosure or "unfair" prices are a **symptom of VMF or of structural market power** held by suppliers or customers — not merely because prices feel unfair. Where an efficient market exists, ownership is unnecessary: any participant can trade any volume at the market price, however "unfair" that price looks relative to costs. The real test is the **structure of both sides**: if buying and selling sides are both competitively structured, integration adds no value; only VMF or a permanent power imbalance justifies it.

### Spurious reason 3 — Capturing more value (p. 78)

The paired 1980s beliefs — move into the high value-added stage, and get closer to the customer — both push toward forward integration, and both usually destroy shareholder wealth. The correlation between a stage's profitability and its value added or customer proximity is **weak and inconsistent**.

**The correct prescription:** integrate into stages where the most **economic surplus** is available, regardless of customer proximity or absolute value added. Caveat: consistently high-surplus stages are by definition protected by entry barriers, and the entrant must scale those barriers for less than the surplus is worth. Those barriers usually include the _skills_ to run the new business — which adjacent-stage experience typically doesn't supply.

**Evidence (Figure 3, Australian cement/concrete chain):** surplus is not proportional to value added. Transport is the highest value-added stage and doesn't even earn an adequate return on capital; fly ash is the smallest value-added stage and earns high surplus. Surplus is not concentrated at the customer end — if anything it sits upstream.

## Beyond p. 76 — Rest of the Article (Condensed)

### Quasi-integration strategies (p. 78)

The second cause of overintegration: managers don't consider the menu. All of these carry lower capital cost and more flexibility than ownership, and often supply adequate protection from VMF and counterparty market power.

| Mechanism                              | What it does                                                                                                                                                     | Example                                                                                                                                                                         |
| -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Joint venture / strategic alliance** | Exchange goods, services, information, or expertise while keeping formal trade relations with others; preserves corporate identity and avoids antitrust exposure | A majority of upstream aluminum plants are now JVs — they move bauxite, alumina, know-how, and manage host-government relations                                                 |
| **Asset ownership**                    | Host owns the critical specific assets in the adjacent stage, contracts out the rest                                                                             | Vehicle and steam-turbine assemblers own the specialized tools, dies, jigs, patterns, and molds unique to key components; suppliers do the manufacturing                        |
| **Franchising**                        | Control distribution without the capital and management drain of ownership                                                                                       | Host avoids owning the (non-specific, non-durable) physical assets but keeps the intangible **brand** rights; the right to cancel enforces quality, service, cleanliness, value |
| **Licensing**                          | The default alternative where technology is being bought and sold                                                                                                | Markets for R&D and technology fail because property rights are hard to protect and innovations often need specialized complementary assets                                     |
| **Long-term contract**                 | Locks terms without ownership                                                                                                                                    | (Listed in the menu)                                                                                                                                                            |

### Figure 4 — Vertical strategy framework for innovators (p. 79, after Teece 1986)

Decision tree with three questions: **Are specialized complementary assets required? → Is copying easy? → Do existing owners of complementary assets have market power?**

Read-offs to remember:

- No complementary assets needed, copying hard → **license to all comers, price for the long run** (typical of petrochemicals, cosmetics).
- No complementary assets needed, copying easy → license to all comers, minimize exposure, **"cream" price** (take the early margin fast).
- Complementary assets needed + their owners have market power → **integrate if the barriers are scaleable**, otherwise acquire or JV with a going firm.
- Complementary assets needed, copying easy, owners have market power → tough to win; license or JV to limit exposure.

General direction: as copying gets easier and complementary assets get more critical, the answer shifts from licensing toward integration — which is exactly what EMI got wrong with the CAT scanner.

### Changing vertical strategies (pp. 79–80)

Change the strategy when market structure changes. The two structural factors most likely to move: **number of buyers and sellers**, and **the importance of specialized assets**.

**Crude oil, the worked case:**

|                            | 1966                                                                     | 1985                                                                            |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Seller concentration       | Top 4 = 59%, top 8 = 84%                                                 | Top 4 = 26%, top 8 = 42%                                                        |
| Buyer concentration        | As concentrated as sellers                                               | Much less concentrated                                                          |
| Asset specificity          | Refineries geared to specific crudes                                     | Refineries flexible across a wide range of crudes                               |
| Intensity / durability     | Capital intensive, 20+ years                                             | Unchanged                                                                       |
| Transaction frequency      | Very frequent                                                            | Unchanged                                                                       |
| Uncertainty                | Daily plant optimization changes                                         | Unchanged                                                                       |
| **Coordination mechanism** | Virtually no spot market; in-house transfers or ten-year fixed contracts | ~50% of transactions on the spot market; independents growing; **VI declining** |

Causes of the shift: OPEC nationalization replaced the "Seven Sisters" with multiple national suppliers, non-OPEC supply (e.g., Mexico) grew, and technology cut asset specificity and switching costs. Note which variables moved — buyers/sellers and specificity — and which didn't.

### Disintegration (pp. 80–81)

Three forces drove the late-1980s/early-1990s disintegration trend:

1. **Correcting past mistakes** — firms that integrated for spurious reasons unwind, even with no structural change.
2. **The market for corporate control** — raiders and restructuring pressure on overintegrated firms.
3. **Structural changes that make trading cheaper and safer** — deregulation adding players (telecoms, banking); growth of Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Mexico expanding the supplier universe; globalization pushing local manufacturing; the need for flexibility and focus when products are complex and life cycles short; IT/communications cutting bilateral trading costs; managers growing comfortable with quasi-integration (the US car industry cut VI and moved to fewer, more cooperative independent supply agreements).

**Counter-trend:** consolidation, as disaggregated conglomerate pieces (e.g., Beatrice Foods) are absorbed by firms building share in specific markets. The authors judge the disintegration forces to be winning.

**Their warning:** the fashion for downscaling will throw out some babies with the bathwater — firms will disintegrate activities that were genuinely critical because of VMF, form alliances that turn out to be institutionalized piracy, and discover that "cooperative" sole suppliers still remember how to flex their muscles once the competing suppliers are gone. Decisions must be **analytical, not fashionable or instinctual**.

### Figure 6 — Vertical restructuring framework (p. 81)

The article's summary decision tree, in order:

1. **Is the stage attractive to the current owner?** (attractive industry + skills match) → if yes, jump to the quasi-integration question.
2. If no → **Is the market subject to VMF?** → if yes, go to the quasi-integration question.
3. If no VMF → **Does owning the stage help the core business defend against, build, or exploit market power?** → if no, **do not participate, exit the stage**; if yes, go to the quasi-integration question.
4. **Would quasi-integration arrangements suffice?** → yes = **adopt the best quasi-integration arrangement**; no = **become or remain at least partially integrated**.

The whole tree is built so that full integration is the _last_ box you land in.

### Figure 7 — The five-step process (p. 82)

1. Lay out the stages of the industry chain or business system — disaggregate to natural business units.
2. Identify participants at each stage and classify transactions across stages (spot, contract, internal transfer); watch for hidden ownership links like JVs.
3. **Static analysis** — search for existing VMF: measure asset specificity in _both_ pre- and post-investment phases, measure transaction frequency, identify bilateral monopolies/oligopolies, and look specifically for information-market failure (R&D, new products, infant industries).
4. **Static → dynamic** — measure economic surplus by stage, find sustained market power asymmetries, test whether integration by weak participants would add more value than it costs and whether integration by powerful participants would strengthen them; then predict changes in power symmetry, buyer/seller numbers, asset specificity, and transaction frequency.
5. Choose the vertical strategy and the supporting organization; quantify against the Table 1 criteria; consider quasi-integration; decide using normal criteria (NPV, risk, executability).

**Process cautions:** quantify switching and transaction costs; ordinary supply-and-demand analysis can't predict small-numbers behavior, so use pay-off matrices and competitive games; get senior executives hands-on so they believe the competitor-behavior assumptions; do a fast first pass then a painstaking second; expect heavy resistance, since vertical integration is one of the last strategy areas where gut feel and tradition still rule.

## Quick Cross-Framework Hooks

- **Five Forces:** reason 3 is literally a Porter entry-barrier play (capital requirements + scale + access to distribution channels). The aluminum and auto-dealership cases are entry-barrier evidence; the ready-mix vs. quarry contrast is a pure "industry structure determines profitability" illustration across two adjacent industries.
- **RBV/VRIO:** the authors' claim that key skills differ substantially across chain stages is a resource-heterogeneity argument — an incumbent's resources are usually _not_ valuable in the adjacent stage, which is why outsiders often make better entrants.
- **Transaction cost economics:** the entire VMF section is applied Williamson (asset specificity, bounded rationality, opportunism), with Simon's bounded rationality cited upstream of it.

### 1. Amazon's Moonshot Plan to Rival Nvidia in AI Chips

**Source:** Matt Day, Ian King and Dina Bass, Bloomberg, Nov. 24, 2024.

**What happened**

- Amazon is building custom AI silicon to loosen Nvidia's hold on a $100B+ market — the goal not raw performance but a simple, reliable system turning AWS data centers into large AI machines, and reducing dependence on incumbents (Intel first, now Nvidia). James Hamilton's original logic: buying millions of units justifies tailoring hardware and stripping unneeded features.
- Hamilton proposed in-house design to Bezos in 2013 on the Arm bet; Amazon commissioned then **acquired Annapurna Labs for ~$350M**. Product sequence: **Nitro** → **Graviton** (Arm CPU, cheaper than Intel, volume making Amazon a top-ten TSMC customer) → **Inferentia** → **Trainium1/Inferentia2** → **Trainium2**, framed as the make-or-break third generation (few products survive the "three-generation rule").
- Trainium2: **4× performance, 3× memory**, most cabling replaced by PCBs, **chips per box cut 8→2** so maintenance downs fewer units. Target cadence ~18 months, aided by in-house testing/lab work and pre-installed liquid-cooling plumbing.
- The binding constraint is **software, not hardware**: Nvidia's comprehensive tool suite lets customers run projects with little customization, while Amazon's Neuron SDK is immature — even a clean port can take hundreds of engineer-hours.
- Demand scaffolding: Amazon pushes adoption when customers renew AWS deals. Databricks agreed to Trainium citing a claimed **30% better price-performance**; **Anthropic agreed to use Trainium after ~$8B total from Amazon** (two $4B tranches), while still using Nvidia/Google.
- Rivals do the same: Google (~decade of AI-chip work, sold to Anthropic/Cohere/Midjourney) and Microsoft (Maia, Cobalt). All three keep praising Nvidia and competing for its chips.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Backward VI to reduce supplier power** | The cleanest stated motive in either set: a buyer integrating upstream because the supplier is scarce, expensive and rationing — Nvidia had just told investors demand would outstrip supply for several quarters |
| **The Grant semiconductor finding, inverted** | Grant: integrated design+fabrication producers (Intel, ST Micro) outperform when the circuit is complex enough to need designer–fabricator collaboration. Amazon integrates **design**, outsources **fabrication** to TSMC — fabless — because leading-edge fabs' MES is far beyond even Amazon's volume |
| **Scale justifying integration** | Hamilton's custom-server argument is the FedEx test passed: buying millions of units clears the threshold where tailoring beats purchasing |
| **Architectural vs. component capabilities** | The software gap is the whole story: Amazon can build the component (silicon) but value depends on the toolchain that makes it usable — the architectural layer where Nvidia leads |
| **Buy, then build** | Annapurna ($350M) supplied the design capability; every later chip is internal. Build-Borrow-or-Buy predicts *buy* — chip design teams have low tradability |
| **Equity alliance as demand guarantee** | The Anthropic investments buy a committed anchor customer to stress and improve the chips — equity flowing *downstream* to secure adoption, not upstream to secure supply |
| **Mutual encroachment (pairs with Part 1 Nvidia)** | The same conflict from the other side: Nvidia moves forward into cloud partly because its biggest customers move backward into chips. Neither move makes sense in isolation |
| **Competitive parity, not advantage** | The honest ceiling: no Nvidia displacement expected soon; success is absorbing internal workloads plus occasional large-customer projects — a cost and supply-security play, not sustained advantage |

**Trap to watch:** "Amazon is vertically integrating into chips" is half true — it integrates design, not manufacture, and that distinction is the whole analysis: make-or-buy differs stage by stage within one apparent activity. Treating "chips" as a single stage misses this.

### 2. Apple & Globalstar — Escalation Along the Continuum

**Sources:** Dan Gallagher, *The Wall Street Journal*, Sept. 9, 2022; Mark Gurman, Bloomberg, Dec. 10, 2024.

**What happened**

*Stage one (Sept 2022):* iPhone 14 could connect directly to satellites via Emergency SOS. Under the partnership, **Apple covers 95% of Globalstar's capex** (including new satellites) and Globalstar **commits 85% of current and future capacity** to the service — an analyst's "underutilized asset made productive in one move." Globalstar projected revenue of $185–230M, then $250–310M for 2026 (~44% above its three-year average), calling the deal transformational. Shares fell 19% the next day (partly on a Musk post touting Starlink talks; the service is emergency texting only) but stood 17% above pre-rumor levels. Competitors: SpaceX/T-Mobile, Iridium.

*Stage two (Dec 2024):* Apple invested **~$1.5B for a ~20% stake** in November, and planned to extend satellite texting to the 2025 Apple Watch Ultra ($799). The feature had widened in stages: emergency (2022) → roadside assistance (2023) → general iMessage texting.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Escalation along the make-or-buy continuum** | The best illustration in either set of a relationship *moving*: supply contract → funded capex plus capacity commitment → minority equity. Each step raises commitment as the specific investment grows |
| **Transaction-specific investment, both directions** | Globalstar's new satellites are built for Apple's service; 85% of current *and future* capacity is committed. Apple's 95% capex funding is equally specific — neither asset has value without the other. Grant's steel-and-strip structure, in orbit |
| **Bilateral monopoly** | One buyer, one seller, purpose-built assets, no market price. The governance response follows theory: not spot, not full acquisition, but progressively deeper commitment |
| **Why not acquire outright?** | Apple gets effective control of capacity (85%) and economics without owning a regulated satellite operator — a strategically dissimilar business. Grant's "managing strategically different businesses" cost is why it stops at 20% |
| **Hold-up risk, equity as the answer** | Having funded 95% of the network, Apple faces Globalstar's leverage at renewal — and Apple has leverage over a firm that committed 85% of capacity to it. Equity converts the standoff into shared upside — the Commonwealth Bank/EDS and oilfield-equity logic |
| **Underutilized → productive asset** | Value comes from higher utilization of existing capacity, not new capability — a supplier-side economies-of-scale story that makes the 85% commitment tolerable to Globalstar |
| **Alternatives as bargaining power** | Starlink/T-Mobile and Iridium keep Apple from being fully captive; the 19% drop on a Musk post is the market pricing substitution risk in real time |
| **Substitute threat created downstream** | Satellite texting on the Watch reduces the reason to carry a standalone Garmin inReach — an upstream vertical relationship creating a substitution threat in a neighboring market |

**Trap to watch:** the 20% stake is not control and not an acquisition. Don't read the 2022 stock drop as the deal being bad for Globalstar — the same article notes record projected revenue and a price well above pre-rumor levels. Price movement on a competitor's announcement measures *substitution risk*, not deal quality.

### 3. Apple's Component Sourcing Strategy

**Source:** Mark Gurman, Bloomberg, Dec. 10, 2024 (same article as above).

**What happened**

- Some Apple Watch models are moving from Intel to **MediaTek** cellular modems — the first major MediaTek component, after 5+ years of evaluation — bringing 5G RedCap (a low-tier 5G for wearables). Separately, Apple is developing its **own iPhone modem** (away from Qualcomm) but has no plans to use it in the Watch.
- Product context: Wearables/Home/Accessories revenue declined for two years (little reason to upgrade); satellite texting is meant to justify the **$799 Ultra over the $399 Series 10 or $249 SE**.
- Apple is also building a blood-pressure feature (flagging possible hypertension rather than giving readings) and continues noninvasive glucose work — a push into a regulated adjacent category.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Supplier switching as an alternative to integration** | Intel → MediaTek reduces dependence without building anything — the cheapest response to supplier power is a credible second source, not ownership |
| **Selective backward integration** | The in-house iPhone modem is genuine backward integration, deliberately *not* applied to the Watch — make-or-buy decided per product line, not per firm |
| **Multi-sourcing vs. single-supplier** | Grant's "recent trends" show a shift toward single-supplier collaboration; Apple runs the opposite on modems (qualify alternatives, then integrate) — the trend is a tendency, not a rule |
| **Differentiation as the demand-side motive** | The satellite feature restores an upgrade reason after two years of declining wearables revenue — a vertical decision (Globalstar) driven by a business-strategy problem (price laddering across SE/Series/Ultra) |
| **Product scope expansion** | Health monitoring pushes into a regulated category; designing blood pressure to flag a state rather than report numbers keeps it on the safe side of a regulatory boundary |

**Trap to watch:** MediaTek replacing Intel is *not* vertical integration — nothing crossed Apple's boundary; only the in-house modem does. Conflating supplier substitution with integration is the "two distinct concepts merged" error.

### 4. CoreWeave / Core Scientific

**Sources:** Lauren Thomas and Ben Dummett, *The Wall Street Journal*, June 26, 2025; Sam Reynolds, CoinDesk, June 27, 2025.

**What happened**

- CoreWeave is in advanced talks to acquire Core Scientific — a top North American owner of digital infrastructure for bitcoin mining/hosting (~$3.7B market value; shares +33% on the report) — after a failed attempt a year earlier.
- CoreWeave provides AI cloud infrastructure (renting Nvidia GPUs to Meta, IBM), went public in March at ~$75B, with **Microsoft = 62% of its 2024 revenue**.
- Key prior fact: in 2024 the two signed **12-year contracts** for Core Scientific to deliver hundreds of megawatts to host CoreWeave — Cantor describes a **12-year, $3.5B lease for 200MW**. CoreWeave's rejected 2024 bid was **$5.75/share**; the stock later traded at more than double. Cantor argued shares could exceed **$30** — $24 for the lease stream (15× REIT multiple) plus $11.70 for replacement value of 570MW of power infrastructure.
- Sector context: bitcoin miners are repositioning as AI infrastructure providers amid a power shortage — AI gives stable long-term cash flows while mining faces four-year halving drops. Not all pivots worked: Canaan shuttered its AI chip unit (shares −75% in six months).

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Long-term contract converting into ownership** | The textbook sequence in ~a year: partnership → 12-year lease with hundreds of MW committed → rejected acquisition → acquisition. Long-term contracts can't anticipate all circumstances over their life; a 12-year commitment in a volatile market is where that bites |
| **Transaction-specific investment** | Power infrastructure configured for a specific tenant, site, and 12-year term — both locked in (Core Scientific to the tenant, CoreWeave to the capacity) |
| **The bargaining problem, priced** | The $5.75 vs. $30+ per-share gap for the same assets a year apart is "no equilibrium price, only relative bargaining power" in a bilateral situation — refusal in 2024, return later, pure bargaining sequence |
| **Table 13.1 — uncertainty over the period** | Extreme cost/technology/demand uncertainty over 12 years raises the advantage of integration; the case follows the criterion almost mechanically |
| **Customer concentration as the other exposure** | 62% of CoreWeave's 2024 revenue from Microsoft — securing supply upstream while acutely dependent downstream, an asymmetry worth naming |
| **Asset redeployment and economies of scope** | Mining and AI are different workloads, but power interconnects, cooling and land are common — an economies-of-scope story about the *infrastructure*, not the *computation*, which is why Canaan's chip pivot failed while Core Scientific's succeeded |
| **Position in the wider chain** | CoreWeave rents Nvidia GPUs, joins Nvidia's Lepton marketplace (Part 1), and now buys its own power infrastructure — integrating backward while being encroached upon from above |

**Trap to watch:** the Cantor note stacks two valuation arguments — capitalized contract cash flows *and* replacement value of the assets. These are alternative methods, not additive, and it's a sell-side estimate, not a market price. Treating "$30+" as fact rather than forecast is the error.

### 5. UPS Healthcare and Labport

**Source:** Cailley LaPara, Bloomberg, Jan. 6, 2025.

**What happened**

- UPS is building a health-care logistics business to offset stagnating parcel revenue: **UPS Healthcare was $10B at end-2023 (~1/10 of revenue), targeting a double by 2026** via acquisitions and new ventures. Margins are the point — high-teens operating margin vs. ~10% for UPS overall (an analyst: potentially $300 on a shipment costing the same to deliver as a box of socks).
- **Labport** is the newest step: ~100,000 sq ft of custom lab space by the Louisville air hub (end-2024), all pre-leased for 10+ years, plus an attached 182,000 sq ft warehouse and a command center. CEO Carol Tomé frames health care as a specialized component the core network (22M packages/day) isn't built for. History: campus (mid-2000s) → global distribution centers → formal unit in 2019 (well-timed for the pandemic cold-chain surge). The unit even wears navy blue rather than UPS brown.
- Anchor customer **HealthTrackRx** consolidated its labs to Louisville in 2022 to sit beside UPS, which moves ~90% of its samples — processed overnight, cutting test prices to ~1/4 pre-pandemic. Labport will double its footprint (~200 workers); UPS is considering assembling testing kits (currently split among vendors). The 2026 target can't be hit organically, requiring more acquisitions (mostly European so far).
- Competition: FedEx (>$500M FY24 health-care revenue), DHL ($200M US investment); market projected to ~$200B by 2032 (ageing populations, Ozempic/Zepbound). A UPS exec cautions it won't be a margin windfall but is stable, sticky, and cross-sells.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Related-constrained diversification** | Health-care logistics shares the network, hubs, aircraft and tracking with parcel delivery but adds distinct capabilities on top — Rumelt's related diversification (the inverted-U peak), not conglomerate expansion |
| **Economies of scope** | The Louisville hub is already built for parcels; the health-care business monetizes its proximity a second time, and Labport literally sells access to that scope advantage as real estate |
| **Co-location without common ownership — Grant's exact point** | The sharpest instance in either set. HealthTrackRx moved its labs adjacent to UPS's hub for the technical economies of proximity — which Grant says justify **co-location, not common ownership**, and here the parties do exactly that: a lease, not a merger. Contrast steel/strip, where co-location *did* lead to integration (differing on asset specificity and number of alternative counterparties) |
| **Forward integration into the customer's operations** | Kit assembly (now done by separate vendors) is where UPS would cross from logistics into its customer's process; the 10-year leases and command center are intermediate degrees |
| **Build-Borrow-or-Buy** | The 2026 target can't be reached organically; low relevance in specialized cold chain plus low tradability of operating capability points to **buy** — matching the recent European acquisitions |
| **Strategically different businesses** | Managing dissimilar businesses shows up as a separate unit, leadership, even uniform color — brand separation *mitigating* the administrative cost of internal dissimilarity |
| **Switching costs and stickiness** | A customer that relocated its labs adjacent to your hub and signed a 10-year lease has enormous switching costs — lock-in manufactured through geography, not contract |
| **Business-level positioning** | Also a differentiation move away from commodity ground shipping (consumers trading down, B2B volume falling) — corporate scope change answering a business-strategy problem |

**Trap to watch:** tempting to call this vertical integration into health care — it isn't. UPS isn't entering diagnostics or pharma; it's diversifying into an adjacent *service market* on a shared asset base. Only the (still hypothetical) kit-assembly idea is genuinely vertical.

### 6. American Giant's $12.98 Walmart T-Shirt

**Source:** Suzanne Kapner, *The Wall Street Journal*, Dec. 30, 2024.

**What happened**

- Walmart sells US-made cotton T-shirts at **$12.98**, produced by American Giant, in a market where **<4% of US apparel (2023) was made domestically**. CEO Bayard Winthrop credits not tariffs but **Walmart's scale plus guaranteed orders** — without a commitment to buy a set quantity, suppliers wouldn't invest in the automation that cut costs. Walmart confirmed **noncancelable purchase orders**.
- American Giant's own scope: buys US-grown/spun/dyed yarn (mainly Southeastern suppliers); **owns** a N.C. cutting/sewing facility; **part-owns** an LA sewing facility opened specifically for the Walmart order (~$1M machinery, 75 hires). Cost engineering: tubular no-side-seam knit, shorter-fiber yarn, machine-affixed flag patches, automated printing.
- Pricing was set from **Walmart demand forecasts by price point**. Result: shirts in 1,700 stores by July 4, competing against plain 100% cotton shirts at half the price — Walmart bars "American Made"/flag on non-US goods. Sweatshirts at **$38.98 vs. a $148 direct hoodie**; American Giant's own tees sell for $40–60.
- Constraint (Winthrop): almost anything can be made domestically unless it's labor-intensive — apparel resists automation because fabric is still mostly sewn by hand. Countervailing: 23 US textile plants closed in 18 months; stated preference for American-made often fails to convert.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **The hold-up problem solved by contract design** | The central lesson: suppliers won't make transaction-specific investments in automation without protection, and the noncancelable purchase order is the security device — Grant's rationale for long-term contracts, stated almost verbatim by a practitioner |
| **Direct contrast with Walmart / Dean Foods (Part 1)** | Same buyer, opposite outcome. With Dean, Walmart squeezed, then integrated and destroyed the supplier; with American Giant it *underwrote* supplier investment. The difference is what Walmart wanted: milk was a commodity it could make itself; US-made apparel is a capability it won't build, so it must keep the supplier viable. **Buyer power can expropriate or guarantee — the choice depends on whether the buyer intends to integrate** |
| **Information sharing as a vendor-partnership feature** | Walmart handed over demand forecasts by price point — the extensive knowledge sharing Grant identifies in Japanese-style vendor partnerships, reducing information asymmetry rather than exploiting it |
| **Taper integration on the supplier's side** | American Giant owns one sewing facility, part-owns a second, and contracts the rest — partial ownership plus market purchasing |
| **Joint venture for a specific relationship** | The LA facility was opened specifically for this order, with shared ownership — a dedicated transaction-specific asset given an equity structure to protect it |
| **Location economies and their limits** | Automation substitutes for the labor-cost gap only where the process is automatable; apparel's residual manual sewing sets the boundary — location economies operate activity-by-activity, not industry-by-industry |
| **Differentiation as the pricing mechanism** | The shirts cost ~double the plain competitors; the premium rests on the flag and "American Made" label, which Walmart polices by barring their use on imports — differentiation manufactured through *enforced certification*, not product attributes |

**Trap to watch:** tariffs are the obvious explanation and the article explicitly rejects it — crediting trade policy uses a true macro fact to explain the wrong mechanism. The participant names contractual commitment, a governance variable, not a policy one.

### 7. Starbucks Pares Its Hedging Programme

**Source:** Gregory Meyer, *Financial Times*, Nov. 24, 2024.

**What happened**

- Starbucks held **<$200M** in fixed-price green-coffee contracts at fiscal year end, down from **$1B in 2019** — a sharp cut in price cover during a rally where futures passed **$3/lb (13-year high, +70% in 12 months)** on Brazil supply deficits.
- Scale: buys **~3% of the world's coffee** for 40,000 cafés; said in 2021 it bought 800M lbs/yr (~$2.4B). Purchasing runs from Lausanne via a subsidiary, **Starbucks Coffee Trading Company**, with **56 "tier one" suppliers** from trading houses (Louis Dreyfus, Olam) to farmer co-ops.
- Contract types: fixed-price and **price-to-be-fixed** (quantity, delivery and a futures premium set, final price later — most purchases), plus derivatives. Year-end positions: $1.1B purchase obligations; price-to-be-fixed $929M; derivatives $154M (lowest since 2020); inventory ~$920M (lowest since 2021).
- Starbucks says its approach is unchanged, coverage slightly below its typical **9–18 month range**, and inventory cushions volatility. Analysts note it is substantially less hedged; a commodities analyst argues you want to be hedged in a rising market.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Governance by contract where the market is thick** | Coffee is a commodity with many suppliers, transparent prices and low switching costs — Grant's conditions for market contracting. Starbucks buys 3% of world output and still owns no farms, like flour millers and wheat. **Volume alone doesn't justify backward integration** |
| **Allocation of risk — Grant's first design variable** | The whole article is about who bears price risk and for how long: fixed-price moves risk to the supplier; price-to-be-fixed keeps quantity certainty while leaving price open; derivatives buy protection separately — three instruments, three risk splits, one physical transaction |
| **Spot vs. long-term contracts** | Price-to-be-fixed is a hybrid: long-term on quantity/delivery, spot-like on price — "long-term contract" isn't one thing; different terms lock on different horizons |
| **Backward integration into the *function*, not the *stage*** | Starbucks Coffee Trading Company is a wholly owned procurement/trading subsidiary — internalizing the trading capability while still buying the physical commodity from others |
| **Supplier portfolio structure** | 56 tier-one suppliers from trading houses to co-ops is deliberate multi-sourcing — against Grant's single-supplier trend, breadth is the risk control for a volatile commodity |
| **Uncertainty and the limits of contracting** | Table 13.1: greater cost/demand uncertainty raises the advantage of integration — but here integration still isn't the answer, because it's *price* uncertainty in a liquid market, which financial instruments handle better than ownership. Not all uncertainty pushes the same way |
| **Inventory as an alternative to hedging** | Physical stocks, derivatives and fixed-price contracts are substitute risk-management technologies with different cost structures — and inventory is also at a multi-year low, weakening the defense |

**Trap to watch:** the claim that the approach is unchanged sits against three simultaneous multi-year lows — fixed-price contracts, derivatives, and inventory. Each instrument alone might be defensible, but the article's own numbers show *all* the cushions thinning at once — a "conclusion contradicted by its own figures" setup.

### 8. Guardian Bikes and Made-in-China Parts

**Source:** Natasha Khan, *The Wall Street Journal*, Jan. 6, 2025.

**What happened**

- Brian Riley moved Guardian Bikes (children's bikes) from China to Seymour, Indiana, in **2022 for quality control, not cost** — he wanted a bike resistant to head-over-heels braking accidents, and mass-retail bikes ship unassembled with no oversight of final assembly. Nearly all parts still come from China (a typical bike has 30–40 parts).
- Current position: Chinese components are **~90% of parts cost, targeting ~20%**. Guardian is starting in-house frame production (American steel) and sourcing grips/reflectors domestically — US-made parts could then reach ~60%. It has asked its Brazilian rim supplier to build in Indiana, and is also considering making rims itself. Labor-intensive parts (hubs, cranks) are harder to source domestically.
- Positioning: direct-to-consumer at **$149–399**. Location logic: two-day US shipping, near steel mills, skilled workers freed by a local ironing-board plant's closure. Learning curve: 100 bikes/day rising to 2,700/day at the 250-person factory; labor costs rose but were partly offset by cheaper freight on loose vs. assembled bikes — Riley accepted losses to get through.
- Trade policy: tariffs on assembled Chinese children's bikes rose **11%→36%**, but assembled bikes were exempted (so Guardian paid duties on imported *parts* while rivals importing finished bikes did not); the **exemption was lifted in 2024**. Others chose differently: Prevelo → Taiwan/Thailand/Cambodia (its CEO: reshoring needs an industry-wide movement); Giant (4M bikes/yr) opened a 100,000-capacity Vietnam plant.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Vertical integration for quality assurance** | The stated motive is monitoring, not cost. Where quality is hard to specify/verify in a contract and final assembly happens outside anyone's oversight, internalizing is the response — the *incomplete-contracting / monitoring-cost* branch of TCE, distinct from the specific-investment branch |
| **The scope of "reshoring" is activity-specific** | Assembly moved; components didn't. The 90% → 20% target measures how much of the *chain*, not the *product*, relocated — make-or-buy and where-to-make decided stage by stage |
| **Absence of a supporting industry cluster** | Porter's Diamond in the negative: Guardian has factor conditions (steel mills, freed workers) and demand, but the *related/supporting industries* corner is empty — hubs and cranks have no domestic base. Prevelo's "reshoring needs an industry-wide movement" is the cluster argument as a constraint |
| **Co-location invitation, not acquisition** | Asking the Brazilian rim supplier to build in Indiana is HealthTrackRx→Louisville again: proximity economies without common ownership. Guardian is also weighing making rims itself — make-or-buy live on a single component |
| **Minimum efficient scale** | Prevelo can't justify specialized factories at his volume; Giant (4M bikes/yr) can open a dedicated Vietnam plant — same industry, opposite answers on scale vs. MES, the FedEx test twice |
| **Location economies and CAGE** | Three responses to one tariff shock: US (Guardian), Taiwan/Thailand/Cambodia (Prevelo), Vietnam (Giant) — all optimize on administrative distance; only Guardian prioritizes geographic proximity to the customer |
| **Learning curve as a real cost of integration** | 100 → 2,700 bikes/day quantifies the capability gap absorbed when internalizing a never-performed activity — Grant's "developing distinctive capabilities" outsourcing argument, paid up front and deliberately |
| **Taxes and regulations as a cost of transacting** | The assembled-bike tariff exemption penalized the domestic manufacturer vs. importers — a policy accidentally taxing the more integrated structure; Table 13.1's tax/regulation row in the unexpected direction |

**Trap to watch:** treating tariffs as the reason Guardian reshored inverts the timeline — the factory opened in 2022 for quality; tariffs are something Riley *hopes* will accelerate a shift already underway. Note also the freight offset: shipping loose components is cheaper than assembled bikes, so it's not a simple wage differential.

## Cross-Article Synthesis

### A. The dominant pattern: contracts escalating into ownership

Four cases in this set show a vertical relationship *moving* along the continuum over time rather than being chosen once. This is the single most quiz-able pattern in the set.

| Case | Starting form | Intermediate | Current / attempted | Trigger for escalation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Apple → Globalstar | Supply agreement | 95% capex funding + 85% capacity commitment | ~$1.5B for ~20% equity | Specific investment on both sides; no market price |
| CoreWeave → Core Scientific | Hosting partnership | 12-year, $3.5B lease for 200MW | Acquisition talks | Long-horizon uncertainty; rising asset value |
| Amazon → chip supply | Buying Intel/Nvidia | Custom servers and switches | Annapurna acquisition; in-house Trainium | Supplier scarcity, rationing, pricing power |
| American Giant ← Walmart | Spot purchasing | Noncancelable purchase orders | Part-owned dedicated LA facility | Supplier needed protection to invest |

**Generalizable statement:** the trigger is almost always the appearance of transaction-specific investment. Once one party must build something that only has value in this relationship, spot contracting becomes untenable and the relationship climbs the ladder — first toward longer and more binding contracts, then toward equity, then toward ownership. Whether it stops at equity or goes to ownership depends on how strategically dissimilar the two businesses are (Apple stops at 20% because satellite operation is a different business; CoreWeave goes to acquisition because data-center power is its own input).

### B. Co-location without common ownership — Grant's test, three ways

Grant argues technical economies justify physical proximity but not shared ownership; the ownership question is answered separately by transaction costs.

| Case | Co-location happened | Ownership followed | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Steel / steel strip (textbook) | Yes | **Yes** | Assets purpose-built for one counterparty; bilateral monopoly |
| HealthTrackRx / UPS | Yes — labs relocated to the hub | **No** — a 10-year lease | Many potential tenants and many logistics customers; assets are general-purpose labs |
| Guardian / Brazilian rim supplier | Requested | **Undecided** — Guardian is also considering making rims itself | Live make-or-buy decision; specificity of rims to Guardian is the open variable |

This trio is the cleanest available way to demonstrate that co-location and integration are separate decisions — and the second and third rows are the ones a flawed analysis would collapse into the first.

### C. Buyer power used two ways — the Walmart pair

Reading the Part 1 Dean Foods case against the Part 2 American Giant case gives a matched pair with one variable changed.

| | Walmart / Dean Foods | Walmart / American Giant |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer power | Very high | Very high |
| Supplier's needed investment | Plant efficiency, bottle weight reduction | Automation, machinery, a dedicated facility |
| Contract form | Ongoing supply, no volume guarantee | Noncancelable purchase orders |
| Information flow | Price pressure only | Demand forecasts by price point shared |
| Walmart's intent | To eventually integrate | To not integrate |
| Outcome | Supplier bankrupted; Walmart built the plant | Supplier invested; domestic capacity created |

**The analytical point:** buyer power is a structural fact; whether it expropriates or underwrites is a *strategic choice* determined by whether the buyer wants the capability inside its own boundary. This is the most defensible cross-set generalization available.

### D. Backward integration to reduce supplier power — how far up the chain?

| Case | Stage integrated | Stage left outsourced | Reason for stopping |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amazon | Chip design (Annapurna, Trainium) | Fabrication (TSMC) | Fab MES far exceeds even Amazon's internal volume |
| Apple | iPhone modem (in development) | Watch modem (MediaTek); fabrication | Volume and strategic weight don't justify it per product line |
| CoreWeave | Data center power infrastructure | GPUs (Nvidia) | Cannot integrate into a supplier with that market position |
| Starbucks | Trading and procurement (Lausanne subsidiary) | Growing, and physical commodity supply | Thick market, many suppliers, low switching costs |
| Guardian | Assembly, frames | Hubs, cranks, most components | No domestic supplier base; labor-intensive |

**Pattern:** every one of these firms integrated the stage where *its own volume clears minimum efficient scale* and stopped at the stage where it doesn't. MES, not strategic importance, is the recurring binding constraint.

### F. Where the frameworks strain

| Observation | Framework limitation exposed |
|---|---|
| Starbucks faces extreme uncertainty yet does not integrate | Table 13.1 treats "uncertainty" as one variable. **Price** uncertainty in a liquid market is better managed by financial instruments than ownership; **technological and demand** uncertainty is what actually pushes toward integration |
| Amazon integrates design but not fabrication | Make-or-buy must be applied per stage, not per activity-label. "Chips" is not one decision |
| UPS separates its health-care unit's brand and uniform | The administrative cost of strategic dissimilarity is real but *manageable* — Grant treats it mainly as a reason not to integrate rather than as something firms actively mitigate |
| Guardian accepted losses and a steep learning curve deliberately | Transaction cost analysis is static; it has no account of paying a known capability cost now to change your options later |

## New / Reinforced Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Source case |
|---|---|---|
| **Fabless** | Designing chips in-house while outsourcing manufacture to a foundry. Splits a single apparent activity into an integrated stage and an outsourced one | Amazon |
| **Minimum efficient scale, applied per stage** | The recurring reason firms integrate one step and stop at the next; the same firm can clear MES for design and fail it for fabrication | Amazon, Guardian, Prevelo |
| **Complexity gap** | The software and tooling layer that determines whether a technically capable component is actually usable by customers; where architectural capability lives | Amazon |
| **Capacity commitment** | Contractually allocating a stated share of a supplier's total present and future capacity to one buyer — a specific investment expressed as a share of output rather than an asset | Apple / Globalstar |
| **Underutilized-to-productive asset conversion** | Value created by raising utilization of existing capacity rather than by new capability; the supplier-side rationale for accepting deep dependence | Apple / Globalstar |
| **Dual sourcing / supplier substitution** | Qualifying a second supplier to reduce dependence *without* integrating. Frequently confused with vertical integration | Apple / MediaTek |
| **Customer concentration** | The share of revenue from a single buyer; the downstream mirror of supplier dependence | CoreWeave (62% Microsoft) |
| **Asset redeployment** | Repurposing infrastructure built for one use toward a higher-value use; works when the shared asset is general-purpose (power, cooling, land) and fails when it is specialized (mining ASICs) | Core Scientific vs. Canaan |
| **Replacement value** | Valuing a company by the cost of rebuilding its physical assets; an alternative to cash-flow valuation, not an addition to it | Core Scientific |
| **Cold chain** | Temperature-controlled logistics for perishable or biologic products; the specialized capability layered onto a general parcel network | UPS |
| **Noncancelable purchase order** | A binding volume commitment from a buyer that gives a supplier the security to make transaction-specific investments — the practical form of Grant's long-term contract | American Giant |
| **Price-to-be-fixed contract** | An agreement setting quantity, delivery month and premium to a futures benchmark, leaving final price to be agreed later; long-term on volume, spot-like on price | Starbucks |
| **Priced coverage** | How many months of future requirements a buyer has locked in at known prices; Starbucks' typical range is 9–18 months | Starbucks |
| **Reshoring** | Relocating production back to the home country. Distinct from insourcing — it changes *where*, not *who* | Guardian, American Giant |
| **Learning curve cost of integration** | The temporary productivity penalty absorbed when a firm internalizes an activity it has not performed before | Guardian (100 → 2,700 bikes/day) |
