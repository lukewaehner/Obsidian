## §0. Foundations (assumed by every other section)

> [!note]- Where else this lives
> **Deep dive:** [[Intro to Strategy]] · [[Strategy and Action]]


### Core Idea

- **Strategy** = goal-directed actions to gain and sustain superior performance relative to competitors. It is *not* a grandiose statement, *not* operational effectiveness, *not* a functional "pricing/brand strategy."
- Strategy = a **unique and valuable position** + **trade-offs** (choosing what *not* to do) + **fit** among activities.
- **Competitive advantage** = superior performance vs. rivals/industry average at a point in time. **Sustainable competitive advantage (SCA)** = that performance held over time *because rivals cannot duplicate the value, or find it too costly to try*.
- **AFI framework**: **A**nalyze (external + internal) -> **F**ormulate (business + corporate strategy) -> **I**mplement (structure, culture, controls, incentives).

### Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Path to good strategy (3 elements)** | (1) Diagnosis of the competitive challenge, (2) a guiding policy, (3) a set of coherent actions. |
| **Corporate strategy** | *Where* do we compete - which industries/markets are attractive? (scope) |
| **Business strategy** | *How* do we compete within a chosen market? (cost leadership vs. differentiation) |
| **Intended strategy** | Outcome of a rational, top-down plan. |
| **Emergent strategy** | Unplanned initiative that bubbles up from the bottom. |
| **Realized strategy** | Intended + emergent, combined. |
| **Autonomous actions** | Employee initiatives responding to unexpected situations (Starbucks Frappuccino). |
| **Serendipity** | Random events/coincidences that shape initiatives (potato chip, Saratoga Springs). |
| **Resource-allocation process (RAP)** | How policy-driven resource allocation shapes realized strategy (Intel: DRAM -> microprocessors). |
| **Black swan event** | Low-probability, extreme-impact, unpredictable (COVID, 2008, Enron, IT breach). |
| **Economic value created** | **V − C**: buyer's willingness to pay minus the firm's total cost. |
| **Consumer surplus** | **V − P**. **Producer surplus (profit)** = **P − C**. |
| **Three performance frameworks** | Accounting profitability (ROIC/ROE/ROA/ROR) · shareholder value creation (TRS, market cap) · economic value creation (V − C). |
| **Two integrative frameworks** | Balanced scorecard · triple bottom line (profits/people/planet). |

**Three approaches to organizational strategy:** strategic planning (intended, top-down, "militaristic" - Apple) · scenario planning (contingency, "what if" - UPS, Boeing) · planned emergence (both, less formal - Airbnb, Uber, Lyft).

**Scenario analysis, in Grant's fuller treatment (Ch. 10).** Herman Kahn's definition: *hypothetical sequences of events constructed to focus attention on causal processes and decision points.* The **multiple scenario approach** builds several distinct, internally consistent views of the future 5–25 years out (shorter in fast-moving sectors), combining economic, technological, demographic and political factors into a few alternative stories. It can be **quantitative** (modelling and simulation) or **qualitative** (narratives, better at engaging decision-makers' imagination).

> [!important] The point of scenarios
> **Scenario analysis is not a forecasting technique** - it is a *process for thinking and communicating about the future*. **The value is in the process, not the results**: surfacing deeply held assumptions, identifying threats and opportunities, generating and stress-testing alternative strategies, building consensus, and forcing "what if?" questions that reveal which strategies are **robust** across scenarios.

*Shell* is the standard case: scenarios every ~4 years, trends analysed 20 years out, used because investment project lives (up to 50 years) far exceed the forecasting horizon (2–3 years). Shell is adamant they are **not forecasts**.

**Note the exam-relevant asymmetry:** *"being profitable"* ≠ *competitive advantage*. Advantage is **relative** - versus rivals or the industry average. A firm can be profitable and still be at competitive parity or disadvantage. (This distinction is called out explicitly on the Flashlight under RBV - see §6.)

## §1. Porter's Five Forces

> [!note]- Where else this lives
> **Deep dive:** [[What is Strategy? and Five Forces]]
> **Articles in play:** [[#Snowflake to Sierra|Snowflake to Sierra]] · [[#Universal Epic Universe|Universal Epic Universe]] · [[#$200 Billion Railroad|$200 Billion Railroad]]


### Core Idea

- The strategist's job is understanding competition, and competition is usually defined **too narrowly** (only today's direct rivals).
- **Industry structure** - the five forces - determines **long-run average profitability**, regardless of whether the industry is high-tech, regulated, emerging or mature.
- The model does **not** say whether an industry is profitable; it explains **why its profitability is what it is**.
- Rule of thumb: **stronger forces -> lower industry profit potential**. Identify the **strongest** force(s) - that's what strategy must address, and it is often *not* the obvious one.
- Analysis is framed from the **incumbent's** perspective, but works equally for evaluating entry.
- Use a **full business cycle** (~3–5 years; longer for capital-intensive industries) - average profitability over that window, not one year.

### 1.1 Threat of New Entrants - the seven barriers by name

Threat = f(**height of entry barriers**, **expected retaliation**). It's the *threat*, not actual entry, that caps profitability.

| # | Barrier | What makes it work | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Supply-side economies of scale** | Volume -> lower unit cost; entrant must enter big or accept a cost penalty | Intel (R&D/fab/marketing); parcel delivery |
| 2 | **Demand-side benefits of scale (network effects)** | Product becomes more valuable as more buyers adopt | eBay's trading network |
| 3 | **Customer switching costs** | Fixed cost of changing suppliers - retraining, migration, re-specification | SAP ERP |
| 4 | **Capital requirements** | Large upfront investment, especially **sunk** costs (ads, R&D) | Satellite constellations; new airlines |
| 5 | **Incumbency advantages independent of size** | Proprietary tech, preferential raw-material access, prime locations, brand identity, experience effects | Walmart/Target freestanding sites |
| 6 | **Unequal access to distribution channels** | Channels limited or locked up; entrant may have to build a new one | Low-cost airlines bypassing travel agents |
| 7 | **Restrictive government policy** | Licensing, foreign-investment limits, patents, safety/scale rules - can *raise or lower* barriers | Liquor retail, taxis, airlines |

**Expected retaliation is higher when:** incumbents have fought entrants before · incumbents hold deep resources (cash, spare capacity, channel clout) · incumbents are likely to cut prices (high fixed costs, share-at-all-costs) · industry growth is slow (entrants can only take share).

**Diversifying entrant** - a firm entering from an adjacent industry using existing capabilities/cash flow (Pepsi -> bottled water; Microsoft -> browsers; Apple -> music). Barriers must be judged against *the specific likely entrant*.

### 1.2 Supplier Power - drivers by name

A supplier group is powerful when it:
1. Is **more concentrated** than the industry it sells to
2. **Does not depend** heavily on that industry for its revenue
3. Faces buyers with **high switching costs** (specialized equipment, co-located facilities)
4. Offers a **differentiated / patented** product
5. Has **no substitute** for what it supplies (pilots' unions)
6. Can **credibly forward-integrate** into the industry

Powerful suppliers extract value via higher prices, reduced quality/service, or cost-shifting.

### 1.3 Buyer Power - drivers by name

Buyer power is high when:
1. **Few buyers**, or each purchases **large volumes** relative to a single seller (amplified in high-fixed/low-marginal-cost industries)
2. Industry products are **standardized/undifferentiated commodities**
3. Buyers face **low or no switching costs**
4. Buyers can credibly **backward-integrate**

**Price sensitivity** rises separately when: the purchase is a large share of the buyer's budget · the buyer earns low profits/is cash-short · the input's quality barely affects the buyer's own product quality.

### 1.4 Threat of Substitutes

- A **substitute** meets the same basic customer need **in a different way, from outside the industry** (energy drinks vs. coffee; videoconferencing vs. business travel; software vs. professional services; digital photography vs. film).
- Threat is high when: the substitute offers an attractive **price–performance trade-off**, and the buyer's **switching cost is low**.
- Watch adjacent industries - technological change can flip price-performance (plastics for steel; flash memory vs. low-capacity hard drives).

### 1.5 Rivalry Among Existing Competitors

**Familiar forms rivalry takes (name them):** price discounting · new product introductions/product innovation · advertising campaigns · service improvements.

**Intensity is high when:** many competitors · roughly equal size (no leader to set norms) · slow/zero/negative growth · **high exit barriers** · firms highly committed / non-economic goals · rivals can't read each other's strategies · products are direct substitutes · **high fixed / low marginal costs** · **excess capacity** · the product is **perishable** (including obsolescence-prone tech and unsold hotel nights).

**Basis matters as much as intensity:**
- **Price competition** is the most destructive - it transfers value straight to customers and is trivially matched.
- **Non-price competition** (features, service, brand) is less corrosive and can even strengthen the industry vs. substitutes/entrants.
- **Zero-sum rivalry** = rivals chasing the same customers/attributes. **Positive-sum rivalry** = rivals serving distinct segments, which raises average industry profitability.

### 1.6 Factors, NOT Forces (classic trap answers)

| Looks like structure | Why it isn't |
|---|---|
| **Industry growth rate** | Fast growth ≠ attractive. PCs grew fast with weak margins (low barriers, powerful buyers, strong substitutes). |
| **Technology / innovation** | High-tech ≠ attractive. "Boring" industries with switching costs or scale barriers often outperform. |
| **Government** | Not a sixth force - it works *through* the five (patents raise entry barriers; pro-union policy raises supplier power; lenient bankruptcy prolongs excess capacity). |
| **Complements** | Products consumed together whose combined value exceeds separate value (hardware + software; cars + gas stations). Not inherently good or bad - they **shift** the forces. The slides do float complements as "a sixth force?" - know both framings, plus **co-opetition** (cooperation among competitors, e.g. Samsung and Google). |

**Criticism of Five Forces (slides):** it is **static** - a point-in-time snapshot. It doesn't capture the changing speed of an industry, the rate of innovation, or structural change in progress. Hence the companion tools: **industry dynamics**, **industry life cycle**, **strategic groups**.

### 1.7 Strategy Implications

1. **Positioning** - find/build a spot where the forces are weakest, then tailor the value chain to defend it. *Paccar*: targeted owner-operators (less price-sensitive, emotionally invested), customizable premium trucks, strong resale, fast roadside support -> ~10% price premium, 68 straight profitable years, >20% ROE in a brutal industry.
2. **Exploiting industry change** - *Music industry*: internet distribution was predicted to flood the market with labels; the real barriers (risk-pooling across artists, promotional leverage) survived and majors consolidated 6 -> 4 - but unauthorized downloading was a genuine substitute and iTunes became a new gatekeeper.
3. **Shaping structure** - two levers:
   - **Redividing profitability** - capture more of the *existing* pool: standardize specs to neutralize supplier power, raise buyers' switching costs, differentiate to blunt price rivalry, raise fixed costs to deter entrants. *Sysco* private label. **Caution:** *IBM's open PC architecture* handed the OS and microprocessor to Microsoft and Intel and commoditized PCs.
   - **Expanding the profit pool** - grow total value (demand, quality, cost, waste), benefiting multiple participants. The forces still govern how the bigger pie is split.
4. **Defining the industry** - the forces help draw correct boundaries; misjudging boundaries is a common competitor error and therefore an opportunity.

### 1.8 Adjacent tools from the same module

- **General environment** (little managerial control; macro - PESTEL) vs. **task environment** (influenceable; industry structure, strategic groups).
- **Strategic group** - set of firms in the same industry pursuing a **similar strategy**. Build a map on two uncorrelated strategic dimensions; bubble size = market share.
  - Rivalry is **strongest within** a strategic group.
  - The external environment and the five forces affect groups **differently**; some groups are more profitable than others.
- **Mobility barriers** - industry-specific factors, based on hard-to-reverse strategic commitments, that restrict movement *between* strategic groups.
- **Competitive industry structure** is defined by: number/size of competitors · degree of pricing power · commodity vs. differentiated product · height of entry barriers - placing the industry on a **fragmented -> consolidated** continuum.

### Confusion Pairs

| Torn between | Ask only this |
|---|---|
| **Substitute** vs. **new entrant** | Does it meet the need **a different way from outside the industry** (substitute) or **the same way from inside** (entrant)? Streaming vs. cable = substitute; a new cable operator = entrant. |
| **Supplier power** vs. **rivalry** | Is value being captured by an **upstream party** (supplier power) or competed away **among peers** (rivalry)? |
| **Buyer power** vs. **price sensitivity** | *Leverage* (few buyers, standardized product, low switching costs) vs. *willingness to use it* (purchase is a big share of budget, buyer is cash-poor). Both raise pressure; the stem tells you which mechanism. |
| **Entry barrier** vs. **exit barrier** | Barriers to entry protect incumbent profits. **Exit** barriers keep failing firms *in*, which **intensifies rivalry** and hurts everyone. |
| **A force** vs. **a factor** | Growth rate, technology, government, complements -> **factors**. If the answer choice is one of these as a "force," it's almost certainly the trap. |
| **Network effects (entry barrier)** vs. **economies of scale** | Demand-side (value rises with *other users*) vs. supply-side (cost falls with *your volume*). |

## §2. PESTEL

> [!note]- Where else this lives
> **Deep dive:** [[What is Strategy? and Five Forces]]
> **Articles in play:** [[#Volvo EVs|Volvo EVs]] · [[#$200 Billion Railroad|$200 Billion Railroad]] · [[#Fast & Furious|Fast & Furious]]


### Core Idea

PESTEL scans the **general environment** - macro forces largely **outside the firm's control**. Its job on the exam is: *which of these matters most for this specific company?* PESTEL is also the tool that supplies the **O** and **T** of a SWOT (**VRIS/VRIO supplies S and W**).

### The Six Segments

| Segment | Covers | Typical exam cue |
|---|---|---|
| **Political** | Processes/actions of government bodies; shaped by lobbying, PR, contributions. Closely tied to legal. | Tariff policy, an administration's posture toward dealmaking, invitations to onshore |
| **Economic** | Macroeconomic: growth rates, employment, interest rates, price stability, exchange rates | Consumers scaling back vacations; a cyclical downturn |
| **Sociocultural** | Cultures, norms, values (constantly in flux, differ across groups); demographic trends - age, gender, family size, ethnicity, religion, socioeconomic class | Audiences losing interest in decades-old franchises; national-pride consumption shifts |
| **Technological** | Application of knowledge - process innovations (lean, Six Sigma, biotech) and product innovations (smartphones, wearables, EVs); AI/ML advances | Autonomous vehicles; generative AI agents |
| **Ecological** | Natural environment, global warming, sustainable growth; relationship can be adversarial *or* an opportunity | Solar integration; carbon-free power procurement |
| **Legal** | Official outcomes of political processes: laws, mandates, regulations, court decisions; note widespread deregulation (airlines, telecom, energy, trucking) | Antitrust suits; regulator approval of a merger |

**Two things the exam likes here:**
1. **Political vs. Legal.** Political = the *process/pressure* (lobbying, an administration's stance, who gets appointed). Legal = the *official output* (a statute, a regulation, a court decision, an antitrust suit). Legal factors "coexist with or result from political will."
2. **PESTEL is about the general environment, not the industry.** If the driver is buyers, suppliers, rivals, entrants or substitutes, it's **Five Forces**, not PESTEL.

**External analysis stack (memorize the order the slides give):** PESTEL -> Five Forces (+ Complements) -> Industry Dynamics -> Strategic Groups.

## §3. Industry Life Cycle

> [!note]- Where else this lives
> **Deep dive:** [[Industry Life Cycle]]
> **Articles in play:** [[#Fast & Furious|Fast & Furious]] · [[#Solar-Powered Cars|Solar-Powered Cars]] · [[#Robotaxi Race|Robotaxi Race]]


### Core Idea

Over the life cycle: the **number and size of competitors** change, **different types of consumers** enter, **supply and demand** change, and **different competencies** are needed to perform well. Two driving forces underneath it all: **demand growth** and the **creation and diffusion of knowledge**.

### 3.1 The Stages - and how to identify one from clues

The Flashlight is explicit: *be able to identify the stage from a set of clues (pricing trends, entry/exit, product standardization)* - not recite the order.

**Grant's Table 10.1 - read across a row to match the clue in the stem to a stage.** This is the single most useful table in §3.

| Clue dimension | **Introduction** | **Growth** | **Maturity** | **Decline** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Demand** | Limited to early adopters: **high-income, avant-garde** | **Rapidly increasing** market penetration | Mass market; **replacement/repeat buying**; customers knowledgeable and **price sensitive** | **Obsolescence** |
| **Technology** | **Competing technologies**; rapid product innovation | **Standardization around a dominant technology**; rapid process innovation | Well-diffused know-how; quest for technological improvements | **Little product or process innovation** |
| **Products** | Poor quality; wide variety of features and technologies; frequent design changes | Design and quality improve; **emergence of dominant design** | Trend to **commoditization**; attempts to differentiate by branding, quality, bundling | Commodities the norm; **differentiation difficult and unprofitable** |
| **Manufacturing & distribution** | Short production runs; **high-skilled labor content**; specialized distribution channels | **Capacity shortages**; mass production; competition for distribution | **Emergence of overcapacity**; deskilling of production; long production runs; distributors carry fewer lines | **Chronic overcapacity**; re-emergence of specialty channels |
| **Trade** | Producers and consumers in advanced countries | Exports from advanced countries to the rest of the world | Production shifts to newly industrializing, then developing, countries | Exports from countries with the **lowest labor costs** |
| **Competition** | **Few companies** | **Entry, mergers and exits** | **Shakeout**; price competition increases | **Price wars, exits** |
| **Key success factors** | **Product innovation**; establishing a credible image of firm and product | **Design for manufacture**; access to distribution; brand building; fast product development; process innovation | **Cost efficiency** through capital intensity, scale efficiency and low input costs | **Low overheads**; buyer selection; signalling commitment; **rationalizing capacity** |

> [!tip] Fastest tells
> **"High-income, avant-garde buyers" + competing technologies -> introduction.** **"Capacity shortages" -> growth** (the only stage where demand outruns capacity). **"Emergence of overcapacity" -> maturity.** **"Chronic overcapacity" -> decline.** Overcapacity language is the cleanest single discriminator in the table.

The course slides add a **shakeout** stage between growth and maturity - growth rate declines, weaker firms are forced out, industry consolidates, **price becomes an important weapon**. Grant folds shakeout into maturity (see the Competition row). Either framing is fine; know that "shakeout" names the consolidation event, not a separate demand regime.

**Four strategic options in decline:** **exit** (bankruptcy/liquidation) · **harvest** (reduce further investment) · **maintain** (support at a given level) · **consolidate** (buy rivals).

### 3.2 Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Dominant design** | A single product or process architecture that dominates a category - usually ≥50% of the market. A "de facto standard": not officially enforced but adopted industry-wide. |
| **Dominant design ≠ standard** | A design can dominate without setting a permanent standard (the **Boeing 707** was a dominant design for large passenger jets but set no lasting aerospace standard). Formal **technical standards** emerge where there are **network effects** - users need to connect with one another, so each customer picks what everyone else picks to avoid being stranded. The **IBM PC (1981)** did both: it set the design parameters *and* the MS-DOS/Intel x86 "Wintel" standard. |
| **Setting a dominant design ≠ profiting from it** | A firm that sets a dominant design **does not normally own intellectual property in it** (unlike a *proprietary* standard, embodied in patents/copyrights). So apart from some **early-mover advantage, there is no necessary profit advantage** from setting the dominant design. A high-value trap: "Firm X's design became the industry standard, therefore Firm X captured the profits" does **not** follow. |
| **Dominant designs in business models** | Not just products. **McDonald's (Ray Kroc, 1955)** established the dominant design for fast food - limited menu, no waiter service, eat-in/take-out, roadside locations, franchise ownership. In new markets, competition is often **between rival business models**: home grocery delivery saw dot-com startups (Webvan, Peapod) lose to "bricks 'n' clicks" incumbents (Albertson's, Giant). |
| **Product innovation** | New or recombined aspects of the *product*. Peaks early. |
| **Process innovation** | New ways to *produce* the product. Peaks later - after a dominant design emerges, emphasis shifts to manufacturing to cut cost and raise quality/reliability. |
| **Shift in the basis of competition** | Non-price -> price as the industry matures. |
| **Life-cycle theory of trade** | New industries begin in high-income countries -> served by exports -> newly industrialized countries import -> with maturity, commoditization and de-skilling shift production to low-labor-cost countries. |

### 3.2b How general is the life-cycle pattern? (the caveats Grant insists on)

The life cycle is a **pattern**, not a law. Four qualifications, each of which can be the correct answer to a "which statement is true" question:

1. **Duration varies enormously.** US railroads: introduction 1827 -> growth in the 1870s -> decline by the late 1950s. US autos: ~25-year introduction (1890s–1913). Personal computers: ~4-year introduction before growth took off in 1978. MP3 players: introduced 1997–98, growth with the iPod in 2001, apparently mature by 2007.
2. **Life cycles are compressing over time** - consumer electronics, communications, pharmaceuticals, and especially e-commerce (online gambling, B2B auctions, online travel went introduction -> apparent maturity within a few years). Cusumano and Yoffie call the resulting challenge **"competing on internet time."**
3. **Some industries never decline.** Industries supplying basic necessities - **residential construction, food processing, clothing** - may never enter decline, because obsolescence of the underlying need is unlikely.
4. **Rejuvenation happens - and it is not a natural phenomenon.** It is *typically the result of companies resisting the forces of maturity* through breakthrough product innovation or new market development. Motorcycles re-entered growth in the 1960s when Japanese manufacturers pioneered recreational use. TV receivers revived repeatedly: colour -> computer monitors -> flat screen -> HDTV. Retailing has been renewed in waves: chain stores (1880s) -> discount stores (1920s–60s) -> category killers -> warehouse clubs -> internet retailers.

**Different countries sit at different stages simultaneously.** The US auto market may be in early decline while China, India and Russia are in growth. Multinationals exploit this: develop and launch in advanced markets, then shift attention to growth markets as maturity sets in at home. (Same logic as the life-cycle theory of trade in §3.2.)

### 3.2c Organizational demographics - who is in the industry, and when

**Organizational ecology** (Hannan, Freeman, Carroll) treats industry evolution as a **Darwinian process**: the population of firms is set by **founding** and by **selection** through competition.

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Firm numbers rise fast early** | Pioneered by a few firms; as they gain **legitimacy**, failure rates fall and new foundings accelerate. Several hundred US auto producers early in the 20th century; 92 TV-receiver companies in 1951 |
| **Two kinds of entrant** | **De novo** - startups. **De alio** - established firms diversifying in from related industries |
| **Shakeout** | With maturity, firm numbers fall, often through one or more shakeouts. On average the shakeout occurs **~29 years into the life cycle** and **halves** the number of producers; afterwards entry/exit rates fall and incumbent survival rises sharply |
| **Resource partitioning** | Once the leaders concentrate on the mass market, **new entry becomes possible at the periphery** - as national brewers took the US mass market, microbreweries and brew pubs established specialist niches |
| **The exception to "maturity -> concentration"** | Where scale economies are unimportant and entry barriers low, maturity and commoditization can make concentration **fall** (credit cards, TV broadcasting, frozen foods). Some industries start as near-monopolies and get *more* competitive (Xerox in plain-paper copiers until the early 1980s) |

**Innovators are rarely the consolidators (Markides & Geroski).** The firms that create a market are usually *not* the ones that scale it - *the skills, mindsets and competences needed for discovery and invention are different from, and conflict with, those needed for commercialization.* Personal computers: pioneers Apple, Commodore, Xerox; 25 years later the leaders were Dell, Lenovo, Acer, HP. US wireless: pioneer McCaw Communications; later leaders Cingular and Verizon.

### 3.3 Crossing the Chasm - adopter categories (know the % and the stage)

| Adopter | Enters during | % of market | Behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Technology enthusiasts** | Introduction | 2.5% | Engineering mind; enjoy beta versions; tinker; give free feedback |
| **Early adopters** | Growth | 13.5% | Driven by imagination - "what can this do for me/my business?"; need the product's potential communicated directly |
| **Early majority** | Shakeout | 34% | Practical; weigh benefits vs. costs; rely on endorsements. **Key to catching the growth wave** |
| **Late majority** | Maturity | 34% | Not confident with the technology; wait for standards; dislike uncertainty; buy from established brands |
| **Laggards** | Decline | 16% | Reluctant; adopt only if necessary; generally not pursued |

### 3.4 Competitive Advantage in Mature Industries

- Maturity **narrows differentiation** (buyer sophistication, standardization), **diffuses process technology** (eroding cost advantage), and **exposes firms to powerful distributors**.
- **Buffett's franchise vs. business:** a *franchise* earns high returns regardless of management; a *business* earns profits only through low-cost operations or tight supply. Media companies slid from franchise -> business.
- **Three cost-advantage drivers in maturity:** economies of scale (ROI–market share link tightens) · low-cost inputs (Valero buying distressed refineries) · low overheads (Exxon/Mobil; PE roll-ups).
- **Turnaround strategies (Hambrick & Schecter):** asset/cost surgery · selective product-market pruning · piecemeal productivity moves.
- **Differentiation persists via** complementary services (financing, warranties) and **image differentiation** (near-identical products - cola, cigarettes).
- **Innovation myth:** mature industries look un-innovative by R&D *spending*, but patent data (McGahan & Silverman) shows output comparable to emerging industries.
- **Strategic innovation** - reconfiguring the value chain or redefining the market: new customer groups (Harley-Davidson, Sony), added related products/services (Arco, Barnes & Noble; "experience economy").
- **Baden-Fuller & Stopford's five conclusions:** maturity is a **mindset, not a market condition** · the **firm**, not the industry, determines outcomes · innovation reconciles opposing goals (quality at low cost) · pick defensible territory · requires entrepreneurial freedom to experiment.
- **Markides:** contrarian positioning drives rejuvenation (Edward Jones; Enterprise Rent-A-Car's suburban focus) - break the **industry recipe** (Spender: shared cognitive frameworks that trap incumbents).

### 3.5 Declining industries - adjusting capacity

The critical stabilizing factor is **capacity adjustment**, which depends on:
- **Predictability of decline** - foreseeable (digital imaging replacing film) allows planning; cyclical/volatile (steel) obscures the trend.
- **Barriers to exit** - durable/specialized assets, cash costs of closure, managerial/emotional attachment.
- **Strategies of survivors** - early recognition and collective action (European gasoline retailers swapping stations), stronger firms acquiring weaker rivals' assets, PE roll-ups.
- Some declining industries stay profitable: vacuum tubes, cigars, leather tanning.

### Confusion Pairs

| Torn between | Ask only this |
|---|---|
| **Growth** vs. **Shakeout** | Is the industry still **adding** entrants and standardizing (growth), or are **weak firms being forced out** and price becoming the weapon (shakeout)? |
| **Maturity** vs. **Decline** | Is demand **flat, replacement-driven, at max size** (maturity) or **falling** with innovation ceasing (decline)? |
| **Product** vs. **process** innovation | Is it a change to *what the thing is* or *how it's made*? Product peaks early; process peaks after the dominant design. |
| **Dominant design** vs. **standard** | Dominant design = de facto, market-selected. Standard = enforced by compatibility need or regulation (EU/GSM, FCC/NTSC). |
| **Life-cycle stage** vs. **maturity as a mindset** | If the stem is about a firm *rejuvenating* a "mature" industry, the answer is **strategic innovation** - maturity is a mindset (Baden-Fuller & Stopford), not a sentence. |

## §4. Industry Convergence & Digital Business Models

> [!note]- Where else this lives
> **Deep dive:** [[Innovation Strategies]]
> **Articles in play:** [[#Snowflake to Sierra|Snowflake to Sierra]] · [[#Nvidia Steps Back|Nvidia Steps Back]] · [[#Amazon Big-Ticket|Amazon Big-Ticket]]


### Core Idea

- **Industry convergence** - when previously **unrelated industries begin to satisfy the same customer need**, caused by technological advance (media: newspapers, magazines, TV, film, radio, music all converging online).
- Technology-based industries are distinguished by **increasing returns to adoption**: the more a technology is used, the more it improves and the more valuable it becomes. Two sources: **learning curve** and **network effects**.
- Increasing returns push markets toward **dominant designs** and **winner-take-all** outcomes - different competitive dynamics from ordinary industries.
- The digital/aggregation twist: when distribution and transaction costs go to ~zero, the winning move flips from integrating **backward into suppliers** to integrating **forward into consumers**, commoditizing suppliers.

### 4.1 Increasing Returns to Adoption

| Mechanism | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Learning effects** | Adoption generates revenue -> reinvested in refinement; firms accumulate experience and use the technology more productively. Learning curve: `y = ax^(−b)` - cost/labor per unit falls with **cumulative output**. |
| **Absorptive capacity** | Prior learning accelerates future learning: accumulated knowledge raises the ability to recognize, assimilate, and use new information. Explains why firms that fall behind the frontier struggle to catch up. |
| **Network externalities** (positive consumption externalities) | The value of a good to a user **rises with the number of other users** of the same/similar good (railroads, telephones, file compatibility). |
| **Installed base** | The number of users of a particular good - the thing network externality value attaches to. |
| **Complementary goods** | Additional goods/services that enable or enhance the value of another good (games for a console, apps for an OS, film for cameras). |
| **The self-reinforcing cycle** | Large installed base -> attracts producers of complementary goods -> availability of complements attracts users -> larger installed base. (Microsoft/Windows is the canonical case.) |
| **Path dependency** | Small historical events can have a large effect on the final outcome; technically superior technologies can lose (timing, sponsorship, lock-in). |

**Result - winner-take-all markets:** the market tends toward natural monopoly/duopoly; the firm whose technology becomes the dominant design earns near-monopoly rents *and* shapes future generations. A firm backing the loser may be forced to adopt the winner's technology (forfeiting its invested capital, learning, and brand) or be **locked out** entirely.

**When do multiple designs coexist?** When customers obtain **most of the network-externality benefit at a minority market share** - the value curve flattens early, creating a wide **indifference region**. Video game consoles are the example: three platforms coexist because there are already plenty of titles and players on each.

### 4.2 Multiple Dimensions of Value (why the better technology loses)

**Value of a new technology = stand-alone technological utility + installed base + availability of complementary goods.**

- For a new technology competing on **stand-alone utility alone**, that utility must exceed the *combined* value of the incumbent's utility + installed base + complements. **NeXT** is the cautionary case: technically superior, incompatible, no software, dead.
- Alternatively, make the new technology **compatible** with the incumbent's installed base and complements - then a moderate functionality advantage can win. **Sony/Philips Super Audio CD** played on standard CD systems; **PlayStation 2** was backward compatible.
- Customers weigh **actual**, **perceived**, and **expected** value. So firms manage perceptions: advertising, announcements of preorders, licensing and distribution agreements - and **vaporware** (announcing products that don't yet exist to stall adoption of a rival's). Sega vs. Nintendo installed-base claims; Nintendo's "Project Reality" delaying 32-bit purchases.
- **Buyer utility map** (Kim & Mauborgne): 6 stages (purchase, delivery, use, supplements, maintenance, disposal) × 6 utility levers (customer productivity, simplicity, convenience, risk, fun and image, environmental friendliness) = 36 cells; each is a possible new value proposition.

### 4.3 Platform vs. Pipeline

| | **Platform business** | **Pipeline business** |
|---|---|---|
| Logic | Enables **interaction** between producers and consumers; purpose is to **enable matches**; provides infrastructure and sets governance | **Linear** transformation along the value chain: R&D -> design -> manufacture -> sell |
| Advantages | Scales by **eliminating gatekeepers**; unlocks new sources of value creation and supply; benefits from community feedback | Control over the whole chain |

### 4.4 Aggregation Theory (Thompson) - the Flashlight's "how do they make money and defend it"

**The flip:** value chain = suppliers -> distributors -> consumers. Pre-internet, distributors won by integrating **backward** into suppliers (supply was scarce, distribution was expensive). With **zero distribution and transaction costs**, the winning move is integrating **forward into consumers** - own the user relationship, **commoditize (modularize) the suppliers**.

**The loop (this is the "defend it" answer):** best user experience -> most users -> attracts the most suppliers (they need access to those users) -> improves the experience further -> repeat. Winner-take-most, self-reinforcing, and **customer acquisition cost falls** as it runs - the inverse of a normal business, where acquisition cost rises as the best-fit early customers are exhausted.

**Three required characteristics of a true aggregator (all three, or it isn't one):**
1. **Direct relationship with users** (paid, account-based, or simply habitual)
2. **Zero marginal cost to serve an additional user** (no COGS, distribution, or transaction cost at the margin)
3. **Demand-driven multi-sided network with falling acquisition costs** - suppliers self-commoditize to reach the users

> Apple overall is **not** an aggregator (hardware has real marginal cost) - but the **App Store** is. Amazon's owned-inventory retail is **not**; **Marketplace/Merchant Services** is. Classification applies at the **business-line** level.

**Levels of aggregator - the Flashlight's "two common levels"**

| Level | Relationship to supply | Constraint | Typical industry condition | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Level 1 - supply acquisition** | **Buys or licenses** supply; market power springs from the user relationship but is manifested through **superior buying power** | Slow to build, precarious short-term; vulnerable to deeper-pocketed rivals or orthogonal business models | Industries where **supply is highly differentiated** | **Netflix** |
| **Level 2 - supply transaction costs** | Doesn't own supply, but **incurs transaction costs** bringing suppliers on (background checks, vehicle verification) | Supply growth throttled by those costs, which throttles demand growth | Industries with **regulatory concerns about supplier quality/safety** | **Uber** (Airbnb in some jurisdictions) |
| **Level 3 - zero supply cost** | Doesn't own supply and incurs **no supplier acquisition or onboarding cost** - suppliers make themselves *more* discoverable (an entire SEO industry exists to do it) | Requires **massive** user numbers | Usually **advertising-based**, therefore free to users (exception: App Stores, where higher revenue-per-customer offsets a smaller market) | **Google**, social networks |
| **Super-aggregator** | 3+ sided (users, suppliers, **advertisers**), zero marginal cost on **all** sides, **self-serve** ad platform | - | - | **Google and Facebook only** (Twitter/Snapchat still lean on sales-force-driven ad sales) |

> [!tip] Which two levels the Flashlight means
> **The contrast the Flashlight most likely means is Level 2 vs. Level 3** - i.e. *does the aggregator pay a per-supplier cost to onboard/vet supply (Uber), or does supply add itself for free and compete for discovery (Google)?* That is the difference that determines how fast the flywheel can spin. Know Level 1 too, since "buys/licenses its supply" (Netflix) is the easiest distractor.

**Conservation of Attractive Profits (Christensen)** - the mechanism underneath aggregation: when one layer of a value chain is **modularized/commoditized**, profit **shifts to whoever integrates a different, still-scarce layer**. Same idea as RBV's imitability paradox, applied to a chain instead of a firm.

**Regulation angle:** the **consumer-welfare standard** doesn't bite, because users *choose* the aggregator for genuinely better service; aggregation may be structurally inevitable (break one up and a replacement forms); and aggregators create real opportunity for suppliers (YouTube creators, Amazon merchants), so blunt remedies risk that layer too.

**Are winner-take-all markets good for consumers?** Trade off **network externality returns** to market share (s-shaped) against **monopoly costs** (higher prices, less variety, stifled innovation - roughly exponential). Below the crossing point, consumers gain; beyond it, monopoly costs exceed the benefits. Steepness of the monopoly-cost curve is partly **discretionary** - a firm can choose not to charge the maximum, or can exert power subtly by selectively aiding some complementors.

### Confusion Pairs

| Torn between | Ask only this |
|---|---|
| **Aggregator** vs. **platform** vs. **pipeline** | Aggregator = owns the **user relationship** + zero marginal cost + supply self-commoditizes. Platform = enables **matches** and sets governance. Pipeline = linear value chain. A platform with real marginal cost per user is not an aggregator. |
| **Level 2** vs. **Level 3** aggregator | Does the aggregator **pay to onboard/vet** each supplier (L2, Uber), or does supply **join free and compete for discovery** (L3, Google)? |
| **Network effects** vs. **economies of scale** | Value rises with **other users** (demand side) vs. unit cost falls with **your own volume** (supply side). |
| **Industry convergence** vs. **diversification** | Convergence is an **industry-level** phenomenon (unrelated industries start meeting the same need). Diversification is a **firm-level** choice to operate in multiple industries. |
| **Dominant design** vs. **disruption** | Dominant design = the architecture the market settles on. Disruption = a cheaper/simpler trajectory attacking from below. A firm can win the design battle and still be disrupted later. |

## §5. Innovation Strategies

> [!note]- Where else this lives
> **Deep dive:** [[Innovation Strategies]]
> **Articles in play:** [[#Solar-Powered Cars|Solar-Powered Cars]] · [[#Snowflake to Sierra|Snowflake to Sierra]]


### 5.1 The Four I's

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Idea** | Abstract concept or research finding |
| **Invention** | Transformation of an idea into a product; modification/recombination of products |
| **Innovation** | **Commercialization** of an invention |
| **Imitation** | Copying a successful innovation |

### 5.2 Types of Innovation - by name (this is what the Flashlight asks for)

**Axis 1 - how much new knowledge:**

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Incremental innovation** | Builds on established knowledge; steady improvement of existing products |
| **Radical innovation** | Novel methods and materials; **entirely new knowledge base** or recombination of existing knowledge; targets **new markets and new technology** |

**Axis 2 - the markets × technology 2×2 (the one the exam uses):**

| Type | Technology | Market | One-line test |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Incremental** | Existing | Existing | Steady improvement for the customers you already have |
| **Radical** | New | New | New knowledge base *and* new market |
| **Architectural** | **Existing/known components** used in a **novel way** | **New market** | *Existing technology leveraged into a new market* - the components are known, the architecture is rearranged |
| **Disruptive** | **New** technology | **Existing** market | *New product/process meets existing customer needs* - enters at the low end and moves up |

**Axis 3 - the Christensen distinction (the load-bearing one):**

| | **Sustaining technology** | **Disruptive technology** |
|---|---|---|
| Effect on performance | Improves it along dimensions **mainstream customers already value** | **Underperforms** on those dimensions, at least initially |
| Nature | Can be incremental **or radical** | Generally **simpler, cheaper, smaller, more convenient** |
| Who wins | Established/leading firms almost always win here | Emerges at the fringe; ignored by leaders |
| Initial customers | Mainstream, high-margin | **New or undemanding** customers who value convenience/price over performance |
| Margins | Higher | **Lower** margins, not greater profits |
| Trajectory | Eventually **overshoots** what the market needs | Improves fast enough to invade the mainstream **from below** |

> [!warning] Trap to memorize
> **Trap to memorize:** *"sustaining" does not mean "incremental."* A radical, expensive, technically heroic innovation aimed at your best customers is still **sustaining**. The distinction is about **which performance dimension and which customer**, not about magnitude.

**Established -> disruptive pairs from the text:** general hospitals -> outpatient clinics/nurse practitioners · manned fighter/bomber aircraft -> unmanned aircraft · full-service brokerage -> online/discount brokerage · desktop -> notebook computers · wireline -> mobile telephony · bricks-and-mortar -> online retailing · printed greeting cards -> free downloadable cards · classroom instruction -> distance education · offset printing -> digital printing.

**Why incumbents focus on incremental innovation (slides):** economic incentives (they must defend their position) · **organizational inertia** (formalized processes and structures) · the **innovation ecosystem** (they depend on particular suppliers, buyers, complementors).

**How to respond to disruptive innovation (slides):** keep innovating to stay ahead · **guard the low end of the market** · **disrupt yourself** rather than wait (reverse innovation).

### 5.3 The Innovator's Dilemma - five principles

Good management, correctly applied, is *why* strong firms fail. Listening to your best customers, chasing the highest-return R&D, and targeting the biggest markets is exactly what starves a disruptive technology.

| # | Principle | Prescription |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Companies depend on customers and investors for resources** - resource allocation is really controlled by them, not executives (**resource dependence**) | Place the project where the resource-allocation process will naturally support it - route around the dependence structurally, don't argue with it |
| 2 | **Small markets don't solve the growth needs of large companies** - a $40M firm needs $8M to grow 20%; a $4B firm needs $800M | Give the project to an organization **small enough to get excited** about a small market - an **autonomous organization** |
| 3 | **Markets that don't exist can't be analyzed** - planning and market research work for sustaining innovation, not for markets with no data | Plan **to learn and discover**, not to execute a forecast; treat projections as hypotheses to test cheaply (discovery-driven planning) |
| 4 | **An organization's capabilities define its disabilities** - capabilities live in **processes** and **values**; those tuned for high-margin mainstream work make the firm structurally unable to prioritize low-margin small-market projects | Assess whether the required processes/values match the parent; if not, **house it separately** |
| 5 | **Technology supply may not equal market demand** - products improve faster than customers can absorb (**performance overshoot**) | Track life-cycle position; overshoot is the leading indicator that the basis of competition is about to shift toward convenience, price, or reliability |

**Opening cases:** Sears (pioneered discount retail and catalog, >2% of all US retail at peak, then a $1.7B restructuring charge) · DEC (dominated minicomputers, rated among the best-managed firms, missed the desktop) · steel minimills (entered at rebar, ~40% of North American production by 1995; only 4 of ~30 integrated leaders survived) · mechanical excavators (cable-actuated leaders failed to make the hydraulic transition).

### 5.3b Which technological changes actually unseat incumbents (Grant Ch. 10)

Not all technological change threatens incumbents equally. Two cuts determine whether the established firm survives:

**Cut 1 - does the technology enhance or destroy existing competence?**

| | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Competence-enhancing** | Builds on what the firm already knows - incumbents usually win |
| **Competence-destroying** | Renders existing capabilities worthless - incumbents usually lose |

**Cut 2 - is the change at the component or the architectural level?**

| Level | Meaning | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Component** | A single process or product feature changes | Incumbents adapt well. In online groceries and online banking the internet was a **component-level** change - a new distribution channel for existing products - so established supermarkets and retail banks dominated with "clicks and bricks" models |
| **Architectural** | The change requires a **new configuration of the product** | Established firms struggle; startups tend to win - **but** ownership of key resources (brands, customer relationships, distribution) can carry incumbents through. The typesetter industry survived three waves of wrenching change because incumbents held customer relationships, sales and service networks, and font libraries |

**Cut 3 - even modest generational change can unseat leaders (Christensen's disk drives).** Across every generation - 14″ -> 8″ -> 5.25″ -> 3.5″ - established firms were on average **two years behind** newcomers in launching new products. Two barriers, and note that neither is architectural:
1. New technologies are **initially inferior** to existing ones
2. Established firms **listen to their customers**, and most customers don't want it - **fringe customers are the lead adopters**

So disruptive technologies unseat incumbents *even when they embody no architectural innovation*. The critical feature is that they **offer a very different package of attributes**:
- **Sony's early transistor radios** sacrificed sound fidelity but offered portability
- **Nucor's minimills** were initially lower quality and higher cost, but offered flexible, small-scale production close to customers
- In telecom equipment, newcomers **Cisco and Juniper** - not Lucent or Alcatel - exploited packet switching

**Who wins a brand-new industry?** Same de novo / de alio question as §3.2c. Where the resources and capabilities of an existing industry are **closely related** to those the new industry needs, **diversifying entrants beat startups**: former bicycle, carriage and engine makers were the best performers in US autos; former radio producers dominated US television manufacturing. But **spinoffs** - new ventures founded by former employees of existing firms in that industry - are often the best performers of all: US autos, the Akron tire industry, and Silicon Valley semiconductors (most leading players, Intel included, trace back to Shockley Semiconductor Laboratories).

### 5.4 Real Options Logic - investing under uncertainty

> [!danger] Reconstructed - verify this one
> *Reconstructed - see the gap note at the top. Barney Ch. 11 explicitly cross-references "real options logic (as developed in both Chapter 8 and Chapter 10)."*

**The core claim:** when the value of an opportunity is **highly uncertain**, do not commit fully. Make a **small, staged investment that buys the right - but not the obligation - to expand later**, and preserve **strategic flexibility** in the meantime.

**How it appears in this course:**
- **Governance choice (Barney Ch. 11, verbatim logic):** *if there is considerable uncertainty about the value of an economy of scope, real options logic suggests a firm should opt for **less hierarchical** forms of governance* (alliance, JV, licensing, minority equity) **to maintain strategic flexibility** - rather than a full acquisition, which is expensive and hard to reverse.
- **Innovation:** it is the economic formalization of Christensen's Principle 3 - under uncertainty, **plan to learn**, run small parallel experiments, and keep the option to scale or abandon.
- **Vertical integration:** the escalation continuum (spot -> long-term contract -> alliance/JV -> equity stake -> full ownership) is a real-options ladder - commitment rises only as uncertainty resolves and specific investment grows.
- **Differentiation:** the "small, parallel experiments with genuine tolerance for failure" prescription for managing a market vision is the same logic.

**The test to apply:** *Does the decision preserve the ability to expand if things go well and to walk away cheaply if they don't?* If a scenario describes a firm taking a **minority stake, a pilot, a JV, or a staged commitment specifically because the payoff is unknown**, the answer is real options / preserving flexibility - **not** risk reduction and **not** a market-power play.

### Confusion Pairs

| Torn between | Ask only this |
|---|---|
| **Sustaining** vs. **disruptive** | Does it improve performance on the dimension **mainstream customers already value** (sustaining, even if radical) or **underperform there while being simpler/cheaper for a fringe or new customer** (disruptive)? |
| **Radical** vs. **disruptive** | Radical = new knowledge base **and** new market. Disruptive = **new technology into an existing market**, entering from below. Radical is about novelty; disruptive is about **trajectory and customer**. |
| **Architectural** vs. **radical** | Architectural reuses **known components/existing technology** in a novel arrangement for a **new market**. If the components are already known, it isn't radical. |
| **Incremental** vs. **process** innovation | Incremental = builds on established knowledge (a category of novelty). Process = innovation in **how it's made** (a category of target). They overlap; answer whichever axis the stem describes. |
| **Overshoot** vs. **commoditization** | Overshoot = performance exceeds what customers can use, so they stop paying for more. Commoditization/modularization = the differentiator is stripped out and profit migrates elsewhere. |
| **Real options** vs. **risk reduction** | Real options = **preserving flexibility under uncertainty** about a specific opportunity's value. Risk reduction (diversification) = **smoothing cash flows** across imperfectly correlated businesses - which shareholders can do more cheaply themselves. |

## §6. Resource-Based View (RBV) - VRIS / VRIO

> [!note]- Where else this lives
> **Deep dive:** [[RBV]]
> **Articles in play:** [[#Robotaxi Race|Robotaxi Race]] · [[#Amazon Big-Ticket|Amazon Big-Ticket]] · [[#Snowflake to Sierra|Snowflake to Sierra]]


> [!important] Heavyweight topic
> The Flashlight flags this as a **heavyweight** topic. Expect to walk the criteria **in order** for an unfamiliar resource, name the sources of inimitability, and distinguish advantage from parity from "merely profitable."

> [!warning] Read this before anything else in §6 - VRIS vs. VRIO
> **This course's lecture material teaches VRIS, not VRIO.** The Module 5 course page ("Internal Analysis - Resource Based View"), the `RBV-1` deck, and the `Differentiation-1` deck all use **V**aluable -> **R**are -> costly to **I**mitate -> costly to **S**ubstitute. Barney's textbook chapter uses **VRIO**, where the fourth question is **O**rganization and substitutability is folded into imitability.
>
> **They are not in conflict - they answer different final questions**, and the exam can use either label:
>
> | | 4th criterion | The question it asks |
> |---|---|---|
> | **VRIS** (lecture, slides) | **Substitutability** | Can a rival get the *same effect* with a **different** resource? |
> | **VRIO** (Barney text) | **Organization** | Is *this firm* actually set up to **exploit** what it has? |
>
> Practical approach: run **V -> R -> I -> S** as the professor instructs, then ask the **O** question as a final check, since Barney's outcome table and the Xerox PARC case depend on it. Both are covered below.

### Core Idea

- Firms in the *same* hostile industry perform very differently (Dell, Walmart, Southwest, Nucor) - so structure isn't the whole story. RBV looks **inside** the firm.
- Two assumptions make RBV work: **resource heterogeneity** (firms hold different resource bundles) and **resource immobility** (those differences persist because resources are costly to copy or inelastic in supply).
- RBV **complements** Five Forces: Five Forces asks *what does the environment reward?*; RBV asks *does this firm have what it takes to capture it?* The **Value** question is the explicit hinge between the two.

### 6.1 Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Resource** | A relatively stable, observable, tradable asset owned by the firm (a brand, a location). Broadly: all assets, capabilities, processes, information and knowledge a firm controls. |
| **Capability** | Skills used to transform inputs into outputs and combine tangible/intangible resources toward a goal (practices producing superior quality or service). |
| **Core / distinctive competence** | Resources and capabilities that are the actual **source** of advantage; distinguish the firm competitively; emerge over time through organizational learning. Expressed through structures, processes, routines. |
| **Tangible resources** | Labor, capital, land, buildings, plant, equipment, supplies |
| **Intangible resources** | Culture, knowledge, brand equity, reputation, patents, designs, copyrights, trademarks, trade secrets - **often the best resources** |
| **Four resource categories (Barney)** | **Financial** capital · **Physical** capital · **Human** capital (individuals' training, experience, judgment, relationships) · **Organizational** capital (attributes of *collections* of people: structure, planning/control systems, culture, reputation) |
| **Economic rent** | Payment to a resource owner above the minimum needed to keep it in use - arises when a resource is inelastic in supply (Ricardian economics) |
| **Penrose's theory of growth** | The firm is an administrative framework plus a bundle of productive resources; growth is limited by that bundle |
| **Value-chain analysis** | Method of identifying resources/capabilities by disaggregating production into vertical activities and asking what each stage requires |

### 6.2 The VRIS Walk (in order - do not skip a step)

| Question | What you're asking |
|---|---|
| **V - Valuable** | Does it let the firm **improve efficiency or effectiveness** - i.e. **raise willingness to pay or reduce cost**, exploiting an opportunity or neutralizing a threat? (This is where external analysis re-enters.) Note: what is valuable **in one industry or at one time may not be in another** - value changes over time. |
| **R - Rare** | Is it possessed by only a **small number** of competing firms? It needn't be **unique - just scarce**. Moving to a low-cost location like China or India is **not** rare -> competitive parity. Brands are a resource, yet several firms each have their own. |
| **I - costly to Imitate** | Almost anything **can** be imitated - the question is whether the **cost of imitating is prohibitive** to competitors. Four sources: physical uniqueness · path dependency · causal ambiguity · social complexity (§6.3). |
| **S - costly to Substitute** | Can a rival achieve an **equivalent effect using a different resource**? E-commerce vs. bricks-and-mortar retailers · gas vs. electric vehicles · hotel rooms vs. rooms in people's houses. |
| *(O - Organized)* | **Barney's 4th question instead of S:** are structure, control systems and **compensation policies** actually set up to **exploit** the resource? Run this as a final check - the outcome table and Xerox PARC depend on it. |

#### The application rules the course states explicitly

These are process rules, and they are exactly the kind of thing an exam question can be built to violate:

1. **Apply the criteria to an activity or a resource - never to the firm as a whole.** Pick one thing the firm *does* or *has*, and test that.
2. **Go in order, and stop as soon as a criterion fails.** If manufacturing in China is valuable but every rival also manufactures in China, it isn't rare - **stop**. You don't need to evaluate imitability, because you've already shown it can't be a basis for sustainable advantage.
3. **Don't skip between activities across criteria.** Saying "manufacturing in China is valuable, *the brand* is rare, *the stores* are costly to imitate" is not an analysis - it's three unfinished ones. Take one activity/resource at a time all the way through.
4. **The criteria are inherently relative to competitors.** Judging rarity means comparing what this firm does against what rivals do. **You cannot evaluate a firm without discussing its competitors.**
5. **Use the criteria to evaluate the firm's cost-leadership and differentiation strategies** - this is *why* RBV is taught inside the business-level-strategy unit. Economies of scale are obviously valuable (they lower cost), but if the firm's main rivals are also large and also enjoy scale, that advantage **isn't rare**, so it cannot be the basis of sustainable advantage - the firm must rely on some *other* basis of cost or differentiation advantage.
6. **Expect most things to fail the test.** Most firms are average, some are below average, and only a small handful clear all four criteria. A scenario where the answer is "this is valuable but not rare -> parity" is the **normal** case, not a trick.

**Where each business strategy plugs in:** run VRIS on a **cost driver** (§7.1) or on a **basis of differentiation** (§8.1). The differentiation deck says this outright: *a product differentiation strategy must meet the VRIS criteria if it is to create competitive advantage.*

**The outcome table - memorize this shape.** (Barney's version, with **O** as the 4th column. In the lecture's VRIS version, substitute "costly to substitute?" for the 4th column and the implications are identical - a resource that is valuable, rare, costly to imitate **and** costly to substitute yields sustained competitive advantage. The lecture deck's own table reads: **Yes-Yes-Yes-No -> temporary competitive advantage.**)

| Valuable? | Rare? | Costly to imitate? | Organized to exploit? | Competitive implication | Performance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| No | - | - | No | **Competitive disadvantage** | Below normal |
| Yes | No | - | - | **Competitive parity** | **Normal** |
| Yes | Yes | No | - | **Temporary competitive advantage** | Above normal, until imitated |
| Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes | **Sustained competitive advantage** | Above normal, not competed away by imitation |

> [!important] This distinction gets tested directly
> **The distinction the Flashlight says "gets tested directly":**
> - **Competitive advantage** = performance **above** rivals/the industry average, from something **valuable AND rare**.
> - **Competitive parity** = **normal** performance - the resource is valuable but **not rare**. Note the asymmetry: using it earns you nothing extra, but *failing* to use it would put you at a **disadvantage**. Valuable-not-rare resources are still **strengths**.
> - **"Being profitable"** = an accounting outcome. A firm can be profitable while at parity (everyone in a good industry is profitable) or even while at a disadvantage in a boom. Profit is **absolute**; advantage is **relative**.
> - **Sustained ≠ permanent.** "Sustained" means the advantage isn't eroded **by imitation**. It can still be destroyed by a **Schumpeterian revolution**.

### 6.3 Sources of Inimitability - by name (the Flashlight names four)

| Source | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Physical uniqueness** | A patent, a specific physical location, a mineral deposit - few resources genuinely qualify |
| **Path dependency** | The resource was built and accumulated over time under earlier conditions; competitors can't buy it instantaneously and can only replicate it at disproportionate cost. Two sub-mechanisms: **asset mass efficiencies** (a strong initial position in technology/channels/reputation makes further accumulation easier) and **time-compression diseconomies** (rushing costs extra - crash R&D, blitz ad campaigns) |
| **Causal ambiguity** | Imitators - sometimes even the advantaged firm's own managers - **cannot identify which resource actually causes the advantage**, so they don't know what to copy. Often organizational capabilities depending on a configuration of activities and social interactions |
| **Social complexity** | The resource is a socially complex phenomenon (culture, interpersonal relations, reputation) that cannot be systematically engineered or purchased even when the value link is understood |

**Related terms in the same family:**
- **Two imitation routes:** **direct duplication** (build the same resource) and **substitution** (use a *different* resource for the same effect - Komatsu's more reliable equipment substituting for Caterpillar's service network).
- **Invisible assets** (Itami) - resources so embedded in routine the firm's own managers don't recognize them.
- **Interconnectedness of asset stocks** - advantage from thousands of small bundled decisions, so there's no single thing to copy (The Mailbox, Inc.).
- **Patents** - raise imitation cost (pharma) but can also **lower** it: filing discloses information and rarely blocks a functionally equivalent substitute. Pfizer's Lipitor: ~$2.5B to develop, $125B+ in sales; on expiry, 45 of 55 million prescriptions moved to generics.
- **Isolating mechanisms** - the slides' umbrella term: favorable expectations of future resource value, path dependence, causal ambiguity, social complexity, IP protection.

### 6.4 Dynamics

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Schumpeterian revolution** | A sudden, unpredictable shift in threats/opportunities (demand, technology, politics) that **destroys the value** of previously VRIO resources - PCs devaluing typewriter-making skill |
| **Imitability paradox** | The **cheaper** a resource is for you to acquire/develop, the **less likely** it sustains advantage - because it's cheap for competitors too |
| **Core rigidity** | A former core competency that becomes a **liability** after the environment shifts |
| **Dynamic capabilities** | The ability to adapt, create, reconfigure and upgrade resources over time to maintain strategic fit |
| **Resource stocks and flows ("bathtub")** | Stock = current level of an intangible resource; flow = ongoing investment needed to offset natural depreciation/forgetting |

### 6.5 Five Implications of RBV (Barney Table 5.4)

1. **Competitive advantage is every employee's responsibility.** The plant manager / line manager / janitor example: all three *could* frame their job as "helping make and sell the best cars in the world," but only the janitor's framing is **rare** - so only it is a plausible source of advantage.
2. **Imitating competitors only ever buys parity.** Benchmarking is for catching up, not for advantage.
3. **Relative cost of implementation matters more than absolute cost.** Not "is this hard?" but "is it harder for competitors than for us?" Firms err by **overestimating** their uniqueness (assuming rarity when rivals have equivalents) and by **underestimating** it (assuming what's easy for them is easy for everyone).
4. **Socially complex resources** (culture, teamwork, empowerment) can be valuable, rare *and* costly to imitate - not just nice to have.
5. **Organization should follow resources, not the reverse.** If structure conflicts with VRI resources, change the organization (Caterpillar and Walmart did; Xerox didn't).

### 6.6 Limitations

- **Sustained ≠ permanent** (Schumpeterian revolutions displace rather than imitate).
- **Limited managerial influence / imitability paradox** - managers can't simply decide to create sustained advantage; only resources built through path dependence, causal ambiguity or social complexity qualify, and those are often unplanned.
- **Unit-of-analysis problem** - unlike Five Forces (industry level, good public data), RBV works at the level of individual firm resources; the resources that sustain advantage are, by construction, hard to observe and describe - so they're hard to study.

### 6.7 Named Examples

| Case | What it illustrates |
|---|---|
| **Xerox PARC** | **The "O" failure case.** PC, mouse, laser printer, Ethernet - valuable, rare, costly to imitate - never commercialized because reporting structure, bureaucratic development process and revenue-only compensation weren't built to exploit them |
| **Dell** | VRIO applied across a value chain: purchasing = temporary advantage; assembly = valuable/rare/costly (200+ patents, embedded process culture) -> closer to sustained; sales/support extended by reputation |
| **Walmart** | Point-of-purchase inventory system = **temporary** (Kmart imitated it); **rural locations** = harder to imitate, plausibly sustained |
| **Southwest / Nucor** | Profitable in industries with massive cumulative losses / declining integrated competitors |
| **USS, Sears** | **Value** failures - resources became *not valuable* because the firms couldn't recognize structural change |
| **Sony** | Individual products reverse-engineered quickly (temporary), but the underlying **miniaturization capability**, reapplied across launches, is sustained |
| **Caterpillar** | Global service network built under unique historical conditions (WWII contracts) - **time-compression diseconomies**; Komatsu **substituted** (more reliable equipment) rather than duplicating |
| **Pepsi vs. Coke / vs. Dr Pepper** | Same resources: valuable but **not rare** vs. Coke -> **parity**; valuable **and rare** vs. Dr Pepper -> advantage. *The single cleanest illustration that rarity is relative to the comparison firm.* |
| **Honda** | Small-engine design/manufacturing core competency built over decades, redeployed across cars, motorcycles, boats, generators, mowers, IRL engines |
| **Five Guys / Beats / Tesla / Netflix** | Core competencies: quality ingredients + free toppings + simple menu (and deliberately **no** drive-throughs) · "coolness" perception · battery/powertrain engineering · proprietary recommendation algorithms |
| **Google/Alphabet** | Tangible resources ~$59B vs. intangible brand value $300B+ - the best resources are often intangible |

### Confusion Pairs

| Torn between | Ask only this |
|---|---|
| **Competitive advantage** vs. **competitive parity** | Is the resource **rare**? Valuable-but-common = parity, full stop. |
| **Parity** vs. **disadvantage** | Parity = valuable, not rare -> normal performance. Disadvantage = **not valuable** (or valuable and *not used*). |
| **Temporary** vs. **sustained** | Is it **costly to imitate**? If rivals can duplicate or substitute at no cost penalty, it's temporary - regardless of how big today's lead is. |
| **"Costly to imitate"** vs. **"Organized"** | Is the obstacle **outside** the firm (rivals can't copy it) or **inside** (the firm can't exploit what it has)? Xerox PARC and Swatch = O-failures, not I-failures. |
| **Resource** vs. **capability** | A tradable, observable **asset** vs. a **skill** for transforming inputs and combining resources. |
| **Path dependency** vs. **causal ambiguity** | *We know what it is but can't build it fast/cheap now* (path dependency) vs. *we can't tell what "it" even is* (causal ambiguity). |
| **Social complexity** vs. **causal ambiguity** | Relationships/culture that can't be engineered or bought even when understood (social complexity) vs. the link itself being unidentifiable (causal ambiguity). |
| **Substitution** vs. **direct duplication** | Same resource rebuilt (duplication) vs. a **different** resource achieving the same effect (substitution). |
| **Core competence** vs. **core rigidity** | Identical resource - the difference is whether the **environment still rewards it**. |
| **Substitutability (S)** vs. **Imitability (I)** | Can a rival get the same effect with a **different** resource (substitute), or must they build **the same one** (imitate)? Komatsu building more reliable equipment instead of copying Caterpillar's service network = **substitution**. |
| **Applying it to the firm** vs. **to an activity** | If an answer choice evaluates "the company" rather than a **specific activity or resource**, it's violating the method - the course states the test applies to an activity/resource, one at a time. |
| **RBV assumptions** | **Heterogeneity** = firms hold *different* bundles. **Immobility** = those differences *persist* because resources don't move freely. Both are needed; heterogeneity alone would be competed away. |

## PART IV - BUSINESS (COMPETITIVE) STRATEGY

### Generic Business Strategies - the frame for §7–§9

**Business-level strategy** = goal-directed actions to secure competitive advantage in a **single product market**. It answers: *who* (which customer segments), *what* (which needs), *why*, and *how*.

Two dimensions:
- **Strategic position:** cost leadership vs. differentiation
- **Competitive scope:** broad vs. narrow

| | **Cost leadership** | **Differentiation** |
|---|---|---|
| **Broad scope** | GM | Cadillac |
| **Narrow scope (focused)** | BIC | Mont Blanc |

- **Differentiation** creates **higher perceived value** vs. competitors (unique features) and charges higher prices -> increases **V**.
- **Cost leadership** creates **similar value** at **lower cost** -> reduces **C**, then may charge lower prices.
- Either wins when **V − C > competitors' V − C**. Cost leaders must maintain **adequate value**; differentiators must keep cost **competitive**.

## §7. Cost Leadership

> [!note]- Where else this lives
> **Deep dive:** [[Low Cost]]
> **Articles in play:** [[#Fast & Furious|Fast & Furious]] · [[#Robotaxi Race|Robotaxi Race]] · [[#Volvo EVs|Volvo EVs]]


### Core Idea

- Lower economic cost (**C**) below competitors **while maintaining acceptable perceived value (V)** - then either harvest a wider margin or underprice rivals for share.
- **Constantly monitored** is the operative phrase: cost cutting that drops V below the customer's acceptability threshold destroys the strategy.
- A cost advantage is **sustained** only when its underlying source is **rare and costly to imitate**.

### 7.1 The named lists - know all three framings

The Flashlight's list is closest to the lecture/Grant framing. The exam draws on readings *and* slides, so hold all three and map them.

**A. Lecture/slides - "drivers that keep costs low":**
1. **Cost of input factors** (raw materials, capital, labor, IT services - *why location matters*)
2. **Economies of scale**
3. **Learning-curve effects** (less time to produce output with experience)
4. **Experience-curve effects** (improvements to technology and production processes)

**B. Grant's seven cost drivers (the Flashlight's phrasing maps here):**

| Cost driver | Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Economies of scale** | Spread fixed costs, specialization, technical volume rules (cube-square) | Microsoft spreading Windows R&D over huge volume |
| **Economies of learning** | Repetition -> individual dexterity + organizational routines | Semiconductor fab yield improvement |
| **Process technology** *(≈ "production technique")* | Superior production processes or organizational methods | Pilkington float glass; Toyota lean production |
| **Product design** *(≈ "design")* | Standardize components; design for automated assembly (DFM) | IBM Proprinter: 150 -> 60 parts |
| **Capacity utilization** | Spread fixed costs over short-run output; adjust capacity fast | Cruise lines filling berths; parcel networks filling trucks |
| **Input costs** | Locational price gaps, non-union labor, buyer bargaining power | Wage arbitrage; Walmart's volume leverage |
| **Residual efficiency** *(≈ "overhead")* | Eliminate organizational slack and **X-inefficiency** | Nucor's flat structure; Walmart's thrifty culture |

**C. Barney's sources of cost advantage (used in the rarity/imitability tables):**
1. Size differences -> **economies of scale**
2. Size differences -> **diseconomies of scale**
3. Experience differences -> **learning-curve economies**
4. **Differential low-cost access to productive inputs**
5. **Technological "hardware"** (machines, market-purchasable)
6. **Technological "software"** (culture, labor relations, tacit routines)
7. **Policy choices**

### 7.2 Scale vs. Learning - the distinction they love to test

| | **Economies of scale** | **Learning-curve economies** |
|---|---|---|
| Function of | **Output per period** (volume at a point in time) | **Cumulative output** (experience over time) |
| Reverses? | **Yes - diseconomies of scale** beyond the optimum | **No** - costs asymptotically approach a technological floor |
| Key term | **Minimum efficient scale (MES)** - lowest output at which long-run average unit cost is minimized | **Law of experience** (BCG): value-added unit costs fall a constant % (typically 20–30%) each time cumulative volume doubles |
| Formula | - | `y = a · x^(−β)` |

**Major sources of economies of scale:** spreading fixed costs · specialized systems and equipment · technical/physical properties (cube-square rule). **Diseconomies of scale:** firms too big; coordination complexity; inflexibility and slowness. *(Crown Cork & Seal ran 75 lines in one plant vs. an optimal 10–15.)*

**Economies of scope** (contrast term): savings from producing **two different outputs** together, sharing the same resources or technology - this is the **diversification** concept (§11), not a scale concept.

### 7.3 Sustainability - which cost sources actually last

| Source | Likely rare? | Costly to duplicate? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Economies of scale | No (unless MES ≈ total industry demand) | Easy - rivals expand or merge | Temporary / parity |
| Avoiding diseconomies | No | Easy - shrink or restructure | Temporary |
| **Learning-curve economies** | **Yes in emerging industries**, no in mature ones | Variable - easy if knowledge leaks | Temporary -> sustained |
| **Differential low-cost access to inputs** | **Yes** - often location-dependent or historical | **Costly** - physical uniqueness, path dependency | **Sustained** |
| Technological **hardware** | No (purchasable) | Easy | Temporary |
| Technological **"software"** | **Yes** - rooted in history, culture, relationships | **Costly** - social complexity, causal ambiguity | **Sustained** |
| Policy choices | No, if the attribute is observable | Costly only if rooted in complex processes | Temporary -> sustained |

### 7.4 Organizing for Cost Leadership

- **Structure:** **U-form / functional**, **flat hierarchy** (Nucor ~5 layers vs. rivals' 12–15), **lean corporate staff**, narrow set of core functions.
- **Controls:** strict quantitative cost targets, frequent reporting, close supervision of materials/labor/inventory, an embedded **cost philosophy** of thrift.
- **Compensation:** rewards explicitly tied to verified cost reduction; broad-based incentives so every worker hunts efficiencies (Walmart sharing shrinkage savings).

### 7.5 Cost Leadership vs. the Five Forces

| Force | How cost leadership blunts it |
|---|---|
| **Entry** | Creates a cost-based entry barrier |
| **Rivalry** | Protects margin in a price war; allows selective underpricing |
| **Buyers** | Absorbs price-push pressure; deters backward integration |
| **Suppliers** | Margin flexibility to absorb input inflation; volume leverage reduces supplier power |
| **Substitutes** | Low prices keep the current product attractive vs. alternatives |

**Risks:** quality/value falling below the acceptability threshold · **blindness to radical innovation** while optimizing current process · imitation by rivals (or cheaper offshore sourcing).

### Confusion Pairs

| Torn between | Ask only this |
|---|---|
| **Economies of scale** vs. **learning curve** | Is cost falling because of **volume this period** (scale) or **cumulative experience to date** (learning)? "Since 2015, packages per employee went from 175 to 3,870" = learning. "One plant serves 600 stores" = scale. |
| **Learning curve** vs. **experience curve** | In the slides: learning = **less time** to produce with experience; experience = **improvements to technology and production processes**. If the stem emphasizes process/tech improvement rather than labor time, pick experience. |
| **Capacity utilization** vs. **economies of scale** | Scale = you built a bigger, cheaper-per-unit operation. **Utilization** = you're spreading fixed costs of the operation you already have over **more or less** short-run output. Idle warehouses, empty trucks, unfilled cabins = utilization. |
| **Input costs** vs. **economies of scale** | Cheaper *per-unit inputs* because of **where/from whom you buy** (input costs) vs. because of **how much you buy or make** (scale). Parent-company purchasing leverage sits at the boundary - if the stem stresses **bargaining power from volume**, either can be argued; pick the one the stem's *mechanism* names. |
| **Residual efficiency / overhead** vs. **process technology** | Removing **slack and X-inefficiency** without changing formal scale or technology (residual) vs. adopting a **superior production process** (process technology). |
| **Product design** vs. **process technology** | Redesigning **the product** for cheaper assembly (fewer parts, standard components) vs. changing **how it's produced**. |
| **Diseconomies of scale** vs. **premature scaling** | Operating **above** the optimal scale (diseconomies) vs. expanding **before mastering yield/learning** at one site (Northvolt) - the second is a learning-curve/quality failure that looks like the first. |
| **Cost leadership** vs. **low price** | Cost leadership is about **C**. A firm can be a cost leader and *not* cut price (harvesting margin). A firm can cut price without any cost advantage - that's just a bad idea. |

## §8. Differentiation

> [!note]- Where else this lives
> **Deep dive:** [[Differentiation]]
> **Articles in play:** [[#Universal Epic Universe|Universal Epic Universe]] · [[#Amazon Big-Ticket|Amazon Big-Ticket]] · [[#Solar-Powered Cars|Solar-Powered Cars]]


### Core Idea

- **Product differentiation** = increasing customers' **willingness to pay** by making them **perceive** the firm's product as more valuable than rivals'.
- It is **always a matter of perception**. Two nearly identical products can be perceived as different (craft vs. mass-market beer from the same contract brewer); two genuinely different products can be perceived as the same (Casio's more advanced quartz movement vs. Rolex's mainspring). Firms can even suffer differentiation they never intended.
- **Almost anything can be a basis** - the limit is managerial creativity, not a fixed list. But a base must fill a **real customer need** (image, comfort, status, style, taste, safety, quality, service, reliability, belonging, nostalgia, furthering a cause…).
- Differentiation raises **revenue** (V up); cost leadership lowers **cost** (C down). Both raise economic profit.
- For **sustained** advantage the basis must be **VRIS**: **V**aluable, **R**are, costly to **I**mitate, costly to **S**ubstitute.

### 8.1 The 12 Bases (Barney Table 7.1) - organized in three categories

> The Flashlight names seven of these: *product features, consumer marketing, linkages with other firms, distribution channels, service/support, product reputation, product mix.* Learn all twelve - the seven named are the ones most often confused with each other.

**A. Product attributes (exploit the actual product)**

| # | Basis | Illustration |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Product features** | The shape of a golf club head; a squeeze bottle for olive oil |
| 2 | **Product complexity** | Multiple functions on a watch; Mont Blanc vs. a BIC pen |
| 3 | **Timing of introduction** | Being first to market (or entering later once complements exist) |
| 4 | **Location** | Next to a freeway exit; a hotel by a transit hub |

**B. Firm–customer relationships**

| # | Basis | Illustration |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | **Product customization** | A unique diamond bracelet for one customer |
| 6 | **Consumer marketing** | Image advertising creating brand loyalty to a soap |
| 7 | **Product reputation** | Sponsoring a local shelter to build community response; years of consistent interaction |

**C. Firm linkages (within and between firms)**

| # | Basis | Illustration |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | **Linkages among functions within a firm** | A circuit board designed in one division used in others; **architectural competence** - coordinating genetics/chemistry/pharmacology in drug R&D |
| 9 | **Linkages with other firms** | A sporting goods store sponsors a race with donated shoes and gets free radio advertising; **co-branding**; **product placement** |
| 10 | **Product mix** | A furniture store adding home gyms, computers, lawn mowers; bundling technologically linked products or products sharing a customer base |
| 11 | **Distribution channels** | A doughnut shop selling through gas stations |
| 12 | **Service and support** | An oil-change shop offering pick-up and delivery from an office garage |

**The slides' compressed version - "three drivers that increase perceived value":** product features · customer service · **complements**. (Land Rover console fridges; Zappos free two-way shipping / Trader Joe's; Google + Project Fi, AT&T + original iPhone exclusivity.)

**Bases are not mutually exclusive** - firms typically stack several at once.

### 8.2 Rarity and Imitability

**Rarity is assumed by definition:** if a product is genuinely differentiated it is rare enough - customer preference (higher volume **and/or** a price premium) *is* the evidence. Staying rare depends on continuing managerial creativity: you stay ahead only by innovating faster than rivals copy your last move.

**Cost of duplication by basis (Barney Table 7.3) - memorize the three tiers:**

| Tier | Bases | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Low-cost duplication possible** | **Product features** | Rivals buy it and reverse-engineer it; launching reveals the basis immediately |
| **May be costly** | Product mix · linkages with other firms · product customization · product complexity · consumer marketing | Sometimes easy, sometimes rests on socially complex relationships |
| **Usually costly** | Linkages among functions · timing of introduction · location · **reputation** · distribution channels · service and support | Rooted in firm history, uncertainty, and/or social complexity |

**Sources of imitation cost (same family as RBV):** physical uniqueness · **historical uniqueness** (Caterpillar's WWII service network) · **causal ambiguity** · **path dependency** · **social complexity**.

**Substitution** works two ways: (a) **within the 12** - customization and complexity are near-substitutes, so a rival emphasizing one erodes the other; (b) **another strategy entirely** - a rival matches your product-mix advantage through a strategic alliance. Bases with few obvious substitutes (**timing, location, distribution channels, service and support**) sustain advantage best.

### 8.3 Differentiation vs. the Five Forces

| Threat | How differentiation blunts it |
|---|---|
| **New entry** | Entrants must absorb the extra cost of overcoming incumbent differentiation, on top of normal startup costs |
| **Rivalry** | Firms occupy distinct niches -> less head-to-head competition for the *same* segment |
| **Substitutes** | The differentiated product looks more attractive than the alternative |
| **Supplier power** | Loyal customers absorb pass-through more easily - though pushed too far this invites suppliers to raise prices further |
| **Buyer power** | Customers who want *this specific* product must buy from this firm |

### 8.4 Differentiation & the environment

- **Fragmented industries** - a brand can consolidate a commodity market (Xerox branding jam-resistant office paper).
- **Emerging industries** - first-mover advantages (technological leadership, asset preemption, switching costs).
- **Mature industries** - shifts from radical innovation to product refinement and bundling.
- **Declining industries** - lead the shrinking market or hold a defensible niche.

### 8.5 The economics

Under perfect competition, firms face a **horizontal demand curve** and earn only normal profit. Differentiation gives the firm a **downward-sloping demand curve** - **monopolistic competition** (Chamberlin): each firm holds a mini-monopoly in its own niche and earns **economic profit** where MR = MC if price exceeds average total cost. Entry into the niche shifts the incumbent's demand curve down and left, eroding profit toward normal - **unless the basis is rare and hard to imitate**.

**Identifying bases empirically:** **multidimensional scaling** (inductive - customers rate similarity, dimensions are extracted) and **regression analysis of price determinants** (deductive - regress price on hypothesized attributes).

### 8.6 Organizing to implement differentiation

**Structure:** U-form supplemented with **product development teams** (temporary, cross-functional) or **product management teams** (permanent - signals a shift toward M-form).
**Compensation:** **balanced scorecard** - multiple weighted measures (customer survey scores, on-time delivery, patents/new products, ROIC) because implementation cuts across sales, manufacturing and R&D.

**Four organizing dilemmas (Brown & Eisenhardt) - too much vs. too little:**

| Dilemma | Too much | Too little | Resolution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interfunctional collaboration | Lockstep - slows innovation | Implementation fails | U-form + product development/management teams |
| Institutional control | Bureaucracy - stifles innovation | Chaos, no direction | Broad guidelines with managerial freedom inside them; five leadership roles: **institutional leader, critic, entrepreneur, sponsor, mentor** |
| Connection to the past | History as a constraint (Disney's "what would Walt do") | Fails to exploit past advantage (Apple Newton) | Deliberately combine old and new |
| Commitment to a market vision | Inflexible overcommitment (Monsanto) | No direction | Small parallel experiments + genuine tolerance for failure |

**Innovation process stages (Van de Ven):** Initiation (gestation -> shock -> plans) -> Development (proliferation -> setbacks -> shifting criteria -> fluid staffing -> top-management involvement -> external relationships -> infrastructure) -> Implementation/Termination. **Not linear** - projects cycle back repeatedly.

### Confusion Pairs

| Torn between | Ask only this |
|---|---|
| **Product features** vs. **product complexity** | A *changed attribute* (features) vs. the *number/sophistication of components* (complexity). |
| **Consumer marketing** vs. **product reputation** | Marketing = **paid, deliberate image-shaping** without necessarily changing the product. Reputation = **socially complex, built over years of consistent interaction** - and therefore much harder to duplicate. If the stem stresses years of trust, it's reputation. |
| **Product mix** vs. **linkages with other firms** | Is the extra value from **your own broader range/bundle** (product mix) or from **tying to another firm's product or brand** (linkages)? A bundle of *someone else's* subscription = linkage. |
| **Distribution channels** vs. **service and support** | *How the product reaches the customer* vs. *what you do for them before/after the sale*. |
| **Location** vs. **distribution channels** | Where the firm physically is vs. the path the product takes to market. |
| **Timing of introduction** vs. **product features** | Is the advantage *being first* or *what the thing does*? "First U.S. carrier to offer an AI perk" = timing. |
| **Differentiation** vs. **cost leadership** | Does the move raise **V** (willingness to pay) or lower **C**? Adding a feature to justify a premium = differentiation. Automating to cut unit cost = cost. |
| **Defensive differentiation** | A basis (certification, verified listings, hologram stickers) used to **protect existing brand equity from counterfeiters** rather than to win new customers. Not the textbook's growth framing - worth naming if a stem describes damage control. |

### 8.7 Named Examples

| Example | Basis | Point |
|---|---|---|
| Rolex vs. Timex/Casio | Features + perception | Differentiation is **perceptual**, not technical |
| Graza olive oil | Product features + consumer marketing + distribution | Squeeze bottle copied by Brightland and California Olive Ranch within ~1 year - **features are the easiest basis to duplicate**; response was a new packaging twist (canned refills) |
| Liquid Death, Fiji, Evian | Packaging/branding only | Near-identical underlying product |

| Apple Watch Ultra | Features + timing + product-line tiering | Satellite texting reserved for the $799 tier vs. SE $249 / Series $399 - differentiation used to **segment one product line** |
| Celsius | Consumer marketing | Ad spend $85M -> $222M to broaden beyond a fitness niche - marketing-based differentiation is **imitable** by Monster/Red Bull |
| Disney Cruise Line | Product mix (IP linkages) + service + reputation | ~2× Royal Caribbean's price; 82% rebooking |
| EssilorLuxottica × Meta | Linkages with other firms | Pre-2022 tech-led eyewear failed; the Meta partnership worked by keeping the **iconic brand** front and center |
| Pfizer (Nurtec, Abrysvo) | Service/support + product mix | Revived sales through sales-force reallocation, a support hotline, and bundling - differentiation applied to the **process around an unchanged product** |
| AG1 / Now Foods | Reputation, verification | **Defensive** differentiation against counterfeiters |
| Starbucks China vs. Luckin | Failed feature novelty | A pork-flavored latte doesn't offset a rival's structural cost advantage |

## §9. Stuck in the Middle (and Blue Ocean)

> [!note]- Where else this lives
> **Deep dive:** [[Differentiation]] · [[Low Cost]]


### Core Idea

**Porter's original claim:** a firm attempting **both** cost leadership and differentiation simultaneously will perform **worse** than firms that pick one.

**The Flashlight is precise about why - and the reason is not simply "it costs too much":** the two strategies require **contradictory organizational setups and conflicting activities/positioning**.

| Requirement | Cost leadership | Differentiation |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Simple, **flat**, few layers, lean staff | U-form **plus cross-functional** product development/management teams |
| Control systems | **Tight** quantitative cost targets, close supervision, frequent reporting | **Loose** - broad guidelines with managerial freedom; tolerance for failure |
| Compensation | Rewards for **verified cost reduction** | **Balanced scorecard** across multiple dimensions; rewards for creative flair |
| Culture | Thrift, waste elimination | Experimentation, innovation |

Trying to do both means the **activities conflict** - the tight control that produces cost discipline is exactly what suppresses the experimentation differentiation requires. The failure is one of **fit** among activities (§0: strategy is a *system* of mutually reinforcing activities), not of budget.

### The two rebuttals (know both - the exam may test the modern view)

1. **Differentiation -> share -> cost reduction.** A widely adopted differentiated product gains volume; volume drives scale and learning economies; costs fall. **McDonald's** differentiated on cleanliness/consistency, became the share leader, and is now also a cost leader.
2. **The contradiction can be managed directly.** An MIT study of ~70 auto plants found six with simultaneously very low costs *and* very high quality - achieved through best-available manufacturing technology **plus** participative management (quality circles, team production, TQM) **plus** high employee loyalty. Those relations are **socially complex** and therefore hard to imitate - a source of sustained advantage in their own right.

**Porter has softened:** low-cost firms still need **competitive** (not leading) levels of differentiation to survive, and vice versa.

### Blue Ocean / Value Innovation

- **Blue ocean strategy** - a hybrid pursuing **differentiation (V up) and low cost (C down) simultaneously** through **value innovation**. Blue oceans = untapped market space, new demand, profitable growth. **Red oceans** = known market space of existing industries.
- **The four actions:** **Eliminate** (which factors should be eliminated?) · **Reduce** · **Raise** · **Create**.
- **IKEA:** *eliminated* salespeople, high-rent small retail shops, after-sales service; *reduced* staffing, delivery, assembly; *raised* the number of home-furnishing items offered; *created* a new way to shop for furniture.
- Other examples: **Trader Joe's**, **Cirque du Soleil**.
- **Strategy canvas** - graphs a company's performance relative to competitors across the industry's factors of competition; reveals **focus or divergence** (JetBlue vs. low-cost airlines vs. legacy carriers).
- Value innovation is **difficult to succeed at** - the slides pose it explicitly against "stuck in the middle."

### Confusion Pair

| Torn between | Ask only this |
|---|---|
| **Stuck in the middle** vs. **blue ocean / value innovation** | Is the firm **failing at both** because its activities conflict and it has no coherent position (stuck), or has it **redefined the factors of competition** - eliminating/reducing some to fund raising/creating others (value innovation)? Blue ocean is a *deliberate reconfiguration*, not a compromise. |

## §9.5 Corporate Strategy Foundations (Modules 9–12 deck)

> [!note]- Where else this lives
> **Deep dive:** [[Vertical Integration]] · [[Vertical Integration - Full Depth]]


> Off-Flashlight but on the slides, and it frames §10–§12. The deck opens corporate strategy as *"the decisions leaders make on where to compete."*

### Three dimensions of firm boundaries

1. **Vertical integration** along the value chain (§10)
2. **Diversification** of products and services (§11)
3. **Geographic scope** (§App. A)

**Underlying concepts that guide all three:** core competencies · economies of scale · **economies of scope** · **transaction costs** (the cost-effectiveness of vertical integration vs. diversification).

### Why firms need to grow - five named reasons

| # | Reason | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **To increase profits and shareholder returns** | Reinvest in the business; higher wages/benefits as motivation. *If they don't, there may be a hostile takeover* |
| 2 | **To lower costs and achieve economies of scale** | |
| 3 | **To increase market power** | |
| 4 | **To reduce risk through diversification** | Especially if one SBU isn't performing |
| 5 | **To motivate management** | To avoid the **principal–agent problem** |

> Note the tension the course sets up deliberately: reasons 4 and 5 are the same motives §11 shows to be **empirically weak or actively against shareholders' interests**. "Why firms grow" ≠ "why growth creates value."

### Transaction costs - internal vs. external

**Intellectual roots:** **Ronald Coase** (property rights, transaction costs, government inefficiencies) and **Douglas North** (New Institutional Economics - what institutions are).

| | Examples | What drives them |
|---|---|---|
| **External transaction costs** | Searching for contractors · negotiating, monitoring and enforcing contracts | Largely **environment-dependent** - a product of where the firm operates |
| **Internal transaction costs** | Recruiting and retaining employees · setting up a shop floor | Typically **rise with firm size** - firm-dependent |

**The make-or-buy rule stated plainly:**
- If **cost(in-house) < cost(market)** -> **vertically integrate** - own production of inputs, or own output distribution channels
- If **cost(market) < cost(in-house)** -> **buy**

### The two disadvantages - one for each mode of organizing

| Organizing **within firms** | Organizing **through the market** |
|---|---|
| **Principal–agent problem** - the "separation of ownership and control." The **principal** (owner) wants shareholder value; the **agent** (manager/employee) should act on their behalf but pursues **their own interests** (corporate jets, golf outings) | **Information asymmetry** - one party (usually the **seller**) is better informed because it holds private information. Can crowd desirable goods/services out with inferior ones, because the seller has information buyers don't. *Example: partnering with a local firm for manufacturing* |

> This pairing is the cleanest one-line answer to "why isn't everything a firm / why isn't everything a market?" **Each mode has its own characteristic failure.**

### Two named alternatives to vertical integration

| Alternative | Definition | Benefits / examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Taper integration** | Backward or forward integrated **plus** reliance on outside suppliers or distributors | **Exposes in-house suppliers/distributors to market competition** · enhances flexibility · knowledge recombination. Apple and Nike run their own stores *and* sell through Best Buy / Dick's |
| **Strategic outsourcing** | Moving internal value-chain activities to other firms - typically **"non-core"** activities (which is *why* you must first identify your valuable core competencies) | Outsourcing and offshoring: banking, IT, healthcare, HR services (e.g. Infosys in Bangalore for IT) |

### The make-or-buy continuum - with the deck's own examples

| Form | Characteristics | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Short-term contracts** | **Less than 1 year** · **no guarantee of quality** · bidding for contracts | GM used short-term contracts for standard car components and **lost to Toyota over quality issues** |
| **Long-term contracts / licensing / franchising** | **Longer than 1 year** to overcome quality issues and build trust | Licensing in biotech · franchising: McDonald's, Subway |
| **Equity alliance** | One partner takes **partial ownership** of another · greater commitment · **"try before you buy"** | **Coca-Cola holds 18.5% equity of Monster** |
| **Joint venture** | **Two or more partners create a new organization** | **Hulu** - NBC, Disney and Providence Equity |
| **Full ownership (parent company)** | Parent **owns and has control (fiat)** | GM owned Opel and Vauxhall - but **eventually divested** |

### Build–Borrow–or–Buy (Capron & Mitchell)

The framework for **closing a resource gap**. Work the questions in order; **acquisition is the last resort**.

| Question | Test | If yes -> |
|---|---|---|
| **Relevance** | Are internal resources **similar** to what the firm needs to develop, **and superior** to competitors' in the target area? *(Are a golf course's resources the same as Topgolf's?)* | **BUILD** internally |
| **Tradability** | Can a **contract** transfer ownership or allow use of the resource? | **BORROW** - licensing, franchising, contracts (Subway, McDonald's) |
| **Closeness** | Is close coordination needed, achievable through **equity alliances or joint ventures**? | **BORROW with equity** |
| **Integration** | Only when **low relevance + low tradability + high need for closeness** | **BUY** - M&A is complex and costly; consider other options first |

**Post-integration failures abound** - the deck's example is **Microsoft–Nokia** (was it cultural? US vs. Finnish).

## §10. Vertical Integration & Transaction Costs

> [!note]- Where else this lives
> **Deep dive:** [[Vertical Integration]] · [[Vertical Integration - Full Depth]]
> **Articles in play:** [[#Nvidia Steps Back|Nvidia Steps Back]] · [[#Solar-Powered Cars|Solar-Powered Cars]]


### Core Idea

- Corporate strategy sets **scope** - vertical, product, geographic - analyzed with one toolkit: economies of scope, transaction costs, and the costs of corporate complexity.
- **The one rule:** a firm's boundary is set by **relative cost**. Internalize an activity when the **transaction costs** of buying it exceed the **administrative costs** of running it inside; otherwise buy. **Both sides have costs** - never argue "markets have costs, therefore integrate."
- Make-or-buy is **not binary**. Between spot markets and full ownership sits a continuum: long-term contracts, franchising, vendor partnerships, alliances, JVs, minority equity.
- The answer is always "it depends" - and it depends on **firm-specific** resources and strategy, not just industry conditions.

### 10.1 Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Market mechanism** | Coordination by independent decisions guided by prices - Adam Smith's *invisible hand* |
| **Administrative mechanism** | Coordination by managerial decision through hierarchy - Chandler's *visible hand* |
| **Transaction costs** | The costs of **using the market** (not the purchase price): **search, negotiation/contracting, monitoring, enforcement** |
| **Administrative costs** | The costs of organizing the same activity **inside** a hierarchy |
| **Relative cost rule (Coase)** | Internalize when market transaction costs exceed internal administrative costs |
| **Vertical integration** | Ownership of vertically related activities; measured as **value added ÷ sales revenue** |
| **Backward / forward integration** | Upstream (own your inputs) / downstream (activities previously done by customers) |
| **Full / partial integration** | All of a stage's output transferred internally / the firm is a net buyer or seller of the intermediate good |
| **Taper integration** | Deliberately mixing in-house supply with outside suppliers - keeps internal units exposed to competition, adds flexibility |
| **Technical economies** | Cost savings from physically integrating processes at one site - these justify **co-location, NOT common ownership** |
| **Virtual / hollow corporation** | A firm coordinating a supplier network / the failure state where it has outsourced so much it can no longer adapt |
| **Architectural vs. component capabilities** | Ability to design/coordinate the **whole system** vs. deep expertise in **one part** - the test for whether a heavy outsourcer has hollowed out |

### 10.2 The Transaction-Cost Trigger - the causal chain to memorize

**Technical economies force plants next to each other** (steel -> steel strip: hot-roll while still molten) -> **purpose-built, co-located assets** -> **transaction-specific investment** (worthless outside this relationship) -> **bilateral monopoly** (one buyer, one seller - no equilibrium price, only bargaining power) -> **opportunism / hold-up** -> market contracting becomes inefficient -> **integrate**.

**Two traps built into that chain:**
1. **Technical economies justify co-location, not common ownership.** Ownership is answered only by transaction costs.
2. **"Just write a better contract" fails** - the future is uncertain, so **contracts are inevitably incomplete** (bounded rationality, Simon; opportunism, Williamson).

**Commodity inputs with thick markets get bought, not made** (gold for jewelers, wheat for millers, steel strip for can-makers like Crown and Ball).

### 10.3 Asset Specificity + Transaction Frequency - the Flashlight's exact ask

> [!important] The Flashlight's exact ask
> *"Know how **asset specificity** and **transaction frequency** together determine make-vs-buy."* Neither one alone is sufficient - it is the **combination** that signals vertical market failure. High specificity with rare transactions gets a detailed one-off contract; frequent transactions with low specificity get standardized market purchasing. Only **both together** justify ownership.

**Three types of asset specificity:**
- **Site specificity** - co-located assets
- **Technical/physical specificity** - equipment usable only by one counterparty
- **Human-capital specificity** - skills tied to one relationship

Plus **asset intensity** (magnifies the *cost* of disruption) and **asset durability** (extends the *time horizon* of lock-in risk).

**The pre/post-investment flip - the key mechanism:** before assets are committed, many parties bid competitively; *after* the mine and refinery are built, specificity locks the pair into a bilateral monopoly. **Vertical market failure is created by the investment itself, not by the initial market structure.** (Bauxite: costly to ship, refining strips 60–70% of volume, refineries tuned to their own bauxite's chemistry -> ~90% of bauxite moves under VI or JV.)

**Figure 2 - the coordination-mechanism map. Integration is warranted in EXACTLY ONE cell:**

| | **Low asset specificity** | **High asset specificity** |
|---|---|---|
| **Seldom transacted** | Standardized contracts (office lease, credit sale) | Detailed one-off contract (major public construction) |
| **Often transacted** | Standardized transactions (groceries) | **VERTICAL INTEGRATION** (bauxite, specialized auto components) |

**Figure 1 - market structure (sellers × buyers):** the high-trading-risk zone is the bilateral monopoly/oligopoly corner. The edges are **power asymmetry** problems (a different rationale), not market failure.

| | One buyer | Few buyers | Many buyers |
|---|---|---|---|
| **One seller** | High trading risk | High trading risk | Sellers dominate |
| **Few sellers** | High trading risk | High trading risk | Sellers dominate |
| **Many sellers** | Buyers dominate | Buyers dominate | No one dominates |

### 10.4 The Six (Seven) Administrative Costs - why NOT to integrate

| Administrative cost | Mechanism | Anchor example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Differences in optimal scale** | Producing below MES costs more than the transaction costs avoided | **FedEx** buys 40,000 vans/yr vs. a 200,000 assembly MES -> buy. It's the **gap**, not absolute size |
| **2. Developing distinctive capabilities** | A specialist serving many customers learns faster than a captive unit | Xerox/Kodak/Philips can't match EDS/IBM/Accenture in IT. **Counter-case (linked capabilities):** IBM mainframes, Walmart IT/logistics |
| **3. Strategic dissimilarity** | Different systems, planning, control, HR, top-management skills | Manufacturing + retailing integration is rare; drove de-integration at Marriott, Whitbread, Scottish & Newcastle |
| **4. Incentive problem** | Market interfaces have **high-powered** incentives; internal transfers are **low-powered** (consequences slow and undramatic) | Partial fix: **shared-service organizations** forced to compete with outside providers |
| **5. Competitive effects** | Independent suppliers/customers won't deal with a firm they now see as a competitor (monopoly-extension/foreclosure is rare - Standard Oil, Alcoa) | After Disney bought ABC, rival studios (DreamWorks) cooled on developing shows with ABC |
| **6. Flexibility (cuts both ways)** | **Markets** win for demand uncertainty and new technical combinations; **VI** wins for system-wide simultaneous adjustment | Markets: construction; contract-made iPod/Xbox/Dell. VI: Zara, American Apparel |
| **7. Compounding risk** | A failure at any owned stage halts profit at every other stage | The 1998 GM brake-plant strike halted all 24 US assembly plants |

**Decision criteria (Grant Table 13.1)**

| Favors **vertical integration** | Favors **market / outsourcing** |
|---|---|
| Few firms in the adjacent stage | Dissimilar optimal scale between stages |
| Transaction-specific investments needed | High need for continual capability investment |
| Large information asymmetries | High need for entrepreneurial flexibility |
| Taxed/regulated intermediate transactions | Highly uncertain demand |
| High uncertainty over the relationship's life | |
| Strategically **similar** stages | |

### 10.5 Stuckey & White - the four legitimate reasons (and the spurious ones)

**Governing rule: do NOT vertically integrate unless it is absolutely necessary to create or protect value.** The burden of proof is on integration. VI is expensive, hard to reverse, and management's track record is poor.

| # | Reason | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Vertical market failure** - trading is too risky **and** contracts can't fix it | **The big one.** Requires all three structural preconditions: small numbers of buyers/sellers · high asset specificity/durability/intensity · frequent transactions. Amplified by uncertainty, bounded rationality, opportunism |
| **2** | **Defending against market power** in an adjacent stage | Two traps: **paying away the surplus** in the acquisition premium (an Australian ready-mix player paid ~20× cash flow against a ~10% cost of capital), and assuming an **insider advantage** that doesn't exist |
| **3** | **Creating/exploiting market power** | **Entry barriers** (integrated incumbents force entrants into *every* stage - upstream aluminum: 6 majors, thin intermediate markets, ~$2B efficient-scale entry) and **price discrimination** (forward-integrate into the price-sensitive segment to kill resale - aluminum into can stock, cable, auto castings) |
| **4** | **Industry life cycle** | *Young:* forward-integrate to **build a market** that doesn't exist yet - works only if you block **free riders** (proprietary tech/brand); the authors call this **overrated**. *Declining:* fill gaps as independents exit (Culbro, cigars) |

**Information/R&D market failure:** markets for information fail - the buyer can't value it before seeing it, and the seller can't reveal it without giving it away. **EMI/CAT scanner:** EMI invented it but lacked distribution and service assets; GE and Siemens, already integrated, reverse-engineered it, supported it better, and took the market.

**Spurious reasons (trap answers):**

| Bad justification | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| **"It smooths earnings"** | Contiguous stages are **positively correlated** (same end demand - zinc mining + smelting), and shareholders can diversify themselves. Smoothing benefits **managers**, not shareholders |
| **"It assures supply/outlets"** | Valid **only** where foreclosure or unfair pricing is a symptom of real market failure or structural power. In an efficient market you can always trade at the market price; owning it just tempts you to mis-set transfer prices. **Test: are both sides competitively structured?** If yes, integration adds nothing |
| **"Capture more value / get closer to the customer"** | The correlation of profitability with **value added** or customer proximity is weak and inconsistent. What matters is **economic surplus** (return above full costs including a fair return on capital). In the Australian cement/concrete chain, **transport** is the biggest value-added stage and doesn't cover its cost of capital, while **fly ash** is the smallest and earns high surplus. **Prescription: integrate into the highest-*surplus* stages** |

**Quasi-integration - usually the right answer.** Long-term contracts, JVs/alliances, licensing, **asset ownership** (own only the critical specific assets - tools, dies, jigs, molds - and contract out the rest), **franchising** (control distribution without the capital; the right to cancel enforces quality). Most upstream aluminum plants are now JVs. Licensing is the default for technology **unless** the innovation needs specialized complementary assets whose owners hold power - then integrate (the EMI mistake).

**Structure change forces strategy change.** Oil, 1966 -> 1985: top-4 seller share fell 59% -> 26%; refineries went from crude-specific to flexible; coordination shifted from ~all VI/10-year contracts to ~50% spot with VI declining. But **don't disintegrate on fashion** - genuinely market-failure-critical activities should stay in, and "cooperative" sole suppliers rediscover their muscle once rivals are gone.

**The decision tree (Figure 6), built so full integration is the last box:** Is the stage attractive to you (attractive industry + skills match)? -> if no, is it subject to **vertical market failure**? -> if no VMF, does owning it help **defend/build/exploit market power**? -> if no, **exit the stage**. On every "yes" branch, first ask **would quasi-integration suffice?**

### 10.6 The single most testable point

**Even in the same industry, firms rationally integrate to opposite degrees**, because they hold different resources and pursue different strategies:

- **Zara** far more than H&M/Gap · **Armani** more than Donna Karan · **Starbucks** owns its outlets while peers franchise · **Capital One** keeps IT in-house while most banks outsource · **Toyota/Nissan** produced ~20–23% of car value in-house vs. Ford ~50% and GM ~70%, yet got closer supplier collaboration through **vendor partnerships**.

**Any question implying there is one "correct" level of integration for an industry is testing whether you know that industry conditions *underdetermine* the answer.**

**Zara - the case where integration wins despite paying every administrative cost:** 68% of Inditex sales; design->store in **3 weeks** vs. a 6-month industry lag; ~40,000 designs/yr, ~¼ produced, ~half made in-house; Comditex supplies 40% of fabric **mostly undyed** so color can change late; stores order/receive twice weekly, dispatched in 8 hours; items ≤2 weeks in store. Integration wins because the coordination benefit (**speed**) outweighs the scale/dissimilarity/incentive/risk penalties - **in this industry, for this strategy**.

### Confusion Pairs

| Torn between | Ask only this |
|---|---|
| **Market failure** vs. **strategy/scale** (the Flashlight's exact ask) | Is the firm integrating because **trading is unsafe and contracts can't fix it** (specific assets + frequency + small numbers -> hold-up), or because it wants **scale, cost, market power, or capability**? Only the first is transaction-cost-driven vertical market failure. |
| **Co-location** vs. **ownership** | Technical economies force **physical proximity**. Ownership is answered separately, by transaction costs. Steel/strip co-located **and** integrated (specific assets, one counterparty); HealthTrackRx moved next to UPS on a **lease**; Guardian invited its rim supplier to build nearby. |
| **Vertical integration** vs. **supplier switching** | Does anything **cross the firm's boundary**? Replacing Intel with MediaTek is switching, not integration. The cheapest answer to supplier power is usually a **credible second source**, not ownership. |
| **Vertical integration** vs. **diversification** | Are you moving **along your own value chain** (vertical) or into a **different business/industry** (diversification)? |
| **Vertical integration** vs. **economies of scope / capability externalization** | Selling your internal capability to outsiders (Amazon's retail ad service, AWS pattern) is **scope expansion**, not integration - you haven't bought a value-chain stage. |
| **Acquisition** vs. **equity alliance** | Does **control** transfer? A deliberate sub-50% stake (Meta/Scale AI at 49%) is an equity alliance - "try before you buy" - not an acquisition. |
| **Which uncertainty?** | **Price** uncertainty in a liquid market -> contracts and derivatives (Starbucks buys ~3% of world coffee and owns **no farms**). **Technology/demand** uncertainty over a long horizon -> ownership (CoreWeave's 12-year lease converting toward acquisition). |
| **MES gap** vs. **strategic importance** | The recurring binding constraint is **scale**, not importance. Amazon integrates chip **design** but outsources **fabrication** to TSMC - fab MES is beyond even Amazon's volume. |

## §11. Diversification

> [!note]- Where else this lives
> **Deep dive:** [[Diversification]]
> **Articles in play:** [[#Universal Epic Universe|Universal Epic Universe]] · [[#Amazon Big-Ticket|Amazon Big-Ticket]] · [[#Volvo EVs|Volvo EVs]]


### Core Idea

- **Corporate diversification** = operating in **multiple businesses/industries** - *not* acquiring in the same industry, and generally *not* vertical integration.
- It creates value only when **both** conditions hold: (1) a real **economy of scope** exists, **and** (2) realizing it through **hierarchical governance** (inside the firm) is cheaper than through markets, alliances, **or outside investors simply holding a diversified portfolio**.
- **Being big ≠ being diversified.** Many big firms are single-business firms (Target, Costco). Test with SIC/NAICS codes or the **segments/operating segments** in the 10-K.
- Why it happens: the home industry matures and growth slows; opportunities look better elsewhere.

### 11.0 Three types of diversification (slides - by *what* is being varied)

Before the relatedness spectrum, the deck names three types by **dimension**:

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Product diversification** | Increase in the **variety of products/services**; active in several product markets |
| **Geographic diversification** | Increase in the variety of **markets / geographic regions** - regional, national, or international |
| **Product–market diversification** | **Both** product and geographic diversification |

> [!warning] The axis switch
> **Rumelt's model applies to product markets, not geographic ones.** Types 1 and 2 are defined by **% revenue from the dominant/primary business**; Types 3 and 4 by the **relationship of core competencies across business units**. That is the axis switch worth remembering: single/dominant = a *revenue* test; related/unrelated = a *competency* test.

### 11.1 Types (the relatedness spectrum)

| Type | Threshold |
|---|---|
| **Limited (single-business)** | **95%+** of revenue from one business |
| **Limited (dominant-business)** | **70–95%** from one business |
| **Related-constrained** | <70% from one business, and **all** businesses share numerous common inputs, technologies, channels, or customers |
| **Related-linked** | <70%, but businesses connect through only a **few** links, or different pairs share **different kinds** of links |
| **Unrelated** | <70%, and businesses share **few or no** common attributes |

**Subtlety:** a firm that looks unrelated on activity-sharing grounds can still be **related** if a genuine **core competence** links it. **Hanson Trust's** businesses shared almost no activities, but its disciplined acquisition-and-management playbook for mature, low-tech businesses *is* the common thread -> related-constrained, not unrelated.

### 11.2 The Four Motivation Families - by name (the Flashlight's ask)

#### 1. Operational economies of scope

**Economy of scope** = the combined value of businesses run together exceeds the sum of their values run independently. Formal test: **NPV(all together) > Σ NPV(each alone)**. Cost: A+B together < A+B apart; Value: A+B together > A+B apart.

| Mechanism | How it creates value | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Shared activities** | Value-chain activities run jointly (R&D, manufacturing, distribution, sales, service) -> cost reduction **or** revenue enhancement (bundling, borrowed reputation) | Hard to manage across businesses; standardization can hurt each business's ability to serve its own customers; a shared activity transmits a **poor** reputation as readily as a good one. **Sharing overhead should not be the basis for diversification** |
| **Core competencies (transferring skills)** | Intangible knowledge/experience transferred across businesses even without shared physical activities | Must pass **three tests**: (1) similarities meaningful enough that sharing expertise is real, not superficial; (2) the transfer involves activities **important to competitive advantage**, not peripheral ones; (3) the skills are **advanced/proprietary enough to be beyond competitors' capabilities**. Two systematic miscalculations: **over-estimating transferability** of specific resources, and **over-estimating the value of very general resources** in a new market |

Related terms: **invented competencies** (illusory, after-the-fact rationalizations for unrelated moves) · **dominant logic** (a firm-wide way of thinking that can obscure how intangible a core competence really is).

#### 2. Financial economies of scope

| Mechanism | Theory | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal capital allocation** | Diversified-firm managers have better information about their own businesses than external capital markets | Higher-quality information isn't guaranteed - managers **inflate** their division's prospects; requests get discounted internally to compensate; **escalation of commitment** keeps funding losers; efficiency degrades further under unrelated diversification |
| **Risk reduction** | Imperfectly correlated cash flows lower the variability of firm cash flows | **Doesn't directly benefit outside equity holders** - they can diversify a portfolio at near-zero cost, far cheaper than the firm can do M&A. Indirect benefit: it makes employees, suppliers and customers more willing to make **firm-specific investments** (which they *can't* diversify away), which benefits equity/debt holders indirectly |
| **Tax advantages** | Offset losses against profits; modestly increased debt capacity | Empirically small; firms have many other ways to reduce tax burden |

#### 3. Anti-competitive economies of scope

| Mechanism | Logic |
|---|---|
| **Multipoint competition -> mutual forbearance** | Firms meeting in several markets tacitly avoid aggressive competition in one for fear of **retaliation in another**. Requires large enough stakes and strong strategic linkage - really a **related**-diversification phenomenon. Michelin/Goodyear; Maxwell House/Folger's; Caterpillar/John Deere; BIC/Gillette; America West–Continental (Houston–Phoenix fares, 1989). **Not guaranteed:** the Disney / AOL–Time Warner advertising dispute escalated instead |
| **Deep pockets / exploiting market power** | Monopoly profits in one business **cross-subsidize predatory pricing** in another to drive out competitors, then extract monopoly profits there too. Raised at the AT&T breakup. Slide examples: Microsoft (Bing, mobile OS, Azure, Teams vs. Slack); Google (Google+, cloud) |

Empirical check: research on regulated utilities diversifying in the 1980s found **not** unfair cross-subsidization but that **poor management skills carried over**, making diversification *less* profitable.

#### 4. Managerial / employee motivations

- Top-management compensation historically tracks **firm size (sales)**, not profitability - a consistent finding in finance and economics. That's an incentive to grow via diversification (especially **unrelated** diversification through M&A, which grows sales fastest) regardless of value creation.
- **Agency theory:** managers (agents) act in their own interest rather than owners'. Senior managers only need to worry about profitability if it falls far enough to invite a hostile takeover or board action.
- This motivation is **inconsistent with equity holders' interests by construction** - an agency problem, not an economy of scope.

### 11.3 The lecture's scorecard - theory vs. empirics

| Motivation | Theoretically sound? | Empirically supported? |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Operational economies of scope | ✔ (the strongest) | **✘** |
| 2. Financial motivations | **✘** | **✘** |
| 3. Anti-competitive motivations | ✔ | **✘** |
| 4. Managerial motivations | (works for managers, not owners) | - |

**The lecture's blunt verdict: meaningful operational economies of scope are "REALLY HARD to achieve."** Why: diversification usually happens through **big M&A**, so you **pay out all future economies of scope in the acquisition premium**, and integration is more complex and slower than planned. And a CEO may diversify not only to raise pay but **to keep the job** - top-line growth matters to investors and boards.

### 11.4 Motivation -> type mapping (and why it matters)

| Motivation | Related | Unrelated |
|---|---|---|
| Shared activities | ✔ | |
| Core competencies | ✔ | |
| Internal capital allocation | ✔ | |
| Risk reduction | ✔ | ✔ |
| Tax advantages | ✔ | ✔ |
| Multipoint competition | ✔ | |
| Exploiting market power | ✔ | ✔ |
| Firm size / employee compensation | ✔ | ✔ |

**Implication:** unrelated diversification can draw on only **4 of 8** motivations - and of those, employee compensation actively works *against* equity holders while tax and market-power gains are usually small. **This is the core textbook argument for why related should outperform unrelated.**

### 11.5 Empirical evidence - the four-step story

1. **Rumelt (1974, 1982):** related diversifiers outperform unrelated - **but focused firms outperform all diversified types**.
2. **Diversified firms vs. portfolios of focused firms:** diversified firms underperform; one study put the **diversification discount** at ~24% less value.
3. **Selection-effect challenge:** maybe low performers *choose* to diversify. Multi-stage techniques controlling for this found no negative correlation, and some found a small premium.
4. **Most recent correction:** that second wave omitted an alternative - **returning free cash flow to shareholders**. Once dividends/buybacks are modeled, the discount reappears.

**Ranking, on average:** (1) **return cash to shareholders** - best for shareholder wealth · (2) diversify **and** return cash - small positive · (3) diversify **without** returning cash - **destroys** shareholder wealth.

**Three generic diversification–performance model shapes:**

| Shape | Rationale | Support |
|---|---|---|
| **Value-enhancing (monotonic up)** | Market power, scale/scope from multiple-use resources, capital-allocation advantages, risk reduction | Some individual studies |
| **Inverted-U** | Synergies exploitable only to a point; advantage largely restricted to **related** diversification; the less related, the more costs outweigh benefits | **Largest and most cited body of support** (Rumelt; Grant, Jammine & Thomas; Hoskisson & Hitt; Palich et al.) |
| **Value-destroying (monotonic down)** | Influence costs from internal power struggles; inefficient internal capital markets; agency-driven overexpansion | Berger & Ofek; Rajan et al.; Maksimovic & Phillips |

**Class synthesis - "where are we now":** Related > Unrelated traditionally -> newer econometrics narrow it to **Related ≈ Unrelated** -> and across the board, **Focus > Diversifiers**.

### 11.6 Sustained advantage from diversification

- **Rarity** isn't about whether the firm is diversified (nearly all large firms are) - it's about **how many competitors are exploiting the same economy of scope**.
- **Direct duplication is EASY for:** shared activities, risk reduction, tax advantages, employee-compensation-driven growth (tangible or widely available levers).
- **Direct duplication is HARD for:** core competencies (intangible), internal capital allocation (requires hard-to-build information-processing capability), multipoint competition (socially complex coordination), market power (you must already have it somewhere).
- **Substitution:** grow each business independently large enough to capture the same scale advantages alone (**Nestlé**: minimal cross-business activity sharing, each country operation scaled on its own), or use a **strategic alliance** instead of ownership. Alliances are a *poor* substitute precisely when the economy of scope requires **high transaction-specific investment** - which is the case where ownership was the only route anyway.

### Confusion Pairs

| Torn between | Ask only this |
|---|---|
| **Economies of scope** vs. **economies of scale** | Cost advantage from applying **one resource across several products/markets** (scope) vs. from **volume of the same thing** (scale). |
| **Diversification** vs. **vertical integration** | A **new business/industry** vs. a **stage of your own value chain**. |
| **Diversification** vs. **product-line extension** | Is it a genuinely different **industry** (check segments/SIC), or a broader range within the same one? Canada Goose adding T-shirts is line broadening, not corporate diversification - but the *shared-activity logic* still applies. |
| **Related-constrained** vs. **related-linked** | Do **all** businesses share **numerous** common links (constrained), or do only some pairs share **different kinds** of links (linked)? |
| **Unrelated** vs. **related via core competence** | Ask whether an **intangible competence** (not shared activities) runs through all of them - Hanson Trust looks unrelated but isn't. |
| **Shared activities** vs. **core competence** | **Tangible** activities run jointly (a common sales force, plant, R&D lab) vs. **intangible** know-how transferred with no shared activity. Shared activities are **easy** to duplicate; core competencies are **hard**. |
| **Risk reduction** vs. **internal capital allocation** | Smoothing **cash-flow variability** vs. **allocating capital** better than external markets can. |
| **Risk reduction** vs. **real options** | Portfolio smoothing across businesses vs. preserving **flexibility on one uncertain opportunity**. |
| **Market power** vs. **mutual forbearance** | **Aggressive** - cross-subsidized predatory pricing to drive rivals out (deep pockets) vs. **passive** - tacitly *not* competing hard because of multimarket retaliation risk. |
| **Why most diversification fails** | Answer the Flashlight's version: the rationales are real in theory, but (a) financial and anti-competitive motives are empirically weak, (b) outside investors can replicate the financial ones for free, (c) **the premium paid in the acquisition captures the future economies of scope**, and (d) integration is slower and harder than planned. Absent a compelling economy of scope, **returning cash to shareholders creates more value**. |

### Named Examples

**Textbook:** 3M (substrates/coatings/adhesives -> invisible tape, Post-its) · Honda (engines) · Hanson Trust · Texas Instruments and P&G (shared R&D/manufacturing/grocery distribution) · **KFC under PepsiCo** and **Levi Strauss** (failures to exploit shared activities - redundant regional quality programs; six duplicate order-processing systems) · **PepsiCo -> Pizza Hut/Taco Bell/KFC** (attempted marketing core-competence transfer, only moderate success, spun off) · Kodak digital imaging, PolyGram movies (illusory competence transfer) · ABB and CIBA-Geigy (deliberately restrict activity sharing) · Nestlé (substitution).

**Recent cases:** UPS Healthcare (strong related-constrained: shared hub/logistics infrastructure, higher-margin specialized service) · SharkNinja (core competence in fast R&D/consumer-insight cycles across many appliance categories) · Boeing space divestiture · Medtronic diabetes separation · De Beers closing Lightbox · Apple's cancelled car · Capri Holdings (a **shared** repositioning across Michael Kors and Versace backfired for both - shared strategy transmits failure as well as success).

## §12. Mergers & Acquisitions

> [!note]- Where else this lives
> **Deep dive:** [[M&A]]
> **Articles in play:** [[#$200 Billion Railroad|$200 Billion Railroad]]


### Core Idea

- **>$2 trillion** spent on acquisitions a year; failure rate **70–90%**.
- Root cause: executives **mismatch the target to the strategic purpose** of the deal -> they pay the wrong price and integrate the wrong way.
- Two lenses stack: **(A) the seven synergy types** - which motivation is actually in play, and whether it can build advantage at all; **(B) LBM vs. RBM** - are you buying **resources** or a **business model**?
- A third factor neither framework names but that shows up constantly in real deals: **cultural/organizational fit**.

### 12.1 The Seven Types of Synergies - by name (memorize the split)

| # | Synergy | Mechanism | Builds advantage? | Key risk / failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Cost savings** | Eliminate redundant jobs, facilities, admin functions. Largest **within the same industry**. Called **"hard synergies"** - "hard" means **high certainty**, not difficulty | **Yes - most certain** | Cost categories defined differently across firms · costs sit in different org locations than expected · **eliminating 200 positions ≠ 200 salaries** (people move divisions) · **takes too long** (insufficiently detailed plans, resistance, delayed tough calls) |
| 2 | **Revenue enhancements** | Cross-sell/bundle through a shared channel, sales force or facility -> "one-stop shopping" | **Rarely** - routinely overestimated, harder to estimate, more often falls short | Only works if customers need both products **at the same time and in the same place**; rivals **poach** customers during integration chaos |
| 3 | **Transferring skills or technology** | Plug the target's tech and engineers into your product development; best practices flow **either direction**; buy components compatible with your own products | **Yes**, where customers pay for the added functionality | Usually only works for **small, tightly scoped** tech acquisitions |
| 4 | **Market power** | Within-industry consolidation -> push through price increases; also increases **bargaining power over suppliers** | **Yes - but only in oligopolistic/concentrated industries**, never fragmented ones | **Regulators** are the binding constraint - and the **definition of "industry"** is contested |
| 5 | **Maintain competitive parity** | Match a rival's acquisition so you don't fall behind | **No** - restores parity by definition | Purely reactive; not advantage-seeking |
| 6 | **Tactical / defensive** | Keep a resource/technology **from rivals**; prevent rivals from attacking you (patent wars) | **Sometimes** - the "*not always*" item | Often looks **financially irrational** on its face - the lecture's verdict is blunt: "financially, do not make sense" |
| 7 | **Financial engineering (incl. tax)** | Lower WACC, refinance the target's debt at your better rate, pool working capital, **tax inversion** | **No** - yields **parity**, since competitors imitate | Should **never** be the sole rationale for a deal |

**The single most quotable split (the closing slide):**

| CAN lead to competitive advantage | Do NOT lead to competitive advantage |
|---|---|
| Cost savings | Financial engineering (pooled working capital, tax benefits) |
| Revenue enhancements | Maintaining competitive parity |
| Transferring skills or technology | Managerial desire to increase pay |
| Market power | |
| **Tactical - *"not always"*** | |

> [!warning] The trick item
> **Tactical/defensive is the trick item.** It sits on the **CAN** side, qualified with *"not always,"* while the lecture also says these deals "financially do not make sense." **Both are true at once** - don't let one talk you out of the other.

### 12.2 Deal Taxonomy - including the Flashlight's "buying distressed value"

| Type | Definition | Tell |
|---|---|---|
| **Horizontal** | Direct competitors combine | "rival," same industry |
| **Vertical** | Buying your own supplier or customer | your own value chain |
| **Conglomerate** | Unrelated industries; portfolio diversification | no operating link |
| **Merger of equals** | Both healthy and comparable | rare in practice; often just a label |
| **Rescue / distressed** *(the Flashlight's "buying distressed value")* | A strong firm bails out a weak one | **Market tell: target's stock up, acquirer's down** - the market says the acquirer is overpaying |
| **Bolt-on / tuck-in** | Small; plugs one capability into an existing line | size relative to the acquirer |
| **Transformative megadeal** | Reshapes the company; high variance | "bet the company" |
| **Strategic alliance / JV / consortium** | Cooperation **without** transfer of ownership or control - an **alternative to M&A** | no stake changes hands |
| **LBO** | Value comes from leverage + tax shield | "more a stock purchase than a strategic acquisition" |
| **Divestiture** | The undo button - the tell that a past deal failed | spinoff/sale |

### 12.3 The New M&A Playbook - LBM vs. RBM (Christensen et al., HBR 2011)

**Business model = four interdependent elements:** customer value proposition · profit formula (revenue model + cost structure) · **resources** · processes.

**Only resources can be extracted and plugged in**, because resources exist *apart* from the firm and survive its dissolution. **Profit formulas and processes cannot be transplanted** - they are inseparable from the organization that created them.

| | **LBM - "Leverage My Business Model"** | **RBM - "Reinvent My Business Model"** |
|---|---|---|
| You're buying | **Resources** (people, tech, customers, facilities, cash) | The **whole business model** |
| Purpose | Boost **current performance** - premium price or lower cost | **Transformative long-term growth** |
| Integration | **Dissolve** the acquired model; fold resources in; let the rest die | **Keep it intact - operate it separately** |
| Pricing error | Acquirers **overpay** (unrealistic about the size of the boost) | Acquirers **underpay** or walk away (same-industry comparables make it look expensive) |
| Right comparables | Same industry, similar products | **Other disruptors, regardless of industry** |
| Share-price effect | Priced in within ~**1 year**; best case a one-time jump to a new plateau, then growth ≈ cost of capital (~8%) | Can persistently beat expectations |

**When an LBM resource acquisition actually lowers costs:**

| Acquirer's cost structure | Effect |
|---|---|
| **High fixed costs** - especially manufacturing, distribution, sales | **Substantial real savings**; the added resources (usually **customers**) are **plug-compatible** and spread existing fixed costs |
| Low fixed / mostly variable, or overlap is mainly **administrative** (HR, legal, purchasing) | **Minimal savings** - the deal **replicates** rather than reduces the cost position |

*Heating-oil illustration:* buying a competitor **in your own neighborhood** buys **customers** who ride your existing trucks - real duplicate fixed costs disappear. Buying a similar firm in a **different city** just **replicates** the cost structure somewhere new, because you still need their trucks.

**Why RBM pays:** share price tracks growth **relative to investors' expectations**. Disruptors start simple/cheap at the low end and climb tier by tier; analysts see only the current tier, so they **persistently underprice** them. **Nucor**: rebar -> upmarket, ~**27% compound annual stock appreciation 1983–1994**, then leveled off once analysts caught up (**discountability**). Across 37 disruptors, high P/Es looked overpriced yet buy-and-hold returned **~46% annually over 10 years**.

**The canonical disaster - Daimler–Chrysler ($36B, 1998):** Chrysler's real value was its **processes and lean profit formula** (modularized design, design cycle 5 years -> 2, one-fifth Daimler's overhead). Daimler promised $8B of "redundant cost" synergies and folded the resources in - **treating an RBM as an LBM** - and the profit formula and processes that made Chrysler valuable disappeared with it.

**Integration done right:** **EMC–VMware** (kept separate) · **Best Buy–Geek Squad** (high-touch service run alongside low-touch retail).

**Closing line:** the decision to acquire is really *whether it is faster and cheaper to buy something you could, given time and resources, build yourself.*

### 12.4 Organizational Design (Module 13 - bundled with M&A)

**Two building blocks:** **formal** (structure & control) and **informal** (culture). **Two firm characteristics that determine fit:** **firm size** and **organizational complexity**.

**Four structural building blocks:** specialization · formalization · centralization · hierarchy.

| | **Mechanistic** | **Organic** |
|---|---|---|
| Specialization/formalization | Much | Little |
| Hierarchy | Tall | Flat |
| Decision making | Centralized | Decentralized |

| Structure | Best fit | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | Small, low complexity; founders decide and run operations | Fast, direct control | No professional systems; doesn't scale |
| **Functional** | One core business line; functions correspond to value-chain stages, leaders report to the CEO | Deep domain expertise | Suboptimal cross-department communication (fix: cross-functional teams); **cannot handle diversification** |
| **Multidivisional (M-form)** | Diversified by product and/or geography; each **SBU** has **P&L responsibility**, operates independently, led by its own CEO | Clear accountability; widely adopted | Extra hierarchy layer (bureaucracy, red tape, duplication); slower decisions; SBUs compete politically for resources while still needing to cooperate |
| **Matrix** | Needs SBU expertise **plus** a second dimension (e.g. geography) | Combines M-form and organic benefits | Hard to implement; unclear reporting; **principal–agent problems**; slower decisions |

Firms move **Simple -> Functional -> Multidivisional -> Matrix** as size and complexity grow.

**Structure ↔ global strategy fit:** International -> **functional** · Multi-domestic -> **multidivisional** · Global standardization -> **multidivisional** · Transnational -> **global matrix**.

**Organizational culture:** shared **values** (what's important) and **norms** (expected attitudes/behaviors), expressed through **artifacts** - physical space, symbols, events, vocabulary/stories.

**Organizational inertia cycle:** mastery of/fit with the current environment -> success (measured financially) -> structure, metrics and systems built to manage size -> **inertia** -> the firm can't re-adapt when internal shifts (growth, business-model change, new markets, TMT change, M&A) or external **PESTEL** shifts occur, pulling it away from fit and restarting the cycle.

### 12.5 The Five Sources of Organizational Inertia (Grant Ch. 10)

The slides give you the *cycle*; Grant gives you the *mechanisms*. Each is a distinct, nameable barrier to change:

| # | Source | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Organizational capabilities and routines** | Capabilities rest on **routines** built by continual repetition. The more highly developed the routines, the harder it is to develop new ones -> firms fall into **competency traps**, where **"core capabilities become core rigidities"** (the same core-rigidity idea as §6.4) |
| 2 | **Social and political structures** | Organizations develop stable social patterns and stable systems of **political power**. Change disrupts the patterns and threatens those in authority, so the organization resists |
| 3 | **Conformity** | **Institutional isomorphism** - firms imitate one another to gain **legitimacy**, locking themselves into common structures and strategies. Pressure comes from governments, analysts, banks and other resource providers - and from **voluntary imitation**, since risk aversion pushes firms to look like their peers |
| 4 | **Complementarities between strategy, structure and systems** | Fit is the problem as well as the goal. A firm builds a complex, idiosyncratic configuration (strategy + structure + systems + culture + skills) matched to early conditions; changing it means changing **everything at once**. Hence organizations evolve by **punctuated equilibrium** - long stability while misalignment widens, then forced radical change |
| 5 | **Limited search and blinkered perceptions** | The Carnegie School: **bounded rationality** (limited information-processing capacity), **satisficing** (seeking satisfactory rather than optimal performance), and a preference for **exploitation** of existing knowledge over **exploration** of new opportunities |

**Underneath it all - evolutionary theory.** Organizations adapt through **variation -> selection -> retention**. The dispute is over the *level*:
- **Organizational ecologists:** evolution happens at the **industry** level. Individual firms are inert; competition is the **selection mechanism**; industry evolution therefore happens through a **changing population of firms** (deaths and births) rather than adaptation by a constant population.
- **Evolutionary theorists (Nelson & Winter):** evolution happens **inside** firms, at the level of the **organizational routine** - search for new routines, replication of successful ones, abandonment of failures. But adaptation is neither fast nor costless, and **search is triggered by declining performance**: when a company is doing well there is little impetus to change.

**The evidence is sobering.** Of the companies in the original 1896 Dow Jones Industrial Average, only **General Electric** remains in the index. Of the world's 12 biggest companies in 1912, just **three** were still in the top 12 in 2006.

### 12.6 Managing Organizational Change

| Approach | Content |
|---|---|
| **Dual strategies / separate units** | Christensen and Overdorf: house disruptive technologies in **organizationally separate units**. **Markides pushes back** - analysing dual business models, he finds success had **little to do with organizational separation**; what mattered was whether the new business model could **access and deploy the firm's existing resources and capabilities**. (Failed dual strategies: the budget airlines launched by British Airways, Continental and United.) |
| **Dual planning systems (Abell)** | Short-term planning for **fit and performance** over 1–2 years, *plus* long-term planning (5+ years) to develop vision, reshape the portfolio, reposition businesses, build new capabilities and redesign structure. Firms are biased toward **exploitation**; too little management time goes to **exploration** |
| **Bottom-up / decentralized change** | Decentralizing alone isn't enough - top management must manage the conditions for change: raise expectations with **stretch targets** (counteracting satisficing); issue company-wide challenges (GE's "be number 1 or number 2," "six sigma," "destroy-your-business.com"); watch for **strategic dissonance** (Grove) - divergent strategic directions inside the firm signalling a **strategic inflection point**, a fundamental change in industry dynamics demanding a radical shift (Intel: DRAM -> microprocessors); and periodically **oscillate between centralization and decentralization** to stimulate search then exploit its results |
| **Top-down punctuated change** | If change comes in punctuated bursts, those bursts must be **orchestrated from the top** - simultaneous change in strategy, structure, systems and top management. Restructuring **typically follows declining performance**; the real challenge is to do it **before** performance forces it, which may require the CEO to **manufacture a perception of impending crisis** |

**Shaping the future rather than adapting to it.** Hamel and Prahalad argue firms that merely adapt are doomed to play catch-up; advantage accrues to those that **initiate** change ("in an age of revolution, the company that is evolving slowly is already on its way to extinction").

> [!warning] But radical transformation usually fails
> **Successes:** Nokia (paper and rubber -> mobile phones), BP (bureaucratic state oil company -> flexible supermajor), Microsoft (rode successive disruptions). **Disasters:** Enron (utility/pipeline -> energy trading, collapsed 2001), Vivendi (water utility -> multimedia, 2002), GEC/Marconi (bankrupt 2002), ICI, Skandia.
> **Why:** competitive advantage rests on organizational capabilities, and **capabilities develop slowly**. Strategic change that takes a firm **beyond its competence domain involves massive risk**. This is the direct counterweight to the "just be revolutionary" answer choice.

### Confusion Pairs

| Torn between | Ask only this |
|---|---|
| **Cost** vs. **revenue** synergy | Value from **eliminating duplication** (cost, hard, certain) or **selling more** (revenue, soft, needs same-time-same-place)? |
| **Market power** vs. **cost savings** | Does the described effect show up as a **price increase that sticks** (market power - needs concentration) or **lower internal costs from overlap** (cost savings)? Both may be present - answer **the effect the stem describes**. |
| **Advantage** vs. **parity** | Is it rare and hard to imitate, or **matching what rivals already did**? "So we're not left behind" -> parity. |
| **Transferring skills** vs. **tactical/defensive** | **What triggered the bid** - a product roadmap (transfer; customers pay for the functionality) or **a rival's move**, with no plan to use the asset (defensive)? |
| **Real synergy** vs. **financial engineering** | Does it change the **competitive position** or only the **financials** (tax, WACC, working capital)? Financials are imitable -> parity. |
| **Merger of equals** vs. **rescue/distressed** | Relative strength **plus the market's reaction**: target up and acquirer down = rescue and overpayment. |
| **Alliance/JV** vs. **acquisition** | Does **ownership or control** transfer? |
| **LBM** vs. **RBM** | Are you buying **resources to plug in** (dissolve it) or a **business model** (keep it separate)? |
| **Bolt-on** vs. **transformative** | **Size relative to the acquirer** and how much of the business it changes. |

**Exam rule of thumb:** when two answers both seem true, the question is asking **which mechanism is primary** - follow the effect the stem describes. A price increase -> market power. A closed duplicate plant -> cost savings. A rival-triggered bid with no use plan -> defensive.

## §13. Strategic Fit & Applying Frameworks to Real Cases


> [!quote] What the Flashlight says
> The Flashlight: *"The exam leans heavily on applying frameworks to real, current business examples rather than defining terms in isolation. Practice reading a short business scenario and identifying which specific concept/driver is at play - that's the actual skill being tested throughout."*

### 13.1 The four-step scenario drill (do this in ~20 seconds per question)

1. **What's the unit of analysis?** *Industry* (Five Forces, PESTEL, life cycle) · *Firm's resources* (RBV - VRIS/VRIO) · *How it competes in one market* (cost vs. differentiation) · *Where it competes / its boundaries* (VI, diversification, M&A).
2. **Which direction does the value move?** Raising **V** -> differentiation. Lowering **C** -> cost leadership. Moving value **between parties** -> five forces / market power. Moving value **across businesses** -> economies of scope.
3. **Name the specific driver, not the family.** "Cost advantage" is never the answer if a driver is available; "economies of scale" vs. "capacity utilization" is the actual question.
4. **Check the trap.** Almost every question has one nearly-right distractor from an adjacent concept. Run the relevant Confusion Pair.

### 13.2 Master trigger-phrase mapper

| If the stem says… | Answer |
|---|---|
| "value increases as more people use it" | **Network effects / demand-side scale** |
| "the more it's produced, the cheaper it gets" | **Learning curve / economies of learning** |
| "spreads fixed costs over more units this period" | **Economies of scale** |
| "idle capacity," "subleasing warehouses," "empty trucks/cabins" | **Capacity utilization** |
| "eliminating slack, X-inefficiency, frugal culture" | **Residual efficiency / overhead** |
| "cheaper labor/materials because of where they buy" | **Input costs** |
| "fewer parts, designed for automated assembly" | **Product design** |
| "a superior production process (lean, float glass)" | **Process technology** |
| "customers perceive it as better even though it isn't" | **Differentiation is perceptual** |
| "reserved for the top-tier model to justify the premium" | **Differentiation (features + product-line tiering)** |
| "copied within a year by rivals" | **Product features = low-cost duplication** |
| "built over years of consistent interaction" | **Reputation** (usually costly to duplicate) |
| "bundled with another firm's product/subscription" | **Linkages with other firms** |
| "sold through a new/unusual channel" | **Distribution channels** |
| "valuable but every competitor has it" | **Competitive parity** |
| "no one can figure out which part causes the advantage" | **Causal ambiguity** |
| "accumulated over decades; can't be bought instantly" | **Path dependency** (+ time-compression diseconomies) |
| "culture, trust, relationships that can't be engineered" | **Social complexity** |
| "the firm has it but isn't set up to use it" | **Organization (the O) failure** |
| "an abrupt shift in tech/demand/politics destroyed its value" | **Schumpeterian revolution** |
| "the strength that made it great is now a liability" | **Core rigidity** |
| "asset is worthless outside this relationship" | **Transaction-specific investment -> hold-up** |
| "one buyer, one seller, no market price" | **Bilateral monopoly** |
| "we can't write a contract for every contingency" | **Incomplete contracts** |
| "our internal need is far below efficient plant scale" | **Differences in optimal scale (MES)** |
| "suppliers won't deal with us now that we compete with them" | **Competitive effects** |
| "one plant's strike shut every assembly line" | **Compounding risk** |
| "it assures supply / smooths earnings / captures more value" | **Spurious reasons to integrate** |
| "value together exceeds the sum of the parts" | **Economy of scope** |
| "shareholders could get this themselves by holding a portfolio" | **Financial motivation fails condition 2** |
| "we won't compete hard here because they'd retaliate there" | **Multipoint competition -> mutual forbearance** |
| "monopoly profits fund below-cost pricing elsewhere" | **Deep pockets / exploiting market power** |
| "pay tracks firm size, not profit" | **Managerial motivation / agency problem** |
| "high certainty the savings will be achieved" | **Cost savings = hard synergies** |
| "same time and same place" | **Revenue enhancement** (its only success condition) |
| "so we don't fall behind" | **Maintain competitive parity** |
| "bid only after a rival bid; no plan to use it" | **Tactical / defensive** |
| "WACC, refinancing, pooled working capital, inversion" | **Financial engineering -> parity** |
| "target's stock up, acquirer's down" | **Rescue / distressed acquisition** |
| "keep it separate, don't integrate it" | **RBM** |
| "fold the resources in and let the rest die" | **LBM** |
| "underperforms today but is simpler and cheaper for a fringe customer" | **Disruptive** |
| "improves what mainstream customers already value" | **Sustaining** (even if radical) |
| "known components rearranged for a new market" | **Architectural innovation** |
| "products improving faster than customers can use" | **Performance overshoot** |
| "small staged commitment because the payoff is unknown" | **Real options / preserve flexibility** |

### 13.3 The five most common wrong-answer patterns

1. **Naming the family instead of the driver** ("economies of scale" when the stem describes cumulative experience).
2. **Calling something vertical integration when nothing crossed the boundary** (supplier switching, scope expansion, an equity alliance, adjacent-service diversification).
3. **Confusing "sustaining" with "incremental"** - a radical innovation for your best customers is sustaining.
4. **Treating a factor as a force** (growth rate, technology, government, complements).
5. **Answering "profitable" when the question asks about advantage** - advantage is relative and requires **rarity**.

### Nvidia Steps Back

*Nvidia Steps Back From Cloud Effort to Compete with AWS* - The Information, Sep 2025


**Facts:** Nvidia is stepping back from DGX Cloud, which had put it in **quasi-competition with AWS**, and will primarily use the service for its **own researchers**. | **Make-or-buy** | Nvidia buys compute (renting back its own chips) rather than building - a boundary decision driven by scale and speed |
| **Strategic dissimilarity** | Running a cloud service business is a strategically different business from designing chips - different systems, pricing, customer management. Its prices were uncompetitive, which is what strategic dissimilarity looks like in practice |

## §15. Last-60-Seconds Recall


1. **Structure determines long-run profitability** - Five Forces explains *why* profitability is what it is, not whether an industry is profitable.
2. **Factors, not forces:** growth rate, technology, government, complements.
3. **Exit** barriers intensify rivalry; **entry** barriers protect incumbents.
4. **PESTEL = general environment** (uncontrollable macro). Buyers/suppliers/rivals/entrants/substitutes = Five Forces. SWOT: **VRIS/VRIO for S/W, PESTEL for O/T.**
5. **Identify the life-cycle stage from clues** - no dominant design + high price = introduction; standards emerging + scaling = growth; weak firms exiting + price weapon = shakeout; replacement demand + cost focus = maturity.
6. **Product innovation peaks early; process innovation peaks after the dominant design.**
7. **Increasing returns to adoption** come from **learning effects** and **network externalities**; they produce **dominant designs**, **path dependency**, and **winner-take-all** - unless network value is fully tapped at minority share, in which case designs coexist.
8. **Aggregator = direct user relationship + zero marginal cost + demand-driven multi-sided network with falling acquisition costs.** All three. **L1 buys supply (Netflix) · L2 pays to onboard supply (Uber) · L3 supply joins free (Google).**
9. **Sustaining ≠ incremental.** Sustaining improves what mainstream customers already value - even radically. **Disruptive underperforms there** while being simpler/cheaper for a fringe or new customer.
10. **Architectural = known components, new market. Radical = new knowledge base AND new market.**
11. **Real options = staged commitment to preserve flexibility under uncertainty** - and Barney's rule: high uncertainty about an economy of scope -> choose **less hierarchical** governance.
12. **Run the criteria in order, on ONE activity or resource, relative to competitors - and stop at the first failure.** The course teaches **VRIS** (4th = costly to **substitute**); Barney's text uses **VRIO** (4th = **organized to exploit**). Not valuable -> disadvantage. Valuable, not rare -> **parity** (still a strength; *not* using it is a disadvantage). Valuable + rare, imitable -> **temporary**. All four -> **sustained**.
13. **Sustained ≠ permanent** - it means not eroded *by imitation*. Schumpeterian revolutions still kill it.
14. **Profitable ≠ advantaged.** Advantage is relative and requires **rarity**.
15. **Four sources of inimitability by name:** physical uniqueness · path dependency (asset mass efficiencies, time-compression diseconomies) · causal ambiguity · social complexity.
16. **Xerox PARC = the O failure.** Great resources, wrong organization.
17. **Scale = output per period (and it reverses into diseconomies). Learning = cumulative output (and it doesn't).**
18. **The rare, sustainable cost sources are: learning economies (in emerging industries), differential low-cost access to inputs, and technological "software."** Hardware, scale, and policy choices are duplicable.
19. **Differentiation is perceptual, rarity is assumed, and product features are the easiest basis to copy.** Reputation, location, timing, channels and service/support are the hard ones.
20. **Stuck in the middle fails because the activities and organizational requirements conflict** - tight cost control vs. loose creative control - not merely because it's expensive. **Blue ocean/value innovation** (Eliminate–Reduce–Raise–Create) is the deliberate way to attempt both.
21. **Boundaries are set by relative cost:** integrate when **transaction costs > administrative costs**. Both sides have costs.
22. **Technical economies justify co-location, NOT ownership.**
23. **VI is warranted in one cell: high asset specificity × frequent transactions.** And the **pre/post-investment flip** means market failure is created by the investment, not by the initial structure.
24. **Six administrative costs:** optimal scale (**FedEx**) · developing capabilities · strategic dissimilarity · incentives · competitive effects (**Disney–ABC**) · flexibility - plus **compounding risk** (**GM 1998**).
25. **Spurious reasons to integrate:** smoothing earnings, assuring supply, "capturing more value." **Integrate for economic surplus, not value added.**
26. **Same industry, opposite integration levels** (Zara vs. Gap; Starbucks vs. franchisers) - industry conditions **underdetermine** the answer.
27. **Diversification needs both:** a real **economy of scope** *and* **hierarchical governance being the cheapest way to get it** - otherwise investors can replicate it in a portfolio for free.
28. **Four motivation families:** operational (strongest in theory) · financial (weak both ways) · anti-competitive · managerial (works for managers, not owners).
29. **Unrelated diversification can access only 4 of 8 motivations** - and the empirical ranking is **return cash > diversify-and-return-cash > diversify without returning cash.** Focus beats diversifiers.
30. **Why diversification fails despite good rationales:** the **premium pays out the future economies of scope**, integration takes too long, and operational economies of scope are "REALLY HARD to achieve."
31. **Seven M&A synergies. Advantage:** cost savings · revenue enhancement · skills/technology transfer · market power · tactical (*not always*). **No advantage:** financial engineering/tax · parity · managerial pay.
32. **"Hard" = certain, not difficult.** Cost savings, biggest in same-industry deals.
33. **Cross-selling works only at the same time and the same place.**
34. **Market power needs concentration** - fragmented industry is the wrong answer; regulators are the hurdle and "industry definition" is the loophole.
35. **Target up / acquirer down = rescue / distressed acquisition** (buying distressed value, market says overpayment).
36. **Only resources travel. LBM = fold in and dissolve. RBM = keep separate.** Overpay for LBM, underpay for RBM. **Daimler–Chrysler** integrated the wrong thing; **EMC–VMware** left it alone.
37. **No ownership transfer = alliance, not M&A.**
38. **When two answers both look right, name the mechanism the stem describes** - a price increase -> market power; a closed duplicate plant -> cost savings; a rival-triggered bid with no use plan -> defensive.

## Appendix A - Global Strategy (the third dimension of scope)

> [!note]- Where else this lives
> **Deep dive:** [[Vertical Integration - Full Depth]]


> [!info] Off-Flashlight - read this last
> **Not on the Flashlight.** It is in the Modules 9–12 deck and in your vault, and Module 13 ties **organizational structure** to these four strategies (§12.4) - which *is* Flashlight-adjacent. Treat this as insurance, not a priority: read it after everything in §0–§15 is solid.

### A.1 Globalization

**Globalization** - a shift toward a more integrated and interdependent world economy: closer integration and exchange between countries and peoples. Made possible by **falling trade and investment barriers**, **advances in telecommunications**, and **reductions in transportation costs**.

**Two key facets:**
- **Broadening** - extension of economic and geographic linkages to encompass virtually all major societies and states
- **Deepening** - an increase in the **frequency and intensity** of state and societal interactions

**Four globalization drivers:**

| Driver | Content |
|---|---|
| **Market factors** | World customers are similar across nations · channels of distribution becoming more global · technology making world markets more accessible |
| **Cost factors** | Avoiding cost inefficiencies · **size is a major asset** |
| **Environmental factors** | Government barriers have fallen dramatically · accelerated flow of information across borders |
| **Competitive factors** | Companies match competitors' moves · look for **less competitive markets** (BRIC) · market presence is necessary to execute global strategies |

**Counterweight - Ghemawat's "10% surprise":** the internationally-crossing share of most activity (telephone calls, direct investment, private charity, stock investment, management research, trade) is **far lower** than globalization rhetoric implies. Framed in the deck as *"The World Is Flat" vs. "The Clash of Civilizations."*

### A.2 Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Global strategy** | Part of corporate strategy: gain and sustain competitive advantage while competing against **both foreign and domestic** companies |
| **Foreign direct investment (FDI)** | Investments in **value-chain activities abroad** |
| **Multinational enterprise (MNE)** | Deploys resources and capabilities in **two or more countries** |

### A.3 CAGE Distance Framework

**Distance is the main cost and risk of expansion.** CAGE guides which countries to enter:

| | Distance type |
|---|---|
| **C** | **Cultural** |
| **A** | **Administrative and political** |
| **G** | **Geographic** |
| **E** | **Economic** |

### A.4 The central trade-off: cost reductions vs. local responsiveness

Two opposing forces in global competition - **cost reductions** (a key competitive weapon) vs. **local responsiveness** (tailoring to specific preferences).

**Globalization hypothesis:** consumer needs and preferences are **converging** - food, music, movies, clothing (McDonald's, Coca-Cola, rock music, Hollywood films, Levi's).

**The Integration–Responsiveness framework - the four strategies:**

| | **Low local responsiveness** | **High local responsiveness** |
|---|---|---|
| **High cost-reduction pressure** | **Global-standardization** - economies of scale and location economies through a **global division of labor**, placed wherever capabilities are cheapest; **price is the main weapon**; minimal local adaptation. *Infosys, Lenovo, Siemens Energy* | **Transnational** - *"think globally, act locally"*; best practices, ideas and innovations used everywhere; used by MNEs pursuing a **blue ocean** strategy. **Difficult:** duplication of effort, organizational complexity, costly. *ABB, Bertelsmann, P&G* |
| **Low cost-reduction pressure** | **International** - sells the **same** products/services at home and abroad, leveraging **home-based core competencies**. Works for MNEs with **large domestic markets** and **strong reputations/brand names**. *Harley-Davidson, Rolex, Starbucks* | **Multidomestic** - local consumers ideally **perceive the products as local**; can be **costly and inefficient** through duplication of business functions across countries. Common in **consumer products and food**. *Bridgestone, Nestlé, Philips* |

**Structure must match** (from Module 13 - see §12.4): International -> **functional** · Multi-domestic -> **multidivisional** · Global standardization -> **multidivisional** · Transnational -> **global matrix**.

### A.5 Modes of foreign-market entry

Along a continuum of **rising investment and control**:

**Exporting -> long-term contracts (licensing, franchising) -> equity alliances -> joint ventures -> wholly owned subsidiary (acquisition or greenfield).**

> [!tip] One shape, three topics
> Note this is the **same continuum shape** as the make-or-buy ladder in §9.5 and the vertical-relationship continuum in §10.5 - commitment rises with the specificity of what's at stake. Recognizing that one shape recurs across three topics is worth more than memorizing three lists.

**Advantages of international expansion:** new markets · lower-cost inputs · new competencies.
**Disadvantages:** **liability of foreignness** · loss of reputation · loss of intellectual property.

### A.6 National competitive advantage

**Death-of-distance hypothesis** - challenged by the fact that high-performing firms in certain industries stay **concentrated in specific countries**: US (biotechnology, software, internet) · China and Taiwan (computer manufacturing) · South Korea and Japan (consumer electronics) · Australia (mining) · India (business process outsourcing) · Germany (engineering and cars) · Italy (fashion) · France (wine).

**Porter's Diamond of National Competitive Advantage** - four determinants:

| Determinant | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Factor conditions** | Inputs: skilled labor, infrastructure, capital, natural resources |
| **Demand conditions** | Nature and sophistication of home-market demand |
| **Related and supporting industries** | Presence of supplier **clusters** and complementors |
| **Competitive intensity** | Rivalry and firm strategy/structure in the focal industry |

> [!example] The Diamond in the negative
> **The Diamond "in the negative"** is a live case you already have: Guardian Bikes reshored assembly to Indiana but **90% of component cost still comes from China**, because there is **no domestic supplier cluster** for hubs and cranks - a missing *related and supporting industries* leg.
