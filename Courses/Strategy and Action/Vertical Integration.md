## 1. Core Idea

- **Corporate strategy answers *where* to compete; business strategy answers *how*.** Corporate strategy is fundamentally a set of decisions about the **scope** — the boundary — of the firm.
- Scope has three dimensions: **vertical** (integration), **product** (diversification), and **geographic** (multinationality). All three are analyzed with the same underlying toolkit: economies of scope in resources/capabilities, transaction costs, and costs of corporate complexity.
- **The firm's boundary is set by relative cost.** Where the transaction costs of using markets exceed the administrative costs of organizing internally, activity gets pulled inside the firm; where the reverse holds, it gets pushed out to the market.
- The single biggest driver *toward* vertical integration is **transaction-specific investment** combined with **uncertainty** (which makes complete contracts impossible). The drivers *away* from it are scale mismatches, capability development, strategic dissimilarity, weak internal incentives, flexibility needs, and compounded risk.
- **Make-or-buy is not a binary choice.** Between spot contracts and full ownership sits a continuum of hybrid forms — long-term contracts, franchising, vendor partnerships, equity alliances, joint ventures.
- For product scope, performance follows an **inverted-U**: related diversification outperforms both single-business focus and unrelated conglomerates. For geographic scope, strategy choice is set by the trade-off between **cost reduction pressure** and **local responsiveness pressure**.

## 2. Key Terms & Definitions

### 2.1 Scope and Corporate Strategy

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Corporate strategy** | Decisions about the overall scope of the firm — which industries, which countries, which vertical stages it operates in. "Where do we compete?" |
| **Business (competitive) strategy** | Decisions about how the firm competes within a single chosen market. "How do we compete?" |
| **Product scope** | How broad a range of products/services the firm supplies; the diversification dimension. |
| **Geographical scope** | The spread of the firm's activities across regions and countries. |
| **Vertical scope** | How many linked stages of the value chain the firm performs itself rather than buying. |
| **Specialized company** | A firm operating in essentially one industry sector. |
| **Diversified company** | A firm whose operations span several distinct industries. |
| **Economies of scale** | Unit-cost reductions from producing greater volume of the same thing. |
| **Economies of scope** | Cost advantages from applying the same resource or capability across several different products/markets. |
| **Strategic business unit (SBU)** | A semi-autonomous division of a diversified firm with its own market and P&L. |
| **Principal–agent problem** | Owners (principals) want shareholder value; managers/employees (agents) may pursue their own interests instead. A cost of organizing inside firms, arising from separation of ownership and control. |
| **Hostile takeover** | An unsolicited acquisition attempt; slide framing treats the threat of one as a discipline forcing firms to grow and perform. |

### 2.2 Transaction Cost Economics

| Term                                | Definition                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Market mechanism**                | Coordination via independent decisions guided by prices. Adam Smith's *invisible hand* — no conscious planning required.                                                                                      |
| **Administrative mechanism**        | Coordination via managerial decision imposed through a hierarchy. Chandler's *visible hand* — depends on active planning.                                                                                     |
| **Firm**                            | An organization of individuals bound by employment contracts to a central contracting authority. Not strictly necessary for complex production — subcontracting can do the same job through market contracts. |
| **Transaction costs**               | All the costs of using the market: searching for counterparties, negotiating and drafting contracts, monitoring compliance, and enforcing through arbitration or litigation.                                  |
| **External transaction costs**      | Costs of dealing outside the firm — finding contractors, negotiating, monitoring, enforcing. Largely driven by the environment the firm operates in.                                                          |
| **Internal transaction costs**      | Costs of organizing inside — recruiting and retaining staff, setting up operations. Tend to rise with firm size.                                                                                              |
| **Administrative costs**            | The internal cost of running an activity within the firm's hierarchy; the counterweight to transaction costs.                                                                                                 |
| **Relative cost (Coase)**           | The rule determining firm boundaries: activities are internalized when market transaction costs exceed internal administrative costs.                                                                         |
| **Interaction costs**               | Alternative label for transaction costs — the time and money spent whenever parties exchange goods, services, or ideas.                                                                                       |
| **New Institutional Economics**     | Douglass North's line of work on how institutions (the rules of the game) shape economic exchange and its costs.                                                                                              |
| **Corporate economy**               | The late-1960s thesis (Galbraith) that firms' planning advantages would make giant corporations dominant over markets. Refuted in the 1980s–90s.                                                              |
| **Downsizing / refocusing**         | The reverse trend: firms cutting product scope back to core businesses and vertical scope through outsourcing.                                                                                                |
| **Information asymmetry**           | One party (usually the seller) holds private information the other lacks, which can crowd good offerings out with inferior ones. A cost of using markets.                                                     |
| **Opportunism**                     | Self-interested exploitation of a counterparty's dependence once they are locked in.                                                                                                                          |
| **Strategic misrepresentation**     | Deliberately distorting information to improve one's bargaining position in a dependent relationship.                                                                                                         |
| **Hold-up**                         | The specific opportunism risk that arises when one party's assets are worthless outside the relationship, letting the other extract better terms after the fact.                                              |
| **Transaction-specific investment** | An asset built specifically to serve one counterparty, with little value in any other use. The core culprit that turns a competitive market into a dependent one.                                             |
| **Bilateral monopoly**              | A market with one buyer and one seller, so no equilibrium price exists — outcomes depend purely on relative bargaining power.                                                                                 |
| **Incomplete contracts**            | Contracts that cannot anticipate every future circumstance, because the future is uncertain. Why "just write a better contract" fails to solve opportunism.                                                   |
| **High-powered incentives**         | The sharp profit-based motivation created by a market interface between buyer and seller.                                                                                                                     |
| **Low-powered incentives**          | The muted motivation of an internal supplier–customer relationship governed by management systems — consequences for poor performance are slow and undramatic.                                                |
| **Search costs**                    | The cost of finding and evaluating a suitable market counterparty.                                                                                                                                            |
| **Fiat / command and control**      | The firm's ability to simply direct action through hierarchical authority rather than negotiate it.                                                                                                           |

### 2.3 Vertical Integration

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Vertical integration** | Firm ownership of vertically related activities. Measured by the ratio of value added to sales revenue — highly integrated firms buy in relatively little. |
| **Backward vertical integration** | Taking ownership of the production of one's own inputs or components (moving upstream). |
| **Forward vertical integration** | Taking ownership of activities previously performed by one's customers (moving downstream, closer to the end user). |
| **Upstream / downstream industries** | Upstream = raw materials, components; downstream = marketing, sales, after-sales service. |
| **Full integration** | All of one stage's output is transferred internally to the next stage — no third-party sales or purchases. |
| **Partial integration** | Stages are not internally self-sufficient; the firm is a net buyer or seller of the intermediate good. |
| **Technical economies** | Cost savings from physically integrating processes at one site (e.g. reduced transport and energy costs). Note: these justify **co-location**, not necessarily **common ownership**. |
| **Minimum efficient scale (MES)** | The output level at which an activity reaches full scale efficiency. If a firm's own needs fall well below MES, in-house production is inefficient. |
| **Shared service organization** | An internal supplier of corporate services (IT, training, engineering) forced to compete against external providers, to restore incentive intensity inside an integrated firm. |
| **Compounding risk** | The exposure created when a problem at one owned stage halts production and profit at every other stage. |
| **Architectural capabilities** | The ability to design, coordinate, and integrate the whole system of components supplied by others. |
| **Component capabilities** | Deep expertise in producing a specific part or subsystem. |
| **Virtual corporation** | An organizational form where the firm's main function is coordinating a network of external suppliers. |
| **Hollow corporation** | The failure state of the virtual corporation — so much is outsourced that the firm loses the ability to evolve and adapt. |
| **Core competences / core products** | Prahalad and Hamel's argument that competences are embodied in specific core products, so outsourcing those products erodes the competence itself. |
| **Systems integrator** | The reduced strategic role of a firm that outsources most functions and only assembles/coordinates. |
| **Taper integration** | A deliberate mix: the firm is partly integrated backward or forward *and* also uses outside suppliers or distributors. Keeps in-house units exposed to competition, adds flexibility, and enables knowledge recombination. |
| **Strategic outsourcing** | Moving internal value chain activities — typically non-core ones — to other firms. |
| **Offshoring** | Relocating activities to another country, usually to exploit cost differences. Distinct from outsourcing, which is about ownership rather than location. |

### 2.4 Designing Vertical Relationships

| Term                     | Definition                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Spot contract**        | A one-off market purchase at the current price, with no ongoing commitment or documentation beyond common law.                                                                                         |
| **Long-term contract**   | A specified series of transactions over time, setting terms and each party's responsibilities. Protects transaction-specific investment but risks being either too rigid or too loose.                 |
| **Vendor partnership**   | A close, trust-based supplier relationship providing security for specific investments plus flexibility, often without complete formal specification.                                                  |
| **Relational contract**  | A vendor partnership operating with no written contract at all — governed by mutual understanding and expectation of future business.                                                                  |
| **Franchising**          | A contract letting the franchisee produce and market the franchiser's product under its trademark and business system in a defined area. Combines corporate brand/systems with local entrepreneurship. |
| **Agency agreement**     | A relationship where one firm acts on another's behalf in the market; low commitment, moderate formalization.                                                                                          |
| **Equity alliance**      | One partner takes a partial ownership stake in another — greater commitment, a "try before you buy" step.                                                                                              |
| **Joint venture**        | Two or more partners create a new, jointly owned organization.                                                                                                                                         |
| **Formalization**        | How elaborately documented a vertical relationship is (one of the two axes classifying relationship types).                                                                                            |
| **Degree of commitment** | How much each party has invested in the relationship (the other classifying axis).                                                                                                                     |
| **Allocation of risk**   | The implicit or explicit division of downside exposure written into any beyond-spot arrangement; depends on bargaining power and efficiency.                                                           |
| **Incentive structure**  | The contractual design that makes performance in each party's interest. Over-specification is itself costly; the promise of future business is often the strongest incentive available.                |

### 2.5 Diversification

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Product diversification** | Increasing the variety of products/services offered; being active across several product markets. |
| **Geographic diversification** | Increasing the variety of regional, national, or international markets served. |
| **Product-market diversification** | Doing both simultaneously. |
| **Single business** | >95% of revenue from one business; lowest level of diversification. |
| **Dominant business** | 70–95% of revenue from the primary business, with one additional activity pursued. |
| **Related-constrained diversification** | <70% from primary business, and *all* business units share competencies. |
| **Related-linked diversification** | <70% from primary business, and *some* business units share competencies. |
| **Unrelated diversification (conglomerate)** | <70% from primary business, and units share few or no competencies. |
| **Diversification–performance relationship** | Inverted-U curve: performance rises from single → dominant → related, then falls off into unrelated. |
| **Build-Borrow-or-Buy framework** | Capron & Mitchell's decision sequence for closing a strategic resource gap. |
| **Strategic resource gap** | The distance between the resources a firm has and those its strategy requires — the trigger for the build/borrow/buy decision. |
| **Relevance** | Whether internal resources are similar to what's needed *and* superior to competitors' in the target area. High relevance → build internally. |
| **Tradability** | Whether the needed resource can be transferred by contract (licensing, franchising). High tradability → borrow contractually. |
| **Closeness** | How tightly the firm must work with the resource holder. Low closeness need → equity alliance or JV suffices. |
| **Integration** | Acquisition is warranted only under low relevance, low tradability, and a high need for closeness — and only if the target can actually be integrated. |

### 2.6 Global Strategy

| Term                                                   | Definition                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Globalization**                                      | The shift toward a more integrated and interdependent world economy through closer exchange between countries and peoples.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| **Broadening**                                         | Extending economic and geographic linkages to encompass virtually all major states and societies.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| **Deepening**                                          | Increasing the frequency and intensity of those interactions.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| **Globalization drivers**                              | Four categories pushing firms international: **market** factors (converging customers, global channels, accessible technology), **cost** factors (avoiding cost inefficiencies, scale as an asset), **environmental** factors (falling government barriers, faster information flow), and **competitive** factors (matching rivals' moves, seeking less contested markets, needing presence to execute globally). |
| **Foreign direct investment (FDI)**                    | Investment in value chain activities located abroad.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| **Multinational enterprise (MNE)**                     | A firm deploying resources and capabilities in two or more countries.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| **Liability of foreignness**                           | The inherent disadvantage of operating outside the home market — unfamiliarity, weaker local networks, added cost.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| **CAGE distance framework**                            | Four kinds of distance that create the cost and risk of foreign expansion: **C**ultural, **A**dministrative/political, **G**eographic, **E**conomic.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| **Globalization hypothesis**                           | The claim that consumer needs and preferences are converging worldwide (food, music, film, clothing). Used to justify standardization.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| **The 10% surprise**                                   | Ghemawat's counterpoint: the internationally-crossing share of most activity (phone calls, direct investment, charity, stock investment, even trade) is far lower than the globalization rhetoric implies.                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| **Death of distance hypothesis**                       | The claim that location no longer matters — undercut by the persistent clustering of high performers in particular countries.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| **Location economies**                                 | Cost or quality benefits from performing an activity in the optimal country for it.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| **International strategy**                             | Low cost-reduction pressure, low local-responsiveness pressure. Sell essentially the same offering at home and abroad, leveraging home-based core competencies. Typical of firms with big home markets and strong brands.                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| **Multidomestic strategy**                             | Low cost-reduction pressure, high local-responsiveness pressure. Tailor offerings so locals perceive them as local. Costly — duplicates functions across countries.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| **Global-standardization strategy**                    | High cost-reduction pressure, low local-responsiveness pressure. Pursue scale and location economies via a global division of labor; compete mainly on price with minimal local adaptation.                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| **Transnational strategy**                             | High pressure on both. "Think globally, act locally" — diffuse best practices everywhere while adapting locally. Hard to execute: duplication, complexity, expense.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| **Modes of entry**                                     | Ordered by investment and control: exporting → long-term contracts (licensing, franchising) → equity alliances → joint ventures → wholly owned subsidiary (acquisition or greenfield).                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| **Greenfield**                                         | Building a wholly owned foreign operation from scratch rather than acquiring one.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| **Porter's Diamond of National Competitive Advantage** | Four interacting determinants of why certain nations breed world-leading firms in certain industries: **factor conditions**, **demand conditions**, **related and supporting industries/complementors**, and **competitive intensity in the focal industry**.                                                                                                                                                     |

## 3. Why the Boundary Moves: Firms vs. Markets

### 3.1 The historical swing

| Period | Direction | Cause |
|---|---|---|
| 19th c. – mid-20th c. | Firms absorb market transactions; scope grows vertically, geographically, across sectors | Falling administrative costs — **technology** (telegraph, telephone, computer expanded managers' communication and decision capacity) and **management techniques** (double-entry bookkeeping onward, scientific management, six decades of rapid method development) |
| Late 1960s | Prediction of a "corporate economy" dominated by a few giants | Observed growth of large corporations at markets' expense |
| 1980s–1990s onward | Sharp reversal: downsizing and refocusing; largest firms' share of US private-sector employment declines | Turbulence — oil shocks (1973, 1979), end of fixed exchange rates (1972), the integrated circuit, surging international competition. Under instability, administrative costs rise as the need for flexibility outruns traditional management systems |
| Recent | Both directions at once | Hybrid collaborative relationships proliferate, *and* the internet cuts market transaction costs (search, payments), reviving arm's-length contracting |

**Study point:** the mechanism is always *relative* cost. Technology can move the boundary in either direction depending on whether it cuts administrative costs (favoring integration) or transaction costs (favoring markets). The internet is the standard exam case of the latter.

### 3.2 Firms vs. markets, side by side

| | **Firm (internal organization)** | **Market** |
|---|---|---|
| **Advantages** | Command and control (fiat, hierarchical authority); coordination; safe environment for transaction-specific investments; community of knowledge | High-powered incentives; flexibility |
| **Disadvantages** | Administrative costs; low-powered incentives; principal–agent problem | Search costs; opportunism (hold-up); incomplete contracting — difficulty specifying and measuring performance, information asymmetries, enforcement costs |

### 3.3 The make-or-buy rule

- If **cost(in-house) < cost(market)** → vertically integrate: own the production of inputs, or own the output distribution channels.
- If **cost(market) < cost(in-house)** → purchase instead.
- Internal costs tend to scale with firm size (firm-dependent); external costs are largely a function of the operating environment (environment-dependent).

## 4. Why Vertical Integration Happens: The Transaction Cost Argument

### 4.1 The steel cans chain — the canonical illustration

The chain runs: iron ore mining → steel production → steel strip production → can-making → canning of food/drink/oil.

| Interface | Governance actually observed | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Iron ore → steel | Market contracts | Commodity conditions |
| Steel → steel strip | Vertical integration | Technical economies from hot-rolling steel immediately after pouring force both parties into co-located, purpose-built plants → transaction-specific investment → bilateral monopoly |
| Steel strip → cans | Market contracts (specialist can producers buy strip on contract) | Low transaction costs: many buyers and sellers, readily available information, low switching costs on both sides |
| Cans → canning | Both vertical integration and market contracts | Mixed conditions |

Generalization from the same logic: few jewelry firms own gold mines; few flour millers own wheat farms. **Commodity inputs with thick markets get bought, not made.**

### 4.2 The causal chain to memorize

**Technical economies → co-located, purpose-built assets → transaction-specific investment → bilateral monopoly → no equilibrium price, only bargaining power → opportunism and strategic misrepresentation → hold-up risk → market contracting becomes inefficient → integrate.**

Two traps embedded here:

1. **Technical economies alone do not justify integration.** They explain why plants must sit next to each other; they do not explain why one company must own both. The ownership question is answered only by transaction costs.
2. **"Just write a better contract" fails** because the future is uncertain — nobody can anticipate every circumstance over, say, a plant's 30-year life. Contracts are inevitably incomplete.

### 4.3 Empirical support cited

- **Automakers:** specialized components are made in-house more often than commodity items like tires and spark plugs.
- **Aerospace:** company-specific components are made in-house more often than bought externally.
- **Semiconductors:** some firms specialize in design or fabrication; others integrate both stages. Integrated producers perform relatively better the more technically complex the circuit — because complexity raises the need for designer–fabricator collaboration.

## 5. Why Vertical Integration Often Fails: Administrative Costs of Internalization

| Factor | Mechanism | Illustration |
|---|---|---|
| **Differences in optimal scale** | If the firm's own requirement is far below the MES of the adjacent activity, internal production is inefficient — and the transaction costs avoided are trivial next to the scale penalty | FedEx buys 40,000+ trucks/vans annually vs. an assembly plant MES of 200,000; Ford produced two million commercial vehicles in 2005. Small brewers don't make their own cans for the same reason |
| **Developing distinctive capabilities** | Specialists serving many customers learn faster; a captive internal department serving only itself doesn't | Xerox, Kodak, Philips can't match IT specialists like EDS, IBM, Accenture. **Counter-case:** where a capability builds on an adjacent one, integration *helps* — IBM's mainframe leadership rested on in-house semiconductors and software; Wal-Mart's retail efficiency rests on in-house IT and logistics |
| **Managing strategically different businesses** | Different stages need different management systems, planning, control, HR, and top-management skills | FedEx owning a truck maker; the general rarity of integrated manufacturing-and-retailing firms. Drove de-integration at Marriott (owning ≠ operating hotels), Whitbread, Scottish & Newcastle |
| **The incentive problem** | Market interfaces produce high-powered incentives; internal transfer relationships produce low-powered ones, where consequences are slow and undramatic | An inefficient in-house tanker fleet costs employees bonuses eventually. Partial fix: **shared service organizations** that force internal suppliers to compete externally |
| **Competitive effects** | Rarely a real monopoly-extension tool — once a firm monopolizes one stage there's no extra monopoly profit in adjacent stages. The realistic risk is that integration makes independent suppliers and customers unwilling to deal with a firm they now see as a competitor | Standard Oil and Alcoa are the classic (and rare) foreclosure cases. After Disney acquired ABC, rival studios became less interested in developing programming with ABC |
| **Flexibility** | Cuts both ways. For rapid response to uncertain demand and to new product opportunities requiring new technical combinations, markets win. For system-wide simultaneous adjustment, integration wins | Markets: construction's cyclical demand; contract manufacturers behind iPod, Xbox, Dell notebooks. Integration: American Apparel's LA design-to-distribution cycle; Zara |
| **Compounding risk** | A problem at any owned stage threatens output and profit at every stage; worse when technology or preferences shift fast | A 1998 union strike at one GM brake plant halted all 24 US assembly plants |

## 6. The Decision Criteria: Integrate or Outsource?

Paraphrased from Grant's summary table. Each row is a diagnostic question and the direction it pushes.

| Question about the vertical relationship | Which way it points |
|---|---|
| How many firms operate in the adjacent activity? | Fewer firms → higher transaction costs → favors **VI** |
| Must either party make transaction-specific investments? | Yes → favors **VI** |
| How evenly is information distributed across the stages? | Greater asymmetry → more opportunism → favors **VI** |
| Are intermediate-product transactions taxed or regulated? | Yes → those are a cost of contracting that VI avoids → favors **VI** |
| How uncertain are circumstances over the relationship's life? | More uncertainty → harder to write contracts → favors **VI** |
| Are the two stages similar in optimal scale? | Greater dissimilarity → favors **market contracts** |
| Are the two stages strategically similar (shared success factors, common resources/capabilities)? | Greater similarity → favors **VI** |
| How much continual investment in upgrading capabilities is needed? | Greater need → favors **outsourcing** |
| How much entrepreneurial flexibility and drive do the separate activities need? | Greater need → high-powered market incentives win → favors **outsourcing** |
| How uncertain is market demand? | More unpredictable → flexibility matters more → favors **outsourcing** |
| Would integration compound risk across the whole chain? | Heavier investment and bigger independent risks per stage → VI is riskier |

**Critical qualifier:** even facing identical external circumstances, two firms in the same industry can rationally choose opposite answers, because they hold different resources and capabilities and pursue different strategies. Within low-end fashion, Zara is far more integrated than H&M or Gap; within designer clothing, Armani is more integrated than Donna Karan.

## 7. Designing Vertical Relationships: The Full Continuum

### 7.1 Classifying by commitment and formalization

Relationship types are positioned on two axes — **degree of commitment** (how much each side invests) and **formalization** (how documented the arrangement is).

| Type | Commitment | Formalization | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spot sales/purchases | Low | Low | Little or no documentation; bound by common law |
| Long-term contracts | Low–moderate | High | Complex written agreements |
| Agency agreements | Moderate | Moderate–high | |
| Franchises | Moderate | High | Complex written agreements |
| Informal supplier/customer relationships | Moderate | Low | Informal by definition |
| Supplier/customer partnerships | Moderate–high | Low | Trust-based |
| Joint ventures | High | Moderate | New jointly owned entity |
| Vertical integration | High | At management's discretion | Substantial investment |

### 7.2 The make-or-buy continuum (slide version)

**BUY** ← arm's-length market transactions | short-term contracts | *Strategic Alliances*: long-term contracts (licensing, franchising) → equity alliances → joint ventures | parent–subsidiary relationship | activities performed in-house → **MAKE**

| Position | Characteristics | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Short-term contracts | Under a year; no quality guarantee; contract bidding | GM used short-term contracts for standard car components and lost ground to Toyota over quality problems |
| Long-term contracts / licensing / franchising | Over a year; time to overcome quality issues and build trust | Biotech licensing-in; McDonald's and Subway franchising |
| Equity alliance | One partner takes partial ownership; greater commitment; "try before you buy" | Coca-Cola holds an 18.5% equity stake in Monster |
| Joint venture | Two or more partners create a new organization | Hulu — NBC, Disney, and Providence Equity |
| Parent–subsidiary | Parent owns and controls by fiat | GM owned Opel and Vauxhall, eventually divesting them |

### 7.3 Trade-offs of the main forms

| Form | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| **Spot contracts** | Work well under competitive conditions — many buyers and sellers, standard product, no specific investment needed | No protection at all once specific investment enters |
| **Long-term contracts** | Avoid opportunism and provide the security needed to make specific investments | Cannot anticipate all circumstances; risk being too restrictive *or* so loose they invite opportunism and disputed interpretation. Rigidity is acute in IT outsourcing deals running 10+ years |
| **Vendor partnerships** | Provide security for specific investments, flexibility under change, and incentives against opportunism — sometimes with no written contract at all | Depend on trust; mutual dependence creates vulnerability for both sides |
| **Franchising** | Marries the large firm's brand, marketing, and systems with the small firm's entrepreneurship and local knowledge; enables coordination and specific-asset investment *while* retaining high-powered incentives and flexibility between strategically dissimilar businesses | The franchisee, as the weaker party, bears most of the risk — its capital is exposed and it pays a flat royalty on sales |

### 7.4 Two design variables beyond the make-or-buy choice

1. **Allocation of risk.** Any arrangement past a spot contract implicitly allocates risk. How it's split depends on bargaining power and efficiency. Franchising loads risk onto the franchisee. Oil-exploration outsourcing has shifted from fixed-price contracts toward risk-sharing deals where the driller takes an equity stake in the project.
2. **Incentive structures.** Contracts must make good performance self-interested — an under-specified concrete-supply contract invites substandard concrete. But completeness is itself costly (the $400 Navy toilet seats reflect specifications that filled many pages). Often the strongest available incentive is simply the prospect of future business: fixed-term operating contracts with performance reviews and re-tendering in privatized public services; the expectation of continued business in Toyota's and Marks & Spencer's vendor partnerships.

### 7.5 Recent trends

- **Hybrid forms proliferate**, trying to combine market flexibility and incentives with the close collaboration of integration.
- **Collaborative vertical networks are not new** — long a feature of craft industries in Europe and India; visible in northern Italy's industrial districts (textiles, packaging equipment, motorcycles). Japanese supplier relationships, with extensive knowledge sharing, reshaped Western practice: a mass shift from arm's-length purchasing to long-term collaboration with fewer suppliers, competitive tendering and multi-sourcing giving way to single-supplier arrangements plus certification and quality programs.
- **Outsourcing intensified by international cost differences** — manufacturing to China, services (call centers, IT) to India.
- **Equity stakes and profit-sharing** used to bind relationships and deter opportunism: Commonwealth Bank of Australia took a stake in its IT supplier EDS Australia; pharma firms take stakes in the biotechs doing their R&D; oilfield services firms increasingly take equity in upstream projects.
- **Counter-trend:** the internet slashed search costs and eased electronic payment, reviving arm's-length competitive contracting through B2B hubs (Covisint in auto parts, Elemica in chemicals, Rock and Dirt in construction equipment).
- **Outsourcing has widened from components to whole chunks of the chain** — payroll, IT, training, customer service, and in electronics the design and manufacture of entire products (Hon Hai builds Apple iPods, Nokia phones, Sony's PlayStation).
- **The resulting risk:** a virtual corporation may hollow out. The critical test is whether a firm that outsources most functions retains the **architectural capabilities** needed to manage partners' **component capabilities**. Even aero-engine makers who outsource key components typically keep R&D in those component technologies — evidence that architectural and component capabilities are complements, not substitutes.

## 8. Product Scope: Diversification

### 8.1 Why firms grow (slide rationale)

- Increase profits and shareholder returns — funding reinvestment and higher wages/benefits; failing to do so invites a hostile takeover.
- Lower costs and achieve economies of scale.
- Increase market power.
- Reduce risk through diversification, especially when one SBU underperforms.
- Motivate management and mitigate the principal–agent problem.

### 8.2 Rumelt's four types

Classified by **% of revenue from the dominant/primary business** (types 1–2) and **the relationship of core competencies across business units** (types 3–4).

| Revenue from primary business | Type | Competency sharing | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| >95% | Single business | One business leverages its competencies | Birkenstock, Coca-Cola, Facebook |
| 70–95% | Dominant business | Dominant and minor businesses share competencies | Harley-Davidson, Nestlé, UPS |
| <70% | Related-constrained | Businesses generally share competencies | ExxonMobil, Johnson & Johnson, Nike |
| <70% | Related-linked | *Some* businesses share competencies | Amazon, Disney, GE |
| <70% | Unrelated (conglomerate) | Few if any shared competencies | Samsung, Berkshire Hathaway, Yamaha |

**The performance relationship is an inverted U** — rising from single business through dominant business to a peak at related diversification (constrained and linked), then declining into unrelated diversification.

### 8.3 Build-Borrow-or-Buy (Capron & Mitchell)

A sequential filter starting from a **strategic resource gap**:

| Step | Question | Answer | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | How **relevant** are internal resources? (similar to what's needed *and* superior to competitors') | High | **Build** — internal development |
| 2 | How **tradable** are the targeted resources? (can ownership or use be transferred by contract) | High | **Borrow** — contractual alliance: contract or licensing |
| 3 | How **close** must you be to the resource partner? | Low closeness need | **Borrow** — alliance with equity: equity alliance or joint venture |
| 4 | How well can you **integrate** the target firm? | High | **Buy** — acquisition |
| — | If integration capability is low | | Revisit build-borrow-buy options or reformulate strategy |

Acquisition is therefore the *last* resort — justified only by low relevance, low tradability, and a high need for closeness. M&A is complex and costly, and post-integration failures are common (Microsoft–Nokia is the slide's cautionary case, with cultural distance a candidate explanation).

## 9. Geographic Scope: Global Strategy

### 9.1 The central trade-off

Two opposing forces govern global competition: **pressure for cost reductions** and **pressure for local responsiveness**. Everything downstream — strategy type, structure, entry mode — follows from where a firm sits on those two axes.

Equivalently framed: the degree of globalization in an industry depends on the benefits of **global integration** versus the benefits of **national differentiation**. The two operative questions are how important global scale economies are, and how different customer requirements are between countries. Jet engines sit high on integration benefits and low on differentiation; packaged grocery products sit the other way; telecommunications equipment demands both.

### 9.2 The four global strategy positions

| | **Low local responsiveness pressure** | **High local responsiveness pressure** |
|---|---|---|
| **High cost-reduction pressure** | **Global-standardization** — scale and location economies via global division of labor; price is the main weapon; minimal adaptation. *Infosys, Lenovo, Siemens Energy* | **Transnational** — "think globally, act locally"; best practices diffused everywhere plus local adaptation. Hard: duplicated effort, organizational complexity, cost. *ABB, Bertelsmann, P&G* |
| **Low cost-reduction pressure** | **International** — sell the same offering at home and abroad, leveraging home-based competencies; suits firms with large home markets and strong brands. *Harley-Davidson, Rolex, Starbucks* | **Multidomestic** — locals ideally perceive the product as local; costly and inefficient because business functions get duplicated per country. Common in consumer products and food. *Bridgestone, Nestlé, Philips* |

Positions are **dynamic**, not fixed: Google launched YouTube Red as an international strategy, moved to a multidomestic position with YouTube Premium, then planned a transnational position.

Industry-level evidence on fit:

| Industry | Character | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer electronics | Global industry | Matsushita (most integrated) most successful; Philips survived; GE exited |
| Branded packaged consumer goods | Substantial national differentiation, few global scale economies | Unilever and P&G most successful; Kao had limited success outside Japan |
| Telecommunications equipment | Requires **both** global integration and national differentiation | Ericsson most successful; NEC only partly; ITT exited |

### 9.3 Assessing and entering foreign markets

- **CAGE** identifies the distance types that create cost and risk: cultural, administrative/political, geographic, economic. It guides which countries to enter.
- **Entry modes** run along an investment-and-control continuum: contract-based (exporting) → strategic alliances (long-term contracts via licensing or franchising, then equity alliances, then joint ventures) → wholly owned subsidiary (acquisition or greenfield).
- **Advantages of international expansion:** access to new markets, access to lower-cost inputs, development of new competencies.
- **Disadvantages:** liability of foreignness, loss of reputation, loss of intellectual property.

### 9.4 Does location still matter?

Against the death-of-distance hypothesis, high performers in given industries remain concentrated in specific countries — US in biotech, software, internet; China and Taiwan in computer manufacturing; South Korea and Japan in consumer electronics; Australia in mining; India in business process outsourcing; Germany in engineering and cars; Italy in fashion; France in wine.

**Porter's Diamond** explains this with four mutually reinforcing determinants around national competitive advantage: **factor conditions**, **demand conditions**, **related and supporting industries/complementors**, and **competitive intensity in the focal industry**.

## 11. Closing Implications

- The conventional analysis — integrate whenever transacting costs more than administering — is **incomplete**. Two things it misses:
  1. Vertical strategies aren't make-or-buy binaries; there's a wide structural menu.
  2. The most important long-run consideration is **organizational capability**. To sustain competitive advantage a firm should restrict itself to activities where its capabilities beat those of firms that specialize in them.
- The hardest cases are those with **linkages between value chain activities**: a contract manufacturer may build the product more cheaply, but losing in-house manufacturing may damage new product development capability.
- Every vertical decision reduces to two questions: **which activities do we perform internally**, and **how do we design the arrangements** — with external *and* internal suppliers and buyers alike (an integrated firm can still use arm's-length negotiated internal contracts).
- Both answers depend on the firm's own competitive strategy and capabilities. The instruction is explicitly *not* to follow conventional wisdom or industry fashion, but to evaluate strategic needs, capabilities at each stage, the characteristics of the transactions involved, and the relative attractiveness of each stage of the chain.

### 1. Amazon — Retail Ad Service

**Source:** trade/news report on Amazon's Retail Ad Service beta (undated; beta announced early 2025). Original reporting credited to Adweek; AWS parallel credited to CNBC.

**What happened**

- Amazon is opening the ad technology behind its own advertising business — described as roughly a $50 billion business, third in US advertising behind Google and Meta — for other retailers to run on their own websites.
- The service places contextually targeted ads on third-party retailers' product, search, and browsing pages, using models trained on Amazon's accumulated shopping signals across two decades.
- Retailers adopting it are plugged into Amazon's existing advertiser base: brands already buying through Amazon can extend placements onto those third-party sites.
- Amazon captures retail-media profit even when the transaction happens off its own property, and gains visibility into additional data — a point flagged as likely to draw FTC interest.
- Explicitly framed as following the AWS pattern: infrastructure built to serve Amazon's own marketplace, later sold as a service to outside firms.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Economies of scope in resources/capabilities** | The ad-tech stack and the trained models are a single resource being applied across a second market at near-zero marginal cost — the textbook justification for widening scope |
| **Shared service organization (Grant, incentive problem)** | Grant's remedy for low-powered internal incentives is exposing internal suppliers to external competition. Amazon goes a step further: the internal supplier is turned into a standalone external business, which forces it to meet market standards |
| **The firm boundary moving outward** | Most course examples are about pulling activity *in*. This is the mirror image — an activity that was inside the boundary for internal reasons gets converted into a market offering, expanding scope without vertical integration into retailers |
| **Information asymmetry / data as a resource** | Access to rivals' shopping data is the strategic prize *and* the regulatory exposure |
| **Competitive effects of integration** | Adopting retailers hand a competitor visibility into their own demand — the same reluctance mechanism as Dreamworks pulling back from ABC after the Disney acquisition |

**Trap to watch:** this is *not* vertical integration. Amazon is not acquiring retailers or a stage of their value chain. Calling it forward or backward integration would be the "wrong concept, right-sounding language" error. The correct label is scope expansion through economies of scope / capability externalization.

### 2. Apple Weighs Using Anthropic or OpenAI to Power Siri

**Source:** Mark Gurman, Bloomberg, June 30, 2025.

**What happened**

- Apple is in talks with Anthropic and OpenAI about licensing large language models to power a rebuilt Siri, sidelining its in-house Apple Foundation Models. No final decision; an internal "LLM Siri" project on in-house models continues in parallel.
- Apple asked both firms for custom versions trained to run on Apple's own Private Cloud Compute servers — infrastructure built on high-end Apple silicon — on the argument that keeping inference on Apple-controlled hardware better protects user privacy.
- Evaluation was run by Siri chief Mike Rockwell and software head Craig Federighi after Siri was taken from AI chief John Giannandrea, who favors in-house model development. After multiple testing rounds they judged Anthropic's technology the most promising fit; corporate development VP Adrian Perica opened talks.
- Talks with Anthropic stalled on price: the article reports Anthropic seeking a multibillion-dollar annual fee escalating sharply year over year, which pushed Apple toward considering OpenAI or others.
- Apple has already committed a multibillion-dollar 2026 budget for running its own models in the cloud; plans past that are unsettled.
- Related moves: Apple considered acquiring Perplexity and briefly talked with Thinking Machines Lab. It killed Swift Assist (in-house coding models) and will instead let Xcode tap third-party models from OpenAI or Anthropic. On-device models remain in-house and are being opened to developers.
- Talent effects: the ~100-person foundation models team, under Ruoming Pang reporting to Daphne Luong, has reacted badly; senior LLM researcher Tom Gunter left after about eight years; the MLX team threatened to leave and was retained with counteroffers. Meta has floated packages reported between $10M and $40M for its Superintelligence Labs.
- Precedent cited: Samsung brands features as Galaxy AI while many run on Gemini; Amazon uses Anthropic to help power Alexa+.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Make-or-buy on a core input** | The classic decision, applied to the input Apple's executives consider most strategically important to how future products operate |
| **Build-Borrow-or-Buy** | All three branches visible at once: *build* (Apple Foundation Models, LLM Siri, on-device models), *borrow* (licensing Claude/ChatGPT — a long-term contract with high tradability), *buy* (Perplexity and Thinking Machines explored). The framework predicts borrow here: the resource is tradable, and integration capability for an AI-lab acquisition is doubtful |
| **Relevance test** | Executives judged their own models inferior — i.e. internal resources fail the "superior to competitors'" half of the relevance test, which pushes away from build |
| **Developing distinctive capabilities (Grant)** | Exactly the Xerox/Kodak/Philips vs. EDS/IBM/Accenture argument: specialists serving many customers learn faster than a captive internal group serving one |
| **Transaction-specific investment** | The requested custom model trained to run on Apple's Private Cloud Compute is specific to Apple — a genuine specific investment on the vendor's side, which is why the arrangement needs a long-term contract rather than spot purchasing |
| **Bilateral bargaining / no equilibrium price** | Few credible suppliers, one very large buyer, a bespoke product. There is no market price to reference, so the deal turns on relative bargaining power — and it stalled precisely there. Apple's counter-leverage is the existence of OpenAI and Gemini as alternatives |
| **Hold-up risk on Apple's side** | An escalating annual fee is the anticipated future extraction. Apple's stated intent to reclaim ownership of models "if its own technology improves" is a hedge against exactly that |
| **Core competence erosion (Prahalad & Hamel)** | The team's fear that outsourcing Siri portends outsourcing other features is the hollow-corporation risk stated from the inside. Talent departures accelerate it — the capability degrades as soon as the decision is signaled |
| **Architectural vs. component capabilities** | Apple's plausible position: retain architectural capability (integration into devices, on-device models, privacy infrastructure) while buying the component (the frontier model). Grant's aero-engine finding — outsource the component but keep R&D in it — maps onto Apple keeping on-device model development in-house |

**Trap to watch:** "Apple should build because AI is strategically important" is intuitive but incomplete. Strategic importance argues for *control*, not necessarily *ownership* — and the capability-development criterion (Table 13.1: the greater the need for continual capability investment, the greater the advantage of outsourcing) cuts the other way. The defensible answer names both forces and the ownership-of-architecture compromise.

### 3. Johnson Controls / Tyco Merger

**Source:** Bob Tita and Dana Mattioli, *The Wall Street Journal*, Jan. 25, 2016.

**What happened**

- Johnson Controls and Tyco International agreed to a $14 billion merger creating a commercial-building systems provider with over $30 billion in annual sales — Johnson Controls' HVAC for large buildings plus Tyco's security and fire-suppression lines.
- Johnson Controls shareholders take about 56% of the combined firm; it is renamed Johnson Controls PLC but keeps Tyco's Irish legal domicile, making it an inversion.
- Both firms had been shrinking first. Tyco fell from roughly $41 billion in sales as a conglomerate — with electronics components and surgical equipment segments — to about $10 billion. Johnson Controls shed automotive-parts units that had once produced more than two-thirds of revenue, under CEO Alex Molinaroli's push toward higher-margin businesses.
- Stated rationale: dissatisfaction, especially among activist investors, with sprawling conglomerates, combined with continued belief in combining businesses within the same or adjacent industries for market clout.
- Claimed synergies: at least $150 million annually in tax savings and at least $500 million in cost savings over the first three years. Effective tax rate expected at 18–19%; Tyco had paid 12% of profit in taxes over three years against Johnson Controls' 29% average.
- Deal origin: Tyco's George Oliver first approached about a smaller transaction involving Johnson Controls' system controls business, then escalated to a full merger. Leadership handoff staged — Molinaroli leads 18 months, then Oliver becomes CEO.
- Terms: Johnson Controls holders receive one share or $34.88 cash; Tyco holders slightly less than one share each. The 11% premium was below the 20% average for "merger of equals" deals the prior year. Heavily stock-structured, which cushioned market volatility.
- Comparable deals named: Pfizer–Allergan ($155 billion, with a split already contemplated) and Dow–DuPont (>$120 billion, to be broken into three sector-focused companies). DuPont CEO Edward Breen was Tyco's CEO during much of its breakup.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept                                                         | How it applies                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| --------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Rumelt's diversification types**                              | Tyco's trajectory runs unrelated (conglomerate) → dominant/related. Johnson Controls exiting auto parts does the same. The merged firm is closer to **related-constrained** — HVAC, security, and fire suppression share the building-systems customer, channel, and service competencies |
| **Diversification–performance inverted U**                      | This is the curve being traversed deliberately: both firms retreated from the low-performing unrelated end toward the peak at related diversification, then combined *within* that band                                                                                                   |
| **Economies of scope**                                          | The value claim is a complete equipment suite for building owners plus data services for power consumption and predictive maintenance — one customer relationship and one data platform spread across more product lines                                                                  |
| **Principal–agent problem / activist investors**                | Activists are the corrective mechanism named in the article; the slide deck's point that failure to create shareholder value invites a hostile takeover is the same discipline in a different form                                                                                        |
| **Table 13.1 — taxes and regulations as a cost of contracting** | Grant lists tax and regulatory treatment of transactions as a consideration in structuring firm boundaries. The inversion is that criterion driving the *legal* boundary rather than the operational one                                                                                  |
| **CAGE — administrative/political distance**                    | The Irish domicile is a pure administrative-distance play; note Tyco's board must meet in Ireland for major strategic decisions, a real governance cost of the structure                                                                                                                  |
| **Build-Borrow-or-Buy — the Buy branch**                        | Full acquisition chosen where closeness needs are high (integrated product suite) and the resources are not separately tradable. The staged CEO handoff is an integration-risk mitigation                                                                                                 |

**Trap to watch:** "bigger" and "more diversified" are not the same thing, and the article's own framing — bigger *but more focused* — is the point. A flawed analysis that reads this as increasing diversification has inverted the case.

### 4. Meta / Scale AI

**Source:** Cory Weinberg, *The Information*, June 10, 2025.

**What happened**

- Meta agreed to take a 49% stake in data-labeling firm Scale AI for $14.8 billion, structured so the cash goes to Scale's existing shareholders rather than into the company. Scale is valued at $28 billion including the investment, up from $13.8 billion the prior year.
- Scale CEO Alexandr Wang, 28, moves into a senior position at Meta running a new "superintelligence" lab, along with other senior Scale technical staff. Jason Droege, Scale's chief strategy officer and a former Uber Eats executive, was in discussions to take over as CEO.
- Existing Scale investors — Accel, Index Ventures, Founders Fund, Greenoaks among them — keep their holdings and take large paydays. Scale has raised more than $1.5 billion since its 2016 founding.
- The article attributes the structure and the sub-50% stake to regulatory caution: Meta had just come off a trial in which regulators argued its Instagram and WhatsApp acquisitions were anticompetitive. Meta had the cash to buy Scale outright.
- Motivation: Zuckerberg was dissatisfied with reception of Llama 4 and is aggressively recruiting AI researchers while restructuring AI operations after executive departures.
- Customer conflict: Scale's customers have included Alphabet (described as one of its largest), Microsoft, Amazon, Nvidia, and OpenAI. Meta is itself a significant customer and plans to increase data-labeling spend as part of the deal.
- Financials: roughly $870 million revenue last year, over $2 billion expected this year, about $150 million lost before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization; more than $900 million cash at year-end. It had missed sales and profit targets set with investors.
- Structural headwind noted: some large AI developers are starting to bring labeling work in-house.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Equity alliance on the make-or-buy continuum** | Textbook placement: partial ownership, greater commitment than a contract, "try before you buy," short of parent–subsidiary control. The Coca-Cola/Monster 18.5% stake is the course's analogue |
| **Why not acquire?** | Grant and the slides treat acquisition as the last resort. Here the binding constraint is regulatory rather than integration capability — an important variant to be able to name |
| **Quasi-backward integration** | Data labeling is an upstream input to Meta's model training. Meta is moving up its own value chain without full ownership |
| **Competitive effects of integration (the Disney–ABC mechanism)** | The clearest instance in the set. Scale's customers — Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon, Nvidia, OpenAI — now buy a critical input from a firm nearly half-owned by a direct competitor. Grant's prediction is that they become less willing to deal, which is precisely the open question the article raises |
| **Customers backward-integrating** | "Some big AI developers are bringing this work in house" is the same threat from the other side — Scale's stage of the chain is being internalized by its buyers, which weakens its standalone position and makes the alliance more attractive to it |
| **Acqui-hire / resource relevance** | The resource actually acquired is arguably Wang and the technical team, not the labeling operation. Under Build-Borrow-or-Buy, human capital has low tradability, which is what pushes past a simple contract toward equity |
| **Compounding risk** | Removing the founder from a company that had already missed targets, while its customer base has reason to defect, concentrates risk in the asset Meta just paid for |

**Trap to watch:** don't call this an acquisition. 49% is deliberately below control, the cash goes to shareholders rather than the balance sheet, and both facts are the analytically interesting part. Also resist treating regulatory avoidance as the *only* motive — the talent transfer and the guaranteed supply relationship stand on their own.

### 5. Nvidia Muscles Into GPU Cloud Market

**Source:** Anissa Gardizy, *The Information*, June 11, 2025.

**What happened**

- Nvidia launched DGX Cloud Lepton, a marketplace letting AI developers rent GPU servers directly from Nvidia, aggregating inventory across many cloud providers plus Nvidia's own.
- More than a dozen GPU cloud providers agreed to supply servers, including CoreWeave and Nebius. Jensen Huang announced at a Paris developer conference that AWS and Microsoft would be the first large clouds to participate.
- The providers face a bind: they see the service as competitive because it gives Nvidia direct customer relationships, but fear losing business to rivals who join if they abstain. One participating executive's summary was that it is better to <cite index="0-0">"play with [Nvidia] in the sandbox than not at all."</cite>
- Parasail declined, with CEO Mike Henry saying it competed with his own service and he did not want to lose control of his customers (which include Elicit and Rasa). He still understood why others joined: GPUs are expensive, so providers must maximize rental revenue, and the marketplace is another customer source.
- Customers must create an Nvidia Cloud Account to rent, mirroring AWS and Azure account structures, and manage rented compute through it.
- Nvidia is offering European VC portfolio companies up to $100,000 in credits to rent through the marketplace, bundled with its server-management software.
- Scale: Nvidia's AI cloud and software business is on pace for about $2 billion annually — roughly what CoreWeave (market cap over $75 billion) generated last year renting Nvidia chips. Nvidia has said cloud and software could someday reach $150 billion, rivaling AWS.
- History: DGX Cloud launched in 2023 targeting large enterprises like SAP and Genentech, and ran inside major clouds' own data centers rather than Nvidia's. AWS initially refused and later reversed. Nvidia pitched it as premium training cheaper than AWS, though prices often appear higher once its software is included. Lepton targets smaller developers and is built on technology from Lepton, a GPU cloud provider Nvidia bought earlier in the year for several hundred million dollars.
- Countervailing move: AWS and other large clouds are developing their own AI chips and pushing customers toward them.
- The DOJ has been investigating whether Nvidia abused its dominant position in chips and the software controlling them; the aggregation move may draw further attention.
- Nvidia's public position is that Lepton connects partners with customers and does not compete with them.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Forward vertical integration** | A component supplier moving downstream into the stage occupied by its own customers — the mirror of the Walmart case |
| **Mutual encroachment** | The clouds are backward-integrating into chip design while Nvidia forward-integrates into cloud. Each side's move is partly defensive against the other's, which is the sharpest illustration in the set that integration decisions are strategic responses, not just cost calculations |
| **Competitive effects of vertical integration** | Grant's mechanism, live: customers become less willing to deal with a supplier now perceived as a rival. Parasail's refusal is the pure case; the dozen who joined show why the mechanism often fails to bite — defection is individually costly when rivals participate |
| **Supplier bargaining power** | The reason resistance collapses. Providers depend on Nvidia for the input that *is* their business, so declining carries risk beyond the lost marketplace revenue. This is Five Forces supplier power creating the conditions for vertical encroachment |
| **Taper integration** | Nvidia sells its own inventory *and* aggregates partners' — partly integrated, partly relying on outside firms, exactly the taper definition. Also true of the 2023 DGX Cloud, which ran on other companies' data centers |
| **Buy, then build** | The Lepton acquisition supplied the technology; Build-Borrow-or-Buy with low tradability of a working marketplace platform |
| **Foreclosure and antitrust** | Grant's point that vertical foreclosure cases (Standard Oil, Alcoa) are rare doesn't mean regulators ignore them — the DOJ interest here is over leveraging a dominant position in chips and control software into an adjacent stage |
| **Switching costs / customer ownership** | The Nvidia Cloud Account is the mechanism. Whoever owns the account owns the relationship; Henry's objection is stated in exactly those terms |

**Trap to watch:** Nvidia's stated position — that it merely connects partners to customers and does not compete — is a claim to evaluate, not a fact to repeat. The account structure and direct customer relationships are the evidence against it. Equally, don't overstate: participants are voluntary and gain a demand channel, so this is not foreclosure in the Standard Oil sense.

### 6. Walmart, Dean Foods — Grocers Move Into Milk Bottling

**Source:** *The Wall Street Journal* (undated in the provided excerpt; events run through 2020, after Dean's November 2019 bankruptcy filing).

**What happened**

- Kroger, Walmart, and Albertsons built their own milk-bottling plants to supply store-brand milk, threatening national-brand processors in a roughly $40 billion US milk industry.
- Dean Foods, until recently the largest US milk processor by sales, and Borden Dairy both filed for bankruptcy (November and January respectively) and were sold. Executives of both blamed grocers' focus on cheap milk, frequently used as a loss leader. KKR and Capitol Peak Partners bought Borden out of bankruptcy.
- Dean was one of Walmart's largest milk suppliers for decades, and Walmart pressed continuously on cost. A Dean plant in Louisville re-engineered bottles from 60 grams to 57 grams, saving about half a cent per gallon. That plant processed up to 1.2 million gallons weekly, 70% of it going to 130 Walmart stores across four states, and prioritized Walmart during disruptions such as snowstorms.
- Around 2012 Walmart proposed Dean run a dedicated plant for Great Value store-brand milk; no agreement was reached. When milk prices fell in early 2015, Walmart expected Dean to cut prices; Dean refused, as CEO Gregg Tanner was consolidating regional brands (Garelick Farms, Mayfield Dairy) under the national DairyPure label and intended to hold branded pricing.
- Walmart's dairy managers believed shoppers increasingly failed to distinguish store brands from premium milks costing up to twice as much. Walmart dropped Dean's brand from some stores; Dean bought billboards near Walmarts hoping to pull demand through.
- Upstream, supply was consolidating: by 2017 the eight largest dairy cooperatives marketed 54% of US milk, making direct purchasing plus in-house processing look feasible to Walmart.
- March 2016: Walmart announced a processing plant in Fort Wayne, Indiana, to supply over 600 Walmart and Sam's Club stores across the eastern Midwest. Dean estimated it would lose roughly 100 million of the 2.6 billion gallons it sold annually; Tanner told investors performance wouldn't be hurt.
- Dean's successor CEO Ralph Scozzafava argued to Walmart that running a bottling plant was a poor use of the retailer's resources. Walmart proceeded.
- Mid-2017: DairyPure volume down an estimated 7.5% year over year; Dean closed a Virginia plant, then planned closures in Pennsylvania, Massachusetts, Minnesota, Michigan, Illinois, and Georgia. Rerouted deliveries created logjams.
- Early 2018: Food Lion (1,000+ East Coast stores) ended its Dean contract after pricing disagreements — analysts estimated another 50 million gallons a year — and began buying from Kroger, which processes roughly 90% of the milk its stores sell.
- March 2018: Dean terminated the contract of Joe Kelsay, a sixth-generation Indiana farmer whose 500 cows had supplied the Louisville plant for 25 years. More than a dozen potential buyers declined him; the one offer came at a price a fifth below Dean's. He and his brother sold the herd in November 2018.
- Walmart's stated rationale: supply-chain efficiency supporting everyday low prices, with its logistics cutting delivery time and extending shelf life.
- February 2019 the board explored a sale; November 2019 Dean filed for bankruptcy; February 2020 Dairy Farmers of America agreed to buy it. A DFA executive noted grocers running their own plants still need outside supply.
- Background demand pressure: per capita US milk consumption down about 40% over four decades, with substitution toward bottled water, juice, and almond/oat alternatives, and declining cereal consumption. About 3,300 dairy herds disappeared in 2019; Wisconsin lost roughly 600 in twelve months and led the nation in farm bankruptcies.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Backward vertical integration** | Walmart, a downstream retailer, takes ownership of the upstream bottling stage — the definitional case |
| **Transaction-specific investment and hold-up** | The Louisville plant sent 70% of output to 130 Walmart stores and prioritized Walmart during disruptions. That dependence is a specific investment in all but name, and the resulting hold-up ran in Walmart's favor — sustained price pressure Dean could not credibly refuse until it did, and then lost the business |
| **Bilateral dependence, asymmetric power** | Grant's bilateral monopoly has no equilibrium price and turns on relative bargaining power. This case shows the asymmetric version: one side has alternatives (many processors, and the option to integrate), the other has concentrated exposure |
| **Table 13.1 — number of firms in the adjacent stage** | Upstream cooperative consolidation (eight co-ops marketing 54% of US milk) is what made direct sourcing practical. Note the direction: it was the *supply* stage consolidating that opened Walmart's move, not the processing stage |
| **Optimal scale (the FedEx test, passing this time)** | FedEx shouldn't build vans because 40,000 units sits far below a 200,000-unit MES. Walmart clears the analogous bar: one plant serves 600+ stores, and milk ranks among its top-ten items. Scozzafava's argument that bottling was a poor use of Walmart's resources is the FedEx logic applied — and it failed on the numbers |
| **Low differentiation as the enabling condition** | Walmart's dairy buyers judged that shoppers no longer distinguished store brands from premium milk. Dean's DairyPure national-branding strategy was a differentiation bet that lost. Where the intermediate product is a commodity, integration is easy and the supplier's only defense — brand — is weak |
| **Loss leader / complementary demand** | Milk's role is drawing traffic that buys other items, which raises the strategic value of controlling its cost and availability well above its own margin |
| **Compounding risk** | Dean's plant-closure cascade created rerouting logjams at surviving plants — a linked-stage failure propagating, the GM brake-strike pattern in slow motion |
| **Kroger as the completed case** | Processing ~90% of the milk it sells, and selling to Food Lion — a retailer that has forward-integrated far enough to become a merchant supplier to rival retailers |
| **Five Forces overlay** | Buyer power (concentrated grocers, low switching costs, high volume per buyer), substitutes (water, juice, plant-based alternatives, declining cereal), and threat of entry-by-customer all compound. Dean's failure is not attributable to any single force |

**Trap to watch:** the article gives two independent causes of Dean's collapse — retailer backward integration *and* secular demand decline plus substitution. An analysis citing only one is incomplete, not wrong. Also note Dean's own strategic error (holding branded prices into a market that had stopped valuing the brand) is firm-level execution, distinct from industry-level structure.

| Johnson Controls / Tyco | Merger | High closeness needs for a combined product suite |

### C. Build-Borrow-or-Buy placement

| Case | Relevance of internal resources | Tradability of target resource | Closeness need | Predicted action | Actual action |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Apple / Siri models | Low (judged inferior) | High (licensable) | Moderate | Borrow — contractual | Borrow attempted; stalled on price |
| Meta / Scale | Low (Llama 4 disappointed) | Low (people, not IP) | High | Buy | Buy blocked by regulation → equity alliance |
| Nvidia / Lepton | Moderate | Low (working platform + team) | High | Buy | Buy |
| Walmart / bottling | High (logistics, scale, capital) | n/a | n/a | Build | Build |

The framework predicts three of four cleanly. Meta is the instructive miss: **Build-Borrow-or-Buy has no regulatory variable in it.** That's a real limitation worth being able to state.

### D. The competitive-effects mechanism, three ways

Grant's claim is that integration makes independent suppliers and customers less willing to deal with the integrated firm. The three cases show the mechanism's full range:

| Case | Predicted reluctance | Actually observed |
|---|---|---|
| Nvidia / GPU clouds | Providers refuse to supply a competitor | Mostly **overridden** — supplier power and fear of rival defection forced participation. Only Parasail refused |
| Meta / Scale | Rival AI labs reduce Scale spending | **Open question** at time of writing — the article flags it as the key risk |
| Walmart / Dean | Suppliers avoid an integrated buyer | **Irrelevant** — Walmart's scale meant suppliers had no leverage to withhold; DFA noted grocers with plants still need outside milk |

**Takeaway for a "what's wrong with this analysis" question:** the reluctance mechanism is real but conditional. It bites only when the counterparty has alternatives. Where the integrating firm holds the scarce input (Nvidia) or the scarce demand (Walmart), the predicted deterrent effect largely fails to materialize.

### 1. Amazon's Moonshot Plan to Rival Nvidia in AI Chips

**Source:** Matt Day, Ian King and Dina Bass, Bloomberg, Nov. 24, 2024.

**What happened**

- Amazon engineers in north Austin are building custom AI silicon aimed at loosening Nvidia's hold on a market described as worth over $100 billion. The stated goal is not raw performance but a simple, reliable system that turns AWS data centers into large AI machines.
- The pattern is long-running: Amazon built its own data centers but originally filled them with other firms' servers and switches. James Hamilton led the replacement of those with custom hardware, reasoning that buying millions of units justified tailoring them and stripping out features AWS didn't need. Reducing dependence on incumbents — Intel first, now Nvidia — is the through-line.
- Hamilton proposed in-house chip design to Bezos in August 2013 and recommended the Arm architecture on the bet that its ubiquity and developer familiarity could displace Intel in server rooms. Amazon commissioned processors from the startup Annapurna Labs, then acquired it two years later for a reported $350 million.
- Product sequence: **Nitro** (a card that stripped control, security and networking functions off the server itself), **Graviton** (Arm general-purpose CPU, cheaper than Intel equivalents, and a volume large enough to make Amazon a top-ten TSMC customer), **Inferentia** (inference, shipped December 2019, later used for Alexa), **Trainium1** and repackaged **Inferentia2**, now **Trainium2**.
- Trainium2 is the third AI generation, which the article frames as the industry's make-or-break point — a chip-veteran source says he has never seen a product break the three-generation rule.
- Design changes for Trainium2: four times the performance and three times the memory of its predecessor, most cabling replaced with printed circuit boards, and chips per box cut from eight to two so that maintenance takes down fewer units. Amazon began testing by mounting prior-generation chips on the new board rather than waiting for TSMC to deliver working silicon.
- Target cadence is a new chip roughly every 18 months, partly by reducing trips to outside vendors — its own oscilloscopes for testing, its own lab work, plumbing pre-installed for future liquid cooling.
- The binding constraint is software, not hardware. Nvidia's strength is a comprehensive tool suite that lets customers run projects with little customization; Amazon's Neuron SDK is described as immature by comparison. Even a clean port can consume hundreds of engineer-hours in verification.
- Demand-side scaffolding: Amazon encourages large customers and partners to adopt the chips when signing or renewing AWS deals. Databricks agreed to use Trainium under a broad AWS agreement, citing a claimed 30% better price-performance. Anthropic agreed to use Trainium for future development after taking $4 billion from Amazon, followed by a further $4 billion (roughly $8 billion total backing), while continuing to use Nvidia and Google hardware.
- Rivals are doing the same. Google began AI chip work about a decade ago and later sold access to cloud customers including Anthropic, Cohere and Midjourney, adding its first CPU in April. Microsoft entered later with the Maia accelerator and Cobalt CPU, then added a security chip and a data processing unit.
- All three cloud giants continue to praise Nvidia and compete for allocation of its new chips.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Backward vertical integration to reduce supplier power** | The cleanest stated motive in either article set: a buyer integrating upstream specifically because the supplier is scarce, expensive and rationing output. Nvidia had just told investors demand would outstrip supply for several quarters |
| **The Grant semiconductor finding, inverted** | Grant reports that integrated design-plus-fabrication producers (Intel, ST Micro) outperform when the circuit is technically complex enough to require designer–fabricator collaboration. Amazon integrates **design** and outsources **fabrication** to TSMC — fabless. The MES logic explains why: leading-edge fabs are among the most scale-intensive assets in industry, far beyond even Amazon's internal volume |
| **Scale justifying integration** | Hamilton's original argument for custom servers is the FedEx test passed: buying millions of units puts Amazon above the threshold where tailoring beats purchasing |
| **Architectural vs. component capabilities** | The software gap is the whole story. Amazon can build the component (silicon) but the value depends on the surrounding toolchain that makes it usable — the architectural layer where Nvidia's advantage sits |
| **Buy, then build** | Annapurna at $350M supplied the design capability; every subsequent chip is internal development. Build-Borrow-or-Buy predicts *buy* here — chip design teams have low tradability |
| **Equity alliance as demand guarantee** | The Anthropic investments buy a committed anchor customer that will stress the chips and improve them — a vertical relationship where the equity flows *downstream* to secure adoption rather than upstream to secure supply |
| **Mutual encroachment (pairs with Part 1 Nvidia article)** | Read alongside the Nvidia/Lepton piece, this is the same conflict from the other side. Nvidia moves forward into cloud partly because its biggest customers are moving backward into chips. Neither move is comprehensible in isolation |
| **Competitive parity, not advantage** | Note the honest ceiling: analysts expect no displacement of Nvidia soon, and success is defined as absorbing internal workloads plus occasional large-customer projects, freeing scarce Nvidia supply for others. That is a cost and supply-security play, not a claim to sustained competitive advantage |

**Trap to watch:** "Amazon is vertically integrating into chips" is only half true. It integrates design, not manufacture, and the distinction is the whole analysis — the make-or-buy answer differs stage by stage within what looks like one activity. An analysis that treats "chips" as a single undifferentiated stage misses this.

### 2. Apple & Globalstar — Escalation Along the Continuum

**Sources:** Dan Gallagher, *The Wall Street Journal*, Sept. 9, 2022; Mark Gurman, Bloomberg, Dec. 10, 2024.

**What happened**

*Stage one (September 2022):*
- Apple confirmed that iPhone 14 models could connect directly to satellites via Emergency SOS, messaging from areas outside cellular coverage. Free to buyers for two years, with no stated price afterward.
- Globalstar disclosed that it operates the service under a partnership in which **Apple covers 95% of Globalstar's capital expenditures** to build the network, including new satellites, and Globalstar **allocates 85% of its current and future network capacity** to the service. An analyst characterized this as converting an underutilized asset into a productive one in a single move.
- With service fees and potential bonus payments, Globalstar projected revenue of $185–230 million for the following year and $250–310 million for 2026, the first full year with all new satellites operational. Even the low end would be a record — about 44% above its three-year average. Globalstar's filing called the deal transformational.
- Market reaction was mixed: shares fell 19% the following day, partly on an Elon Musk post claiming promising Apple conversations about Starlink, and partly because the service is emergency texting only — the phone must be pointed at a satellite, and light foliage can stretch a message to minutes. The stock still stood 17% above its level in late August 2021, when the rumor first surfaced.
- Competitive context: SpaceX and T-Mobile announced a text service; Iridium disclosed a development agreement with an unnamed company. An analyst described the addressable market for off-the-grid satellite-to-smartphone connectivity as large with room for several initiatives.

*Stage two (December 2024):*
- Apple invested about **$1.5 billion in Globalstar in November and took a roughly 20% stake**.
- Apple planned to extend satellite texting to the 2025 Apple Watch Ultra, its $799 top model — the first mainstream smartwatch with the capability. Globalstar shares rose about 15% on the report.
- The satellite feature had already widened in stages: emergency services (2022), roadside assistance (2023), then general iMessage texting.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Escalation along the make-or-buy continuum** | The single best illustration in either set of a relationship *moving* rather than sitting still: supply contract → funded capex plus capacity commitment → minority equity stake. Each step raises commitment as the specific investment grows |
| **Transaction-specific investment, both directions** | Globalstar's new satellites are built for Apple's service; 85% of current *and future* capacity is committed to it. Apple's 95% capex funding is equally specific. Neither asset has much value without the other — Grant's steel-and-strip structure, in orbit |
| **Bilateral monopoly** | One buyer, one seller, purpose-built assets, no market price to reference. The governance response follows the theory exactly: not a spot contract, not full acquisition, but progressively deeper commitment |
| **Why not acquire outright?** | Apple gets effective control of capacity (85%) and economics without owning a regulated satellite operator — a strategically dissimilar business with different capabilities, planning systems and regulatory exposure. Grant's "managing strategically different businesses" cost is the reason to stop at 20% |
| **Hold-up risk, and the equity stake as the answer** | Once Apple has funded 95% of the network, Globalstar has enormous leverage at renewal — and Apple has enormous leverage over a company that has committed 85% of its capacity to one customer. Equity converts the standoff into shared ownership of the upside, the same logic as Commonwealth Bank taking a stake in EDS Australia or oilfield service firms taking project equity |
| **Underutilized asset → productive asset** | The value creation is not new capability but higher utilization of existing capacity — an economies-of-scale story on the supplier's side, which is what makes the 85% commitment tolerable to Globalstar |
| **Alternatives as bargaining power** | Starlink/T-Mobile and Iridium are what keep Apple from being fully captive. The 19% share drop on a single Musk post is the market pricing that substitution risk in real time |
| **Substitute threat created downstream** | Putting satellite texting on the Watch reduces the reason to carry a standalone device such as Garmin's inReach — Apple's vertical relationship upstream produces a substitution threat in a neighboring product market |

**Trap to watch:** the 20% stake is not a control position and this is not an acquisition. Also resist reading the 2022 stock drop as evidence the deal was bad for Globalstar — the same article notes record projected revenue and a share price well above pre-rumor levels. Price movement on a competitor's announcement measures *substitution risk*, not deal quality.

### 3. Apple's Component Sourcing Strategy

**Source:** Mark Gurman, Bloomberg, Dec. 10, 2024 (same article as above).

**What happened**

- At least some Apple Watch models are moving from Intel cellular modems to MediaTek versions — the first time Apple uses a major component from MediaTek, a supplier it had evaluated for more than half a decade. It further reduces reliance on Intel, which had previously supplied Mac processors.
- Separately, Apple is developing its own modem for the iPhone and other devices, moving away from Qualcomm, though there are no current plans to use it in the Watch.
- The MediaTek move brings support for 5G RedCap, a lower-tier 5G tier aimed at connected devices and wearables that don't need fast data. Apple Watches had stayed on 4G LTE even after the iPhone moved to 5G in 2020.
- Product context: Wearables, Home and Accessories revenue had declined for two years, partly because customers had little reason to upgrade. The Series 10 was thinner with a larger screen but not a major departure, and the Ultra went un-updated in 2024. Satellite texting is meant to justify the $799 Ultra over the $399 Series 10 or $249 SE.
- Apple is also building a blood-pressure feature modelled on its sleep apnea detector — flagging possible hypertension rather than giving systolic or diastolic readings — and continues work on noninvasive blood glucose. Samsung already sells blood-pressure capability, with accuracy questions and a calibration requirement.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Supplier switching as an alternative to integration** | Moving Intel → MediaTek achieves reduced dependence without building anything. The cheapest response to supplier power is a credible second source, not ownership |
| **Selective backward integration** | The in-house iPhone modem is genuine backward integration, but deliberately *not* applied to the Watch. Apple integrates where volume and strategic weight justify it and buys where they don't — the make-or-buy decision made per product line, not per firm |
| **Multi-sourcing vs. single-supplier arrangements** | Grant's "recent trends" describe a broad shift from multi-sourcing to single-supplier collaboration. Apple runs the opposite play on modems: qualify alternatives, then integrate. Evidence that the trend is a tendency, not a rule |
| **Product differentiation as the demand-side motive** | The satellite feature exists to restore an upgrade reason after two years of declining wearables revenue — vertical relationship decisions (Globalstar) driven by a business-strategy problem (differentiation and price laddering across SE / Series / Ultra) |
| **Product scope expansion** | Health monitoring pushes Apple toward a regulated adjacent category. The design of the blood-pressure feature — flagging a state rather than reporting numbers — is a scope decision made to stay on the safe side of a regulatory boundary |

**Trap to watch:** MediaTek replacing Intel is *not* vertical integration; nothing crossed Apple's boundary. Only the in-house modem is. Conflating supplier substitution with integration is the exact "two related-but-distinct concepts merged" error.

### 4. CoreWeave / Core Scientific

**Sources:** Lauren Thomas and Ben Dummett, *The Wall Street Journal*, June 26, 2025; Sam Reynolds, CoinDesk, June 27, 2025.

**What happened**

- CoreWeave is in advanced talks to acquire Core Scientific, potentially closing within weeks, after an unsuccessful attempt about a year earlier.
- Core Scientific is among the largest owners and operators of high-powered digital infrastructure for bitcoin mining and hosting in North America. Market value near $3.7 billion at the time, after the share price had fallen roughly 15% year to date; shares jumped over 20% on the report and closed above $16, up 33%.
- CoreWeave provides AI cloud infrastructure, essentially renting access to Nvidia GPUs to firms including Meta and IBM. It went public in March with a market value around $75 billion. **Microsoft accounted for 62% of its 2024 revenue** per its IPO filing.
- The prior relationship is the key fact: in 2024 the two signed a series of **12-year contracts** under which Core Scientific would deliver hundreds of megawatts to host CoreWeave's operations, generating billions in revenue. Cantor Fitzgerald describes it as a 12-year, $3.5 billion infrastructure lease for 200 megawatts of AI capacity.
- CoreWeave's rejected 2024 bid was $5.75 per share, valuing the business north of $1 billion; Core Scientific declined as too low and said it was focused on the existing partnership. The stock later traded at more than double that offer.
- Cantor argued the shares could exceed $30 — valuing the lease stream at $24 per share on a conservative 15x profit multiple typical of data center REITs, plus $11.70 per share for the replacement value of 570MW of power infrastructure — and that even the post-jump price undervalued the company by at least 50%.
- Sector context: bitcoin miners are repositioning as AI infrastructure providers amid a data-center space and power shortage. Rittenhouse Research argued mining-built infrastructure is better applied to AI, since AI produces stable long-term cash flows while mining faces revenue drops every four years at halvings, depends on volatile bitcoin prices, and requires ever-more-efficient chips as gains from silicon shrinkage plateau. Galaxy Digital's late-2022 purchase of the Helios data center, initially read as rescuing a struggling miner, became a strategic AI asset.
- Not all pivots worked: Bit Digital's shift to Ethereum staking sent its stock down 15% in a session; Canaan shuttered its AI chip unit after failing to gain traction, with shares down nearly 75% in six months.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Long-term contract converting into ownership** | The textbook sequence, compressed into roughly a year: partnership → 12-year lease with hundreds of megawatts committed → rejected acquisition → acquisition. Grant's argument is that long-term contracts cannot anticipate all circumstances over their life; a 12-year commitment in a market this volatile is precisely where that bites |
| **Transaction-specific investment** | Power infrastructure configured for a specific tenant, at a specific site, on a 12-year term. Both parties are locked in — Core Scientific to the tenant, CoreWeave to the capacity |
| **The bargaining problem, priced** | The valuation gap between $5.75 and $30+ per share for the same assets one year apart is what "no equilibrium price, only relative bargaining power" looks like in a bilateral situation. Core Scientific's refusal in 2024 and CoreWeave's return are pure bargaining sequence |
| **Table 13.1 — uncertainty over the relationship's period** | Extreme uncertainty over costs, technology and demand across a 12-year horizon is listed as increasing the advantage of integration. This case follows the criterion almost mechanically |
| **Customer concentration as the other exposure** | 62% of CoreWeave's 2024 revenue from Microsoft. CoreWeave is securing supply upstream while remaining acutely dependent downstream — an asymmetry worth naming in any analysis of its position |
| **Asset redeployment and economies of scope** | Mining rigs and AI training are different workloads, but power interconnects, cooling and land are common assets. The pivot is an economies-of-scope story about the *infrastructure*, not the *computation* — which is why Canaan's chip-unit pivot failed while Core Scientific's succeeded |
| **Position in the wider chain** | CoreWeave rents Nvidia GPUs, participates in Nvidia's Lepton marketplace (Part 1), and is now buying its own power infrastructure. It is simultaneously integrating backward and being encroached upon from above |

**Trap to watch:** two distinct valuation arguments are stacked in the Cantor note — capitalized contract cash flows *and* replacement value of physical assets. These are alternative methods, not additive components in any rigorous sense, and one is a sell-side estimate rather than a market price. Treating "$30+" as a fact rather than a forecast is the error.

### 5. UPS Healthcare and Labport

**Source:** Cailley LaPara, Bloomberg, Jan. 6, 2025.

**What happened**

- UPS has been building a health-care logistics business to offset stagnating core parcel revenue. UPS Healthcare was a $10 billion business at end-2023, roughly a tenth of total revenue, with a goal to double by 2026 through acquisitions and new ventures.
- Margins are the point: overnight refrigerated shipment of injectable medicines commands a large premium. An analyst framed it as potentially $300 on a shipment costing the same to deliver as a box of socks. Health-care operating margin is in the high teens against about 10% for UPS overall in 2023.
- **Labport** is the newest step: about 100,000 square feet of custom laboratory space near the main Louisville air-cargo hub, completed at end-2024, with all available space already leased for 10-plus years. Tenants operating on UPS property can process test results within hours. An attached 182,000-square-foot warehouse offers storage and shipping space. Roughly 15–20 UPS employees staff a visible command center tracking shipments.
- The unit is visibly separating itself from the parent brand — employees wear navy blue rather than UPS brown.
- CEO Carol Tomé frames health care as a specialized component of the UPS system: blood tests and vaccines require close location monitoring, temperature control and occasional interception of mishandled packages, none of which a network moving 22 million packages a day is built for. Her ambition is to be the world's leading complex health-care logistics provider.
- History: a health-care campus near Louisville in the mid-2000s, then specialized distribution centers globally, then a formal business unit in 2019 — timing that proved fortunate when the pandemic and vaccine rollout drove cold-chain demand.
- Anchor customer: HealthTrackRx, a molecular diagnostics firm, consolidated lab operations to Louisville in 2022 to be near UPS, which moves about 90% of its test samples. Specimens collected nationwide fly to Kentucky, are processed overnight and returned to physicians by morning. Condensing operations around UPS helped cut test prices to about a quarter of pre-pandemic levels. Labport will double its Louisville footprint to around 200 workers, and it is considering having UPS assemble testing kits, work currently split among vendors.
- Expansion is likely — possibly more Louisville space or sites near other freight hubs such as Cologne. UPS has bought several cold-chain and health-care logistics companies recently, mostly in Europe. An analyst notes organic volume growth is insufficient to hit the 2026 target, so more acquisitions are required.
- Competition: FedEx signed health-care deals tied to more than $500 million in FY2024 revenue and opened a Netherlands cooling facility; DHL is investing $200 million in US warehouses and technology for health-care and life sciences. The market is projected to more than double from 2023 to 2032, approaching nearly $200 billion, driven by ageing populations, chronic disease and refrigerated medicines such as Ozempic and Zepbound.
- A UPS executive cautions that equipment, infrastructure and labor costs mean health care won't be a margin windfall, but it is stable, has sticky customers, and those customers buy more ancillary services than others.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Related-constrained diversification** | Health-care logistics shares the network, hubs, aircraft and tracking systems with parcel delivery but requires distinct capabilities layered on top. On Rumelt's scheme this is related diversification — the peak of the inverted-U — not conglomerate expansion |
| **Economies of scope** | The Louisville air hub is a fixed asset already built for parcels; the health-care business monetizes its proximity a second time. Labport is literally selling access to that scope advantage as real estate |
| **Co-location without common ownership — Grant's exact point** | The sharpest instance in either article set. HealthTrackRx moved its labs to be physically adjacent to UPS's hub because of the technical economies of proximity. Grant's argument is that such economies justify **co-location, not common ownership** — and here the parties do exactly that: a lease, not a merger. Compare the steel/strip case, where co-location did lead to integration, and ask what differs (specificity of the assets and the number of alternative counterparties) |
| **Forward integration into the customer's operations** | Kit assembly, currently done by separate vendors, is the step where UPS would cross from logistics into its customer's process. The 10-year leases and the command center are intermediate degrees of that movement |
| **Build-Borrow-or-Buy** | The article states plainly that the 2026 target cannot be reached organically. Low relevance of internal resources in specialized cold chain plus low tradability of operating capability points to **buy** — which matches the recent European acquisitions |
| **Strategically different businesses** | Grant's warning about managing dissimilar businesses shows up as an organizational signal: a separate unit, separate leadership, even a different uniform color. The brand separation is a *mitigation* of the administrative cost of internal dissimilarity |
| **Switching costs and stickiness** | A customer that has physically relocated its labs adjacent to your hub and signed a 10-year lease has enormous switching costs. UPS is manufacturing lock-in through geography rather than contract |
| **Business-level positioning** | Note this is also a differentiation move away from commodity ground shipping, driven by consumers trading down to cheaper delivery and B2B volume falling — corporate scope change as a response to a business-strategy problem |

**Trap to watch:** it is tempting to call this vertical integration into health care. It isn't — UPS is not entering diagnostics or pharmaceuticals; it is diversifying into an adjacent *service market* using a shared asset base. The kit-assembly idea is the only genuinely vertical element, and it is still hypothetical.

### 6. American Giant's $12.98 Walmart T-Shirt

**Source:** Suzanne Kapner, *The Wall Street Journal*, Dec. 30, 2024.

**What happened**

- Walmart is selling US-made cotton T-shirts at $12.98, produced by American Giant, in a market where less than 4% of apparel bought in America in 2023 was made domestically.
- CEO Bayard Winthrop's stated cause is not tariffs but **Walmart's scale combined with guaranteed orders**. Without a commitment to buy a predetermined quantity over time, he says suppliers would not have had confidence to invest in the automation and upgrades that cut production costs. A Walmart spokesman confirmed the retailer signed what amounted to **noncancelable purchase orders**.
- Walmart pledged in 2013 to buy more US-made, grown or assembled goods and raised the commitment in 2021, promising billions more annually through 2030.
- American Giant's own vertical scope: it buys yarn grown, spun, dyed and sewn in the US, contracting mainly with Southeastern suppliers; **owns** a cutting and sewing facility in Middlesex, N.C.; and is **part owner** of an LA sewing facility opened specifically for the Walmart order.
- Cost engineering: shirts knitted tubular with no side seam to reduce sewing; shorter-fiber cotton yarn, cheaper with a more rugged hand; machine-affixed flag patches; automated chest printing. The company and suppliers spent about $1 million on machinery and hired 75 people for the LA facility.
- Pricing was set from data Walmart supplied forecasting unit volumes at various price points, letting American Giant tell partners whether the run would be ten shirts or a million.
- Result: shirts in 1,700 Walmart stores by July 4, competing against 100% cotton shirts at half the price that carried no American emblems — Walmart bars suppliers from using "American Made" or the flag on goods not made in the US. Sweatshirts followed at $38.98, against a comparable American Giant hoodie retailing at $148 direct. Its own T-shirts sell for $40–60.
- Winthrop's summary of the constraint: almost anything can be made domestically provided it isn't labor-intensive. Apparel resists full automation — fabric is still mostly sewn by people.
- Countervailing evidence: 23 US textile plants closed in the previous 18 months; a research director notes stated preference for American-made goods often fails to convert into purchases, especially with inflation-squeezed shoppers.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **The hold-up problem solved by contract design** | The central lesson. Suppliers would not make transaction-specific investments in automation without protection. The noncancelable purchase order is the security device — Grant's explanation of why long-term contracts exist, stated almost verbatim by a practitioner |
| **Direct contrast with Walmart / Dean Foods (Part 1)** | Same buyer, opposite governance outcome. With Dean, Walmart used its power to squeeze, then integrated and destroyed the supplier. With American Giant, it used the same power to *underwrite* supplier investment. The difference is what Walmart wanted: with milk it wanted the lowest possible commodity cost and could produce it itself; with US-made apparel it wants a capability it has no intention of building, so it must make the supplier viable. **Buyer power can be used to expropriate or to guarantee, and the choice depends on whether the buyer intends to integrate** |
| **Information sharing as a vendor-partnership feature** | Walmart handed over demand forecasts by price point. Grant identifies extensive knowledge sharing as the distinguishing feature of Japanese-style vendor partnerships; this is the same mechanism reducing information asymmetry rather than exploiting it |
| **Taper integration on the supplier's side** | American Giant owns one sewing facility outright, part-owns a second, and contracts the rest of the chain. Partial ownership plus market purchasing — the taper definition |
| **Joint venture for a specific relationship** | The LA facility was opened specifically to fulfill this order, with shared ownership. A dedicated asset created for one customer relationship is transaction-specific investment given an equity structure to protect it |
| **Location economies and their limits** | Automation substitutes for the labor-cost gap only where the process is automatable. Apparel's residual manual sewing sets the boundary — a concrete illustration that location economies operate activity-by-activity, not industry-by-industry |
| **Differentiation as the pricing mechanism** | The shirts cost roughly double the competing plain shirts. What justifies the premium is the flag and the "American Made" label, which Walmart polices as a credible signal by barring their use on imports. Differentiation is being manufactured through *enforced certification*, not product attributes |

**Trap to watch:** tariffs are the obvious explanation and the article explicitly rejects it. An analysis crediting trade policy uses a true macro fact to explain the wrong mechanism — the causal factor named by the participant is contractual commitment, which is a governance variable, not a policy one.

### 7. Starbucks Pares Its Hedging Programme

**Source:** Gregory Meyer, *Financial Times*, Nov. 24, 2024.

**What happened**

- Starbucks held **less than $200 million** in fixed-price contracts for green (unroasted) coffee at its September fiscal year end, down from **$1 billion as recently as 2019** — a sharp reduction in price cover during a rally.
- Market conditions: benchmark futures passed $3 a pound in New York, a 13-year high, after gaining more than 70% over twelve months, with supply deficits following poor crops in Brazil and elsewhere. The USDA had just cut its Brazil forecast citing irregular rainfall and high temperatures.
- Scale: Starbucks buys about **3% of the world's coffee** to supply 40,000 cafés and retail businesses. It said in 2021 it bought 800 million pounds annually — roughly $2.4 billion at then-current benchmark prices.
- Structure: purchasing is run from Lausanne by a subsidiary, **Starbucks Coffee Trading Company**, headed by Andres Berron. It has **56 "tier one" suppliers** ranging from global trading houses (Louis Dreyfus, Olam) to farmer cooperatives.
- Contract types: fixed-price and **price-to-be-fixed**, the latter establishing quantity, delivery month and a premium to the New York futures market with the final price agreed later. A trader familiar with operations says most purchases use price-to-be-fixed. Starbucks also uses derivatives against market swings on those.
- Positions at year end: $1.1 billion in green coffee purchase obligations; price-to-be-fixed contracts worth $929 million (up year over year but well below 2021–22); derivatives worth $154 million, the lowest September value since 2020; combined unroasted and roasted inventory about $920 million, the lowest fiscal year-end figure since 2021.
- Starbucks says its purchasing approach is unchanged, that it is remaining agile, that current priced coverage is slightly below its typical 9–18 month range, and that its physical inventory cushions spot-market volatility. In 2021 then-CEO Kevin Johnson said it bought 12–18 months ahead, had locked 14 months, and claimed the approach was a competitive advantage over rivals buying nearer term.
- Analyst reaction: substantially less hedged than before, making the next twelve months of coffee prices unusually consequential. A trader notes roasters generally let coverage decline in fast rallies; CFTC data shows commercial traders sharply reducing arabica purchase contracts. A commodities analyst argues that in a rising-price environment you want to be hedged.
- New CEO Brian Niccol is early in a turnaround plan focused on reviving café sales.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Governance by contract where the market is thick** | Coffee is a commodity with many suppliers, transparent prices and low switching costs — Grant's conditions for market contracting. Starbucks buys 3% of world output and still does not own farms, exactly as few flour millers own wheat farms. **Volume alone does not justify backward integration** |
| **Allocation of risk — Grant's first design variable** | The entire article is about who bears price risk and for how long. Fixed-price contracts move risk to the supplier; price-to-be-fixed keeps quantity certainty while leaving price open; derivatives buy protection separately. Three instruments, three risk splits, same physical transaction |
| **Spot vs. long-term contracts** | Price-to-be-fixed is a hybrid: long-term on quantity and delivery, spot-like on price. Useful for showing that "long-term contract" is not one thing — different terms can be locked on different horizons |
| **Backward integration into the *function*, not the *stage*** | Starbucks Coffee Trading Company is a wholly owned procurement and trading subsidiary. Starbucks internalized the trading expertise while continuing to buy the physical commodity from others — integration into a capability rather than a production stage |
| **Supplier portfolio structure** | 56 tier-one suppliers spanning trading houses to cooperatives is deliberate multi-sourcing. Compare Grant's "recent trends" toward single-supplier arrangements: for a volatile commodity input, breadth is the risk control |
| **Uncertainty and the limits of contracting** | Table 13.1 says greater uncertainty about costs and demand raises the advantage of integration. Here uncertainty is extreme but integration is still not the answer — because the uncertainty is *price* uncertainty in a liquid market, which financial instruments handle better than ownership. Worth being precise about: not all uncertainty pushes the same direction |
| **Inventory as an alternative to hedging** | Starbucks points to physical stocks as a cushion. Holding inventory, holding derivatives and holding fixed-price contracts are substitute risk-management technologies with different cost structures — and its inventory is also at a multi-year low, which weakens the defense |

**Trap to watch:** the company's claim that its approach is unchanged sits against three simultaneous multi-year lows — fixed-price contracts, derivatives value, and inventory. The disciplined observation is that each individual instrument might be defensible, but the article's own numbers show *all* the cushions thinning at once. That is a "conclusion contradicted by the article's own figures" setup.

### 8. Guardian Bikes and Made-in-China Parts

**Source:** Natasha Khan, *The Wall Street Journal*, Jan. 6, 2025.

**What happened**

- Brian Riley moved production of Guardian Bikes, a children's bike brand, from China to a factory in Seymour, Indiana, in 2022. Nearly all parts still come from China.
- Structural obstacle: almost all bicycles sold in the US are imported, most made in China or assembled from Chinese parts. A typical bicycle has 30–40 parts, most from different Chinese manufacturers, in a supply chain Chinese firms have dominated for decades.
- Current position: Chinese components represent about **90% of Guardian's total parts cost**, which Riley hopes to reduce to roughly **20%** by the end of the following year. Guardian is starting frame production in-house using American steel and sourcing grips and reflectors domestically; American-made parts could then be about 60% of cost. It has asked its Brazilian rim supplier to consider a facility in Indiana near the factory, and is also considering making rims itself. Labor-intensive parts such as hubs and cranks are harder to source domestically.
- The original motive was quality control, not cost: Riley wanted a bike that resisted head-over-heels braking accidents. Bikes sold through mass retailers often ship unassembled, leaving manufacturers without oversight of final assembly. His argument is that controlling every aspect of production guarantees quality on every unit.
- Positioning: Guardian sells direct-to-consumer at $149–399, between mass-market and bike-shop premium brands.
- Location logic: Indiana allowed two-day shipping to most of the US and sat near steel mills for eventual frame production. Skilled workers became available because a local ironing-board manufacturer had closed.
- Learning curve: workers initially assembled 100 bikes a day; constant assembly-line adjustment raised output, and the 250-person factory can now produce up to 2,700 a day. Labor costs rose sharply but were partly offset by cheaper freight on components versus mostly assembled bikes. Riley describes accepting losses for a period to reach the other side of the strategy.
- Trade policy: tariffs on assembled children's bikes from China rose from 11% to 36% in Trump's first term, but assembled bikes were later exempted — meaning Guardian paid duties on imported parts while competitors importing finished bikes did not. The exemption was lifted in 2024, making Guardian's costs more comparable.
- Alternatives chosen by others: Prevelo ($240–1,400 bikes) moved production to Taiwan after 2018 tariffs, then expanded to Thailand and Cambodia, its CEO saying he cannot build the highly specialized, expensive factories domestically and that reshoring needs an industry-wide movement. Giant Group, which makes about four million bikes a year, opened a Vietnam factory with 100,000-bike capacity to access lower tariffs, alongside facilities in China and Europe.

**Framework mapping**

| Concept | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Vertical integration for quality assurance** | The stated motive is not cost but monitoring. Where quality is hard to specify and verify in a contract — and where final assembly happens outside anyone's oversight — internalizing the activity is the response. This is the *incomplete contracting / monitoring cost* branch of transaction cost economics, distinct from the specific-investment branch |
| **The scope of "reshoring" is activity-specific** | Assembly moved; components didn't. The 90% → 20% target measures how much of the *chain*, not the *product*, has relocated. A clean demonstration that make-or-buy and where-to-make are decided stage by stage |
| **Absence of a supporting industry cluster** | Porter's Diamond, in the negative. Guardian has factor conditions (steel mills nearby, workers freed by a local closure) and demand conditions, but the *related and supporting industries* corner is empty — hubs and cranks have no domestic supply base. Prevelo's point that reshoring requires an industry-wide movement is the cluster argument stated as a constraint |
| **Co-location invitation, not acquisition** | Asking the Brazilian rim supplier to build in Indiana is the same move as HealthTrackRx relocating to Louisville: seek the technical/logistical economies of proximity without common ownership. Guardian is simultaneously considering making rims itself — the make-or-buy decision live and unresolved on a single component |
| **Minimum efficient scale** | Prevelo's CEO cannot justify specialized factories at his volume; Giant, at four million bikes a year, can open a dedicated Vietnam plant. Same industry, opposite answers, driven by scale relative to MES — the FedEx test applied twice with different results |
| **Location economies and CAGE** | Three different responses to the same tariff shock: reshore to the US (Guardian), shift to Taiwan/Thailand/Cambodia (Prevelo), shift to Vietnam (Giant). All three optimize on administrative distance; only one prioritizes geographic proximity to the customer |
| **Learning curve as a real cost of integration** | 100 bikes a day rising to 2,700 quantifies the capability gap a firm absorbs when it internalizes an activity it has never performed. Grant's "developing distinctive capabilities" argument for *outsourcing* is exactly this cost, paid up front and deliberately |
| **Taxes and regulations as a cost of transacting** | The tariff exemption for assembled bikes actively penalized the domestic manufacturer relative to importers — a policy accidentally taxing the more integrated structure. Table 13.1's row on taxes and regulations, working in the unexpected direction |

**Trap to watch:** treating tariffs as the reason Guardian reshored inverts the timeline — the factory opened in 2022 for quality reasons, and the article presents tariffs as something Riley *hopes* will accelerate a shift already underway. Also note the freight offset: labor costs rose but shipping loose components is cheaper than shipping assembled bikes, so the cost comparison is not a simple wage differential.

## Cross-Article Synthesis

### A. The dominant pattern: contracts escalating into ownership

Four cases in this set show a vertical relationship *moving* along the continuum over time rather than being chosen once. This is the single most quiz-able pattern in the set.

| Case | Starting form | Intermediate | Current / attempted | Trigger for escalation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Apple → Globalstar | Supply agreement | 95% capex funding + 85% capacity commitment | ~$1.5B for ~20% equity | Specific investment on both sides; no market price |
| CoreWeave → Core Scientific | Hosting partnership | 12-year, $3.5B lease for 200MW | Acquisition talks | Long-horizon uncertainty; rising asset value |
| Amazon → chip supply | Buying Intel/Nvidia | Custom servers and switches | Annapurna acquisition; in-house Trainium | Supplier scarcity, rationing, pricing power |
| American Giant ← Walmart | Spot purchasing | Noncancelable purchase orders | Part-owned dedicated LA facility | Supplier needed protection to invest |

**Generalizable statement:** the trigger is almost always the appearance of transaction-specific investment. Once one party must build something that only has value in this relationship, spot contracting becomes untenable and the relationship climbs the ladder — first toward longer and more binding contracts, then toward equity, then toward ownership. Whether it stops at equity or goes to ownership depends on how strategically dissimilar the two businesses are (Apple stops at 20% because satellite operation is a different business; CoreWeave goes to acquisition because data-center power is its own input).

### B. Co-location without common ownership — Grant's test, three ways

Grant argues technical economies justify physical proximity but not shared ownership; the ownership question is answered separately by transaction costs.

| Case | Co-location happened | Ownership followed | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Steel / steel strip (textbook) | Yes | **Yes** | Assets purpose-built for one counterparty; bilateral monopoly |
| HealthTrackRx / UPS | Yes — labs relocated to the hub | **No** — a 10-year lease | Many potential tenants and many logistics customers; assets are general-purpose labs |
| Guardian / Brazilian rim supplier | Requested | **Undecided** — Guardian is also considering making rims itself | Live make-or-buy decision; specificity of rims to Guardian is the open variable |

This trio is the cleanest available way to demonstrate that co-location and integration are separate decisions — and the second and third rows are the ones a flawed analysis would collapse into the first.

### C. Buyer power used two ways — the Walmart pair

Reading the Part 1 Dean Foods case against the Part 2 American Giant case gives a matched pair with one variable changed.

| | Walmart / Dean Foods | Walmart / American Giant |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer power | Very high | Very high |
| Supplier's needed investment | Plant efficiency, bottle weight reduction | Automation, machinery, a dedicated facility |
| Contract form | Ongoing supply, no volume guarantee | Noncancelable purchase orders |
| Information flow | Price pressure only | Demand forecasts by price point shared |
| Walmart's intent | To eventually integrate | To not integrate |
| Outcome | Supplier bankrupted; Walmart built the plant | Supplier invested; domestic capacity created |

**The analytical point:** buyer power is a structural fact; whether it expropriates or underwrites is a *strategic choice* determined by whether the buyer wants the capability inside its own boundary. This is the most defensible cross-set generalization available.

### D. Backward integration to reduce supplier power — how far up the chain?

| Case | Stage integrated | Stage left outsourced | Reason for stopping |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amazon | Chip design (Annapurna, Trainium) | Fabrication (TSMC) | Fab MES far exceeds even Amazon's internal volume |
| Apple | iPhone modem (in development) | Watch modem (MediaTek); fabrication | Volume and strategic weight don't justify it per product line |
| CoreWeave | Data center power infrastructure | GPUs (Nvidia) | Cannot integrate into a supplier with that market position |
| Starbucks | Trading and procurement (Lausanne subsidiary) | Growing, and physical commodity supply | Thick market, many suppliers, low switching costs |
| Guardian | Assembly, frames | Hubs, cranks, most components | No domestic supplier base; labor-intensive |

**Pattern:** every one of these firms integrated the stage where *its own volume clears minimum efficient scale* and stopped at the stage where it doesn't. MES, not strategic importance, is the recurring binding constraint.

### F. Where the frameworks strain

| Observation | Framework limitation exposed |
|---|---|
| Starbucks faces extreme uncertainty yet does not integrate | Table 13.1 treats "uncertainty" as one variable. **Price** uncertainty in a liquid market is better managed by financial instruments than ownership; **technological and demand** uncertainty is what actually pushes toward integration |
| Amazon integrates design but not fabrication | Make-or-buy must be applied per stage, not per activity-label. "Chips" is not one decision |
| UPS separates its health-care unit's brand and uniform | The administrative cost of strategic dissimilarity is real but *manageable* — Grant treats it mainly as a reason not to integrate rather than as something firms actively mitigate |
| Guardian accepted losses and a steep learning curve deliberately | Transaction cost analysis is static; it has no account of paying a known capability cost now to change your options later |

## New / Reinforced Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Source case |
|---|---|---|
| **Fabless** | Designing chips in-house while outsourcing manufacture to a foundry. Splits a single apparent activity into an integrated stage and an outsourced one | Amazon |
| **Minimum efficient scale, applied per stage** | The recurring reason firms integrate one step and stop at the next; the same firm can clear MES for design and fail it for fabrication | Amazon, Guardian, Prevelo |
| **Complexity gap** | The software and tooling layer that determines whether a technically capable component is actually usable by customers; where architectural capability lives | Amazon |
| **Capacity commitment** | Contractually allocating a stated share of a supplier's total present and future capacity to one buyer — a specific investment expressed as a share of output rather than an asset | Apple / Globalstar |
| **Underutilized-to-productive asset conversion** | Value created by raising utilization of existing capacity rather than by new capability; the supplier-side rationale for accepting deep dependence | Apple / Globalstar |
| **Dual sourcing / supplier substitution** | Qualifying a second supplier to reduce dependence *without* integrating. Frequently confused with vertical integration | Apple / MediaTek |
| **Customer concentration** | The share of revenue from a single buyer; the downstream mirror of supplier dependence | CoreWeave (62% Microsoft) |
| **Asset redeployment** | Repurposing infrastructure built for one use toward a higher-value use; works when the shared asset is general-purpose (power, cooling, land) and fails when it is specialized (mining ASICs) | Core Scientific vs. Canaan |
| **Replacement value** | Valuing a company by the cost of rebuilding its physical assets; an alternative to cash-flow valuation, not an addition to it | Core Scientific |
| **Cold chain** | Temperature-controlled logistics for perishable or biologic products; the specialized capability layered onto a general parcel network | UPS |
| **Noncancelable purchase order** | A binding volume commitment from a buyer that gives a supplier the security to make transaction-specific investments — the practical form of Grant's long-term contract | American Giant |
| **Price-to-be-fixed contract** | An agreement setting quantity, delivery month and premium to a futures benchmark, leaving final price to be agreed later; long-term on volume, spot-like on price | Starbucks |
| **Priced coverage** | How many months of future requirements a buyer has locked in at known prices; Starbucks' typical range is 9–18 months | Starbucks |
| **Reshoring** | Relocating production back to the home country. Distinct from insourcing — it changes *where*, not *who* | Guardian, American Giant |
| **Learning curve cost of integration** | The temporary productivity penalty absorbed when a firm internalizes an activity it has not performed before | Guardian (100 → 2,700 bikes/day) |
