## Core Idea

- A firm's competitive environment (Five Forces) is not the only driver of profit potential — firms in the same tough industry can perform very differently (Dell, Wal-Mart, Southwest, Nucor all outperformed hostile industries).
- RBV explains this by looking *inside* the firm: performance depends on the resources and capabilities a firm controls and how well it exploits them.
- Two assumptions make RBV work: firms hold different bundles of resources (**heterogeneity**), and those differences can persist because resources don't move freely between firms (**immobility**).
- **VRIO** (Value, Rarity, Imitability, Organization) is the operational tool for turning "resources" into a testable prediction about competitive advantage.
- RBV is a *complement* to Five Forces, not a replacement — Five Forces asks what the environment rewards; RBV asks whether the firm has what it takes to capture that reward.

## Key Terms & Definitions

### Foundational Concepts
| Term                                         | Definition                                                                                                                                                                          |
| -------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Resource-based view (RBV)**                | Framework that explains firm performance by the idiosyncratic, costly-to-copy resources a firm controls, rather than only by industry structure.                                    |
| **Firm resources**                           | All assets, capabilities, competencies, processes, attributes, information, and knowledge a firm controls that let it conceive and implement strategy.                              |
| **Resource heterogeneity**                   | Assumption that different firms possess different bundles of resources, even within the same industry.                                                                              |
| **Resource immobility**                      | Assumption that some resources are costly to copy or inelastic in supply, so heterogeneity can persist over time.                                                                   |
| **Resource** (slide-deck usage)              | A relatively stable, observable, tradable asset owned by the firm (e.g., a brand, a location).                                                                                      |
| **Capability**                               | Skills a firm uses to transform inputs into outputs and combine tangible/intangible resources toward a goal (e.g., practices that drive superior quality or service).               |
| **Core competence / distinctive competence** | Resources/capabilities that are the actual source of a firm's competitive advantage — they distinguish the firm competitively and emerge over time through organizational learning. |
| **Tangible resources**                       | Physical, visible assets: labor, capital, land, buildings, plant, equipment, supplies.                                                                                              |
| **Intangible resources**                     | Non-physical, invisible assets: culture, knowledge, brand equity, reputation, patents, designs, copyrights, trademarks, trade secrets.                                              |

### Historical Roots of RBV (Ch. 5.1)
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **General managers** | Managers with profit-and-loss responsibility over more than one function; early theory (Harvard, 1911) treated their quality as the main firm strength/weakness. |
| **Institutional leaders** | Selznick's term for leaders who go beyond managing to defining an organization's purpose/vision and safeguarding its distinctive identity — the root of "distinctive competence." |
| **Ricardian economics** | Analysis (David Ricardo) of rents earned from factors of production, originally land, that are fixed/inelastic in supply. |
| **Economic rent** | A payment to a resource owner above the minimum needed to keep that resource in use — arises when a resource is inelastic in supply. |
| **Penrose's theory of firm growth** | Edith Penrose's view that a firm is an administrative framework plus a bundle of productive resources; firm growth is limited by that resource bundle, not by simple supply-and-demand production functions. |

### Resource Categories (Ch. 5.2)
| Category                   | What it covers                                                                                            | Examples                                                                              |
| -------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Financial capital**      | Money resources used to conceive/implement strategy                                                       | Entrepreneur capital, equity, debt, retained earnings                                 |
| **Physical capital**       | Physical technology, plant, location, raw-material access                                                 | Computer hardware, robots, Wal-Mart's rural sites, L.L. Bean's Maine HQ               |
| **Human capital**          | Training, experience, judgment, intelligence, relationships of *individuals*                              | Bill Gates, Steve Jobs — but also non-executive employees                             |
| **Organizational capital** | Attributes of *collections* of people: reporting structure, planning/control systems, culture, reputation | Merck's and Harley-Davidson's reputations used to access labor/distribution/customers |

- **Value-chain analysis** — Method of identifying resources/capabilities by breaking a product's production into vertical activities (e.g., exploring → drilling → refining → distributing for oil) and asking what financial/physical/human/organizational resources each stage requires.
  - McKinsey generic value chain: technology development → product design → manufacturing → marketing → distribution → service.
  - Porter generic value chain: primary activities (inbound logistics, production, outbound logistics, sales/marketing, service) supported by infrastructure, technology development, and HR management.

### VRIO Framework
| Question                                                                   | Definition                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Value**                                                                  | Do the firm's resources/capabilities let it exploit an opportunity or neutralize a threat (i.e., raise willingness-to-pay or cut costs)? Links internal analysis back to Five Forces/external analysis.                                                                            |
| **Rarity**                                                                 | Is the resource controlled by only a small number of competing firms (fewer than needed for perfect competition)?                                                                                                                                                                  |
| **Imitability**                                                            | Do firms without the resource face a *cost disadvantage* in obtaining or developing it? ("Imperfectly imitable" = costly to copy.)                                                                                                                                                 |
| **Organization**                                                           | Are the firm's structure, control systems, and compensation policies set up to actually exploit the valuable/rare/costly-to-imitate resource?                                                                                                                                      |
| **Nonsubstitutable** (slide-deck 4th "R"/criterion, alongside imitability) | No different resource can be used to achieve an equivalent effect (e.g., gas vs. electric vehicles as alternate routes to "transportation"). Barney's original 1991 formulation folds this into imitability; some slide decks list it as a distinct 4th criterion alongside V-R-I. |
| **Complementary resources and capabilities**                               | Organizational elements (reporting structure, control systems, compensation) that have little value *in isolation* but let a firm fully realize the advantage from its VRI resources.                                                                                              |

### Forms and Sources of Imitation Cost (Ch. 5.2 imitability section)
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Direct duplication** | Imitator tries to build the *same* resource the advantaged firm has (e.g., develop their own R&D skill). |
| **Substitution** | Imitator uses a *different* resource to achieve the same effect (e.g., an information system substituting for a strong top-management team's communication skills). |
| **Time-compression diseconomies** | Extra cost of trying to build a resource quickly instead of over the original, longer timeframe (Dierickx & Cool) — e.g., Caterpillar's WWII-built global service network can't be recreated at equal cost today. |
| **Path dependence** | A resource's value/cost today depends on decisions made earlier, under uncertainty, when the resource was cheaper to acquire — later imitators must pay the now-known full value. |
| **Causal ambiguity** | Imitating firms (or even the advantaged firm's own managers) can't clearly identify which resource(s) actually cause the competitive advantage, making imitation hard to target. |
| **Invisible assets** (Itami) | Resources so embedded in daily routine that a firm's own managers don't recognize them as sources of advantage (e.g., teamwork, culture, supplier relationships). |
| **Interconnectedness of asset stocks / asset mass efficiencies** (Dierickx & Cool) | When advantage comes from thousands of small, bundled resources/decisions rather than one big one, imitators can't identify a single thing to copy (The Mailbox, Inc. example). |
| **Social complexity** | Resource is a socially complex phenomenon (culture, interpersonal relations, reputation) that firms can't systematically engineer or purchase, even when the value-link is well understood. |
| **Patents** | Can raise imitation cost (pharma, instant photography) but can also *lower* it, since filing discloses information to competitors and rarely blocks functionally-equivalent substitutes. |

### Isolating Mechanisms (slide-deck synthesis term for the above)
- **Isolating mechanisms** — umbrella term for barriers to imitation that sustain competitive advantage: favorable expectations of future resource value, path dependence, causal ambiguity, social complexity, and IP protection (patents, trademarks, trade secrets).

### Outcomes and Firm-Level Dynamics
| Term                                               | Definition                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Competitive disadvantage**                       | Resource is not valuable → below-average performance if exploited.                                                                                                                                                        |
| **Competitive parity**                             | Resource is valuable but not rare → average/normal performance.                                                                                                                                                           |
| **Temporary competitive advantage**                | Resource is valuable and rare but not costly to imitate → above-average performance until imitators catch up.                                                                                                             |
| **Sustained competitive advantage**                | Resource is valuable, rare, costly to imitate, and the firm is organized to exploit it → advantage not competed away through imitation.                                                                                   |
| **Sustained competitive advantage** ≠ "forever"    | It just means the advantage isn't eroded by *imitation*; it can still be wiped out by external shocks (see Schumpeterian revolutions below).                                                                              |
| **Schumpeterian revolutions**                      | Sudden, unpredictable shifts in a firm's threats/opportunities (demand shifts, new technology, political upheaval) that can destroy the value of previously VRIO resources — e.g., PCs devaluing typewriter-making skill. |
| **Imitability paradox**                            | The less costly a resource is for a firm to acquire/develop, the less likely it is to be a source of *sustained* advantage — because if it's cheap for you, it's cheap for competitors too.                               |
| **Core rigidity** (slide-deck term)                | A former core competency that becomes a liability after an environmental shift, because it no longer fits the new external environment.                                                                                   |
| **Dynamic capabilities**                           | A firm's ability to adapt, create, deploy, reconfigure, and upgrade its resources over time to maintain strategic fit as the environment changes (e.g., P&G post-recession portfolio cuts).                               |
| **Resource stocks and flows** ("bathtub metaphor") | Resource stocks = current level of an intangible resource (like water in a tub); resource flows = ongoing investment (inflow) needed to offset natural depreciation/forgetting (outflow) and maintain or grow the stock.  |

## The VRIO Logic, Step by Step

1. **Not valuable** → resource is a **weakness**; exploiting it raises costs or lowers willingness-to-pay → competitive disadvantage if used.
2. **Valuable, not rare** → resource is a **strength**, but only generates **competitive parity**; failing to use it, though, causes a disadvantage.
3. **Valuable, rare, not costly to imitate** → **strength and distinctive competence**, giving a **temporary competitive advantage** (first-mover advantage until competitors catch up via direct duplication or substitution at no cost penalty).
4. **Valuable, rare, costly to imitate** → **strength and (sustainable) distinctive competence**, giving a **sustained competitive advantage** — *provided* the firm is organized to exploit it. If the "Organization" question fails, some of the potential advantage is lost (Xerox PARC case: valuable/rare/costly-to-imitate technologies were never commercialized because Xerox's reporting structure, bureaucratic development process, and revenue-only compensation weren't built to exploit them).

| Valuable? | Rare? | Costly to Imitate? | Exploited by Organization? | Competitive Implication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No | — | — | No | Competitive disadvantage |
| Yes | No | — | — | Competitive parity |
| Yes | Yes | No | — | Temporary competitive advantage |
| Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes | Sustained competitive advantage |

## Implications of RBV (Table 5.4, Ch. 5.4)

1. **Competitive advantage is every employee's responsibility**, not just senior managers' — illustrated by the plant manager/line manager/janitor example: all three *could* frame their job as "helping make and sell the best cars in the world," but only the janitor's framing is *rare* (most janitors don't think that way), so only that framing is a plausible source of advantage.
2. **Imitating competitors only ever buys competitive parity.** To beat parity, a firm must do something valuable *and* rare — benchmarking is useful for catching up, not for gaining advantage.
3. **Relative cost of implementation matters more than absolute cost.** The real question isn't "is this strategy hard to implement?" but "is it harder for competitors than for us?" Firms can err by overestimating their uniqueness (assuming rare resources when competitors have equivalents) or underestimating it (assuming their easy implementation means it's easy for everyone).
4. **Socially complex resources** (culture, empowerment, teamwork) can be valuable *and* rare *and* costly to imitate — not just "nice to have."
5. **Organization should follow resources, not the reverse** — if a firm's structure/systems conflict with its VRI resources, change the organization (Caterpillar and Wal-Mart did; Xerox didn't).

## Limitations of the VRIO Framework (Ch. 5.5)

| Limitation                                             | Explanation                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Sustained ≠ permanent**                              | Schumpeterian revolutions (unpredictable shifts in demand, technology, or politics) can destroy the value of even a well-defended resource — the advantage isn't imitated away, it's *displaced*.                                                                                |
| **Imitability paradox / limited managerial influence** | Managers can't simply choose to create sustained advantage — if a resource is cheap to build, competitors can build it too, so only resources built through path dependence, causal ambiguity, or social complexity (often over long, unplanned periods) can sustain advantage.  |
| **Unit-of-analysis problem**                           | Unlike Five Forces (industry-level, good public data), RBV analysis happens at the level of individual firm resources — intraorganizational data is hard to access, and precisely because sustaining resources are often invisible/hard to describe, they're also hard to study. |

## Cross-Framework Connections

- **RBV vs. Five Forces**: Five Forces = external/industry-level (what does the environment reward?); RBV = internal/firm-level (does this firm have what's needed to capture that reward?). The VRIO "Value" question explicitly reconnects the two — a resource is only valuable if it exploits an opportunity or neutralizes a threat identified via Five Forces-style analysis.
- **RBV vs. dynamic capabilities**: VRIO is a static snapshot of whether a *current* resource bundle supports advantage; dynamic capabilities describe how firms *continuously* rebuild that bundle as the environment shifts — the "reinvest, hone, upgrade" loop in the resources → core competencies → activities → competitive advantage chain (slide deck), and the bathtub-metaphor inflow/outflow of intangible resource stocks.
- **RBV vs. Conservation of Attractive Profits / Aggregation Theory** (from prior Innovation Strategies unit): a useful parallel to flag for a synthesis question — both RBV's "imitability paradox" and Christensen's Conservation of Attractive Profits describe how *value migrates toward whatever remains scarce/hard to copy* in a system, just applied at different levels (firm resource vs. industry value chain).

## Real-World Article Examples Applied to RBV

Each entry: what the article reports, then explicit ties to RBV/VRIO concepts above, then open questions for discussion/quiz prep.

### Apple–Globalstar satellite deal (2022)
- **What happened:** Apple funded 95% of Globalstar's capex to build satellite capacity in exchange for 85% of Globalstar's network, launching iPhone 14's emergency-SOS satellite texting. Globalstar's stock jumped on the news; the deal turned an "underutilized asset into a productive asset."
- **RBV connections:** Globalstar's satellite network was a valuable, rare physical-capital resource that sat unexploited — a textbook **Organization** failure (like Xerox PARC) until Apple's capital and installed base supplied the missing organizational/financial complement. Also a **complementary resources** case: neither firm's resource alone was as valuable as the combination (Apple's distribution + Globalstar's spectrum/satellites).
- **Follow-up questions:** Was Globalstar's network valuable before the deal, or did Apple's involvement create the value? Is the resulting advantage temporary or sustained, given Globalstar's dependence on a single customer?

### Apple–Starlink–T-Mobile beta (2025)
- **What happened:** Three years after the Globalstar tie-up, Apple quietly enabled Starlink satellite support for T-Mobile customers — a direct competitor service that works automatically (vs. Globalstar's point-at-the-sky requirement) — and Globalstar shares fell ~11% on the news.
- **RBV connections:** Illustrates **substitution** as an imitation path — SpaceX/T-Mobile didn't copy Globalstar's exact network, they built a functionally equivalent (arguably superior) substitute. Globalstar's exclusivity with Apple, once a source of temporary advantage, is being competed away, showing that even a valuable/rare resource (an Apple partnership) doesn't guarantee sustained advantage if a substitute resource (a rival satellite constellation) emerges.
- **Follow-up questions:** Does Starlink's automatic-pairing design count as *directly imitating* Globalstar's value proposition or *substituting* a different resource (satellite density/architecture) for the same customer need?

### Chick-fil-A lemon automation (Bay Center Foods)
- **What happened:** Chick-fil-A built a ~190,000-sq-ft automated plant that squeezes up to 1.6 million pounds of lemons/day with minimal labor, freeing in-store staff, creating a new byproduct revenue stream (citrus oils sold to fragrance/cosmetics firms), and using nearly 100% of each lemon vs. ~40% under manual squeezing.
- **RBV connections:** A clean **VRIO** case. Valuable — cuts 10,000 daily labor-hours, reduces injuries, adds new revenue. Rare — an engineer unaffiliated with the project estimates only ~5% of US manufacturing plants have comparable automation. Costly to imitate — hard to retrofit existing facilities; Chick-fil-A won't disclose the plant's cost. Organization — deliberately built to integrate with the existing supply chain and franchise-store model. Also a **value-chain** example: Chick-fil-A vertically integrated backward into juice production, a stage most quick-service chains outsource.
- **Follow-up questions:** Is the byproduct (citrus-oil sales) itself a new valuable/rare resource, or just a cost offset? Would this automation model still be advantageous for a chain without Chick-fil-A's scale (~3,200 locations)?

### Cosm immersive sports dome
- **What happened:** Cosm built vertically integrated dome theaters (LED screens, streaming tech) via a string of acquisitions (Evans and Sutherland, Spitz, LiveLike VR) starting in 2019, aiming to sit between a sports bar and a stadium experience; it has raised $250M and is expanding to Atlanta and Detroit.
- **RBV connections:** **Path dependence** — CEO Jeb Terry recounts partners calling the "build the infrastructure to scale" bet unusual, and the strategy only became viable after an early prototype validated the "full stack" approach — a resource built through a specific, hard-to-repeat sequence of acquisitions and technical proof points. The "shared reality" positioning is a **core competence** distinguishing Cosm from both traditional sports bars and VR headsets.
- **Follow-up questions:** Is Cosm's vertically integrated tech stack costly to imitate mainly because of capital requirements, or because of causal ambiguity about which pieces (screens, sports-league partnerships, real estate site-selection) actually drive the experience's appeal?

### Crumbl's teen cookie empire
- **What happened:** Crumbl built $1B+ in franchisee sales via weekly rotating flavors (announced Sunday nights), a heavy TikTok/social presence (9.6M followers, vs. Starbucks' 2.2M), and a recognizable pink-box aesthetic; each new flavor goes through 5–6 iterations tested in ~50 stores over 3–12 months.
- **RBV connections:** The weekly-drop model plus social-media engine functions as a **core competence** — valuable (drives FOMO-based demand and reduces need for paid advertising), rare (no direct competitor runs an identical national weekly-rotation cadence), and costly to imitate in the short run (a rival would need to rebuild both the flavor-testing pipeline and years of organic social following). The brand's aesthetic (Pantone-developed color, collectible boxes) adds a **social complexity** layer.
- **Follow-up questions:** Is this a source of *sustained* advantage, or does it face a Schumpeterian-style risk if teen social-media trends shift away from Crumbl (the article itself asks how sustainable a business built on the "whims" of teenage girls can be)? Does McGowan's move into pies/brownies/merchandise represent a search for less trend-dependent resources?

### GM–Lithium Americas (Thacker Pass)
- **What happened:** GM boosted its stake in Lithium Americas' Thacker Pass lithium mine in Nevada to $625M (plus DOE financing), securing 62%/38% offtake rights and a direct ownership stake in a scarce EV-battery input.
- **RBV connections:** A direct real-world instance of **Ricardian economics** from the Ch. 5 historical-roots section — Thacker Pass is a literal fixed-supply natural resource (a specific mineral deposit), and locking in ownership/offtake rights lets GM capture an **economic rent**-like advantage in future EV supply chains, the same logic as Ricardo's fertile-land example.
- **Follow-up questions:** Does owning a stake in the mine make the lithium supply a *firm* resource for GM (heterogeneity), or is it better modeled as a supplier relationship that reduces Five Forces supplier power? Both framings are defensible — good cross-framework question.

### Intel — Pat Gelsinger's failed turnaround
- **What happened:** Gelsinger tried to revive Intel's "IDM" (integrated device manufacturer — designs *and* manufactures) model into a TSMC-style contract foundry business ("IDM 2.0"), but Intel lost $150B in value, posted a $16.6B quarterly loss, and Gelsinger was pushed out in 2024.
- **RBV connections:** A rich **core rigidity** and **Schumpeterian revolution** case. Intel's historic core competence (owning both design and fabrication) became a liability once the industry split into specialized design firms (Nvidia) and specialized foundries (TSMC) — the environment shifted in a way the old resource bundle no longer fit. Intel's attempt to rebuild foundry-customer relationships also illustrates the **imitability paradox** in reverse: TSMC's customer-service culture and manufacturing lead, built over decades, were *not* something Intel could cheaply replicate on a fast timeline, despite heavy investment (five process nodes targeted in four years). Failed M&A attempts (GlobalFoundries, Tower Semiconductor) show resource acquisition as an alternate — and here, blocked — path to closing a capability gap.
- **Follow-up questions:** Was Intel's IDM resource ever VRIO in the first place, or did it only look that way before the industry's Schumpeterian shift? Compare directly against the TSMC article below.

### Meta–Constellation nuclear power deal
- **What happened:** Meta signed a 20-year, 1,121-megawatt contract with Constellation for power from the Clinton nuclear plant, part of a broader tech-industry rush (Microsoft/Three Mile Island, Amazon/Google small reactors) to lock in stable power for AI data centers.
- **RBV connections:** Illustrates firms racing to secure an increasingly scarce, roughly fixed-supply input (reliable carbon-free power) — another Ricardian-style dynamic (inelastic supply, first movers capture favorable long-term contracts before prices/availability tighten further). Also ties to complementary-resource logic: Meta's capital lets Constellation justify building new capacity neither party could unlock alone.
- **Follow-up questions:** Is a 20-year power contract better modeled as a firm resource (RBV) or as a Five Forces supplier-power dynamic? Does locking in nuclear power constitute a source of competitive advantage over AI rivals without similar deals, or just table-stakes cost management?

### Nuuly clothing rental
- **What happened:** Nuuly, Urban Outfitters' rental brand, turned an operating profit ($5.3M for the quarter) — a rarity in apparel rental — helped by an automated Pennsylvania warehouse, a second $60M Missouri facility, and benefits from parent-company buying power (Nuuly buys ~half its inventory at cost alongside Urban Outfitters/Anthropologie/Free People).
- **RBV connections:** A strong **resource heterogeneity** example — analysts explicitly note that stand-alone rental companies (like Rent the Runway, still posting losses) don't have the resources and know-how Nuuly gets from its parent. This is the textbook's Penrose/heterogeneity logic in action: two firms in the same niche (apparel rental) have fundamentally different resource bundles because one is embedded in a larger organizational-capital structure and one is not.
- **Follow-up questions:** Is Nuuly's advantage over Rent the Runway based on a resource (access to Urban Outfitters' brands/buying power) or a capability (logistics automation expertise)? Could Rent the Runway close the gap through partnership or acquisition, or is Nuuly's access to Urban's brand family inherently non-transferable?

### Nvidia — Jensen Huang's "T5T" emails
- **What happened:** For decades, ~30,000 Nvidia employees have sent Huang informal "Top-5 Things" emails; Huang personally reads all of them to get unfiltered "signals from the edge," bypassing traditional status reports and management layers.
- **RBV connections:** A vivid **invisible asset / socially complex resource** example. The T5T system is organizational capital that's hard to specify or transplant — biographer Tae Kim suggests other companies could learn from it but not copy it in the exact same way, which is close to a real-world statement of **causal ambiguity** (it's unclear exactly which parts of the practice, or Huang's personal habits, generate the advantage) and **social complexity** (it depends on decades of trust and company-wide participation, not a policy any firm could adopt overnight).
- **Follow-up questions:** Is T5T itself the resource, or is it a mechanism that surfaces a deeper resource (distributed employee knowledge)? Would T5T work as well under a different, less workaholic CEO — i.e., is the practice separable from Huang as an individual (human capital) at all?

### Perplexity's high-cost growth
- **What happened:** AI search startup Perplexity generated $34M in revenue but burned $65M in cash last year, funded by repeated VC rounds ($500M round in talks at a $14B valuation), while CEO Aravind Srinivas concedes that AI search bots will increasingly resemble one another.
- **RBV connections:** A useful **counter-example** — capital and discounting can buy user growth, but Srinivas's own admission signals the underlying product may lack rarity/inimitability once funding dries up (the **imitability paradox**: cheap-to-replicate AI wrappers around OpenAI/Anthropic models are, almost by definition, not a source of *sustained* advantage). Perplexity's attempts to differentiate (Comet browser, shopping integration, Sidekick acquisition) can be read as a search for a genuinely VRIO resource beyond "being an AI chatbot."
- **Follow-up questions:** Which of Perplexity's assets (if any) look valuable, rare, *and* costly to imitate — the underlying AI model (licensed from others, so arguably not firm-specific), the user base, or the emerging shopping/ad infrastructure?

### Roche's GLP-1 setback
- **What happened:** Roche's obesity-drug trial data showed high rates of vomiting at high doses, rattling investor confidence in its bid to challenge market leaders Novo Nordisk and Eli Lilly in the GLP-1 weight-loss/diabetes drug category.
- **RBV connections:** Illustrates that catching up to a first-mover's resource position (Novo/Lilly's years of GLP-1 formulation know-how) is costly to imitate — not just because of patents, but because of trial-and-error, tacit scientific knowledge (**causal ambiguity** about *why* side effects differ by dose, drug, and even by sex, per the Eli Lilly Mounjaro study cited). Eli Lilly's acquisition of Versanis (for muscle-wastage-reducing IP) is a **complementary-resource** acquisition to shore up a specific weakness in its existing drug portfolio.
- **Follow-up questions:** Is Novo/Lilly's GLP-1 lead best explained by patents (Ch. 5's discussion of patents as an imperfect isolating mechanism) or by tacit R&D know-how (causal ambiguity/social complexity)? The Pfizer example (abandoned twice-daily pill after side effects) in this same article is a useful second data point.

### Starbucks in China
- **What happened:** Starbucks same-store sales in China fell 14% as domestic rivals (Luckin Coffee, ChaGee, HeyTea) undercut it on price while offering far more frequent new-flavor launches (Luckin: ~60 new products/year); Luckin now has ~3x as many China stores and outsells Starbucks there.
- **RBV connections:** A strong "changes in resource value" case, paralleling Ch. 5's GE-transistor/American Airlines examples: Starbucks' premium foreign-brand cachet was once a valuable, rare resource in China but has become *less valuable* (even a liability) as consumer preferences shifted toward local flavors and "guochao" (Chinese-pride) sentiment. Its resource bundle (US-centric menu, higher prices, larger-format stores for lingering) still has some value (positioned as a meeting/study space, unlike smaller app-order-focused rivals) but is no longer rare relative to fast-innovating local chains.
- **Follow-up questions:** Is this resource devaluation closer to a Schumpeterian revolution (abrupt shift in consumer sentiment/politics) or a gradual competitive-imitation dynamic (rivals catching up on quality while beating Starbucks on rarity of flavor variety)? Compare to the USS/Sears examples in Ch. 5, where firms failed to recognize a changing environment.

### Swatch — activist investor vs. Hayek family control
- **What happened:** Swatch Group's shares have fallen sharply over a decade under CEO Nick Hayek; an activist investor is pushing for a board seat, criticizing family-centric governance, while Swatch still owns 150+ production sites that make components for itself and third-party watchmakers.
- **RBV connections:** A clear **Organization**-question failure, structurally similar to Xerox PARC: Swatch's vertically integrated manufacturing base (including Micro Crystal's quartz-crystal leadership) is plausibly valuable, rare, and costly to imitate, but critics argue the firm isn't organized to exploit it — family control, resistance to analyst/investor feedback, and refusal to give individual brand leaders more autonomy are cited as blocking a portfolio refresh. Family-centric governance, once a stabilizing strength (Hayek's father is credited with saving Swiss watchmaking via the original Swatch/quartz strategy), risks becoming a **core rigidity** given the shifting market (luxury polarizing toward the very top, e.g., Patek Philippe, while Swatch's own high-end brands like Breguet decline).
- **Follow-up questions:** Is Swatch's component-manufacturing capability alone enough to sustain competitive advantage if the "O" in VRIO keeps failing? How does this compare to Xerox PARC as a case of valuable/rare/costly-to-imitate resources going unexploited?

### Temu's ad-spending ascent
- **What happened:** Temu (backed by China's PDD Holdings) spent $1.7B on US ads in 2023 (headed to ~$3B in 2024), briefly becoming the 5th-largest US digital advertiser and driving up ad costs for rivals like Etsy and Dollar General, following the subsidize-then-monetize playbook PDD used against Alibaba in China.
- **RBV connections:** Good **imitability-paradox** counter-example: heavy ad spend funded by capital is not, by itself, a rare or costly-to-imitate resource (any well-capitalized entrant could match the spend) — Temu's *real* resource is arguably the replicable *process knowledge* PDD built executing this exact playbook once already in China against Alibaba/JD.com, which is more causally ambiguous and organizationally embedded than the capital itself.
- **Follow-up questions:** Is "willingness to lose money for years to buy share" a resource, a capability, or just a financing advantage (patient Chinese parent-company capital)? Could a well-funded US rival replicate Temu's approach, or is the PDD playbook itself (informed by prior execution) the harder-to-copy piece?

### TSMC's 2024 growth forecast
- **What happened:** TSMC forecast up to 25% revenue growth in 2024 (more than double the overall chip market's expected growth), driven by AI demand; management publicly rejected comparisons between Intel's upcoming 18A node and TSMC's more advanced N2, framing the "foundry" model (optimizing for many customers) as structurally different from Intel's "IDM" model (optimizing only for itself).
- **RBV connections:** Pairs directly with the Intel article — TSMC's foundry specialization is the resource/capability Intel's Gelsinger explicitly tried (and struggled) to replicate. TSMC's technology leadership plus its customer-centric foundry business model function as a valuable, rare, and (per TSMC's own framing) hard-to-directly-compare capability — a real-time example of Ch. 5's Sony discussion, where *repeated, compounding* technology leadership (not any single node) is the actual source of sustained advantage.
- **Follow-up questions:** Cross-reference with the Intel article: does TSMC's advantage over Intel look more like a rarity gap (better process tech) or an imitability gap (a customer-service culture/business model Intel structurally can't replicate while still serving itself first)?

### Volkswagen–Rivian joint venture (initial $5B deal and $800M follow-on)
- **What happened:** VW invested up to $5B (later raised by $800M) into a 50/50 joint venture with Rivian to co-develop EV software/architecture, after VW's own in-house software efforts (and its written-off Argo AI investment) failed to deliver competitive technology; the JV later unveiled a working prototype built in 12 weeks.
- **RBV connections:** A clean **complementary resources / buy-vs-build** case. VW has scale, manufacturing capital, and global distribution but lacks a valuable, rare, hard-to-build-in-house software capability; Rivian has the software/architecture capability but lacks VW's capital and manufacturing scale. Neither firm's resource alone is sufficient; the JV combines them. VW's earlier failed attempts (in-house software stack, Argo AI) show that this specific capability was **costly to imitate/build internally** even for a well-funded incumbent — supporting the Ch. 5 point that valuable/rare resources built through years of specialized work (Rivian's software architecture) aren't simply purchasable off the shelf, only accessible via partnership/acquisition of the resource-holder itself.
- **Follow-up questions:** Is Rivian's software capability sustained (valuable, rare, costly to imitate) on its own, or does it only become fully exploitable once combined with VW's complementary manufacturing/scale resources (an "Organization" story at the JV level, not just within one firm)? How does this compare to GM/Lithium Americas as a different resource-access strategy (equity stake + offtake vs. jointly-owned operating venture)?
