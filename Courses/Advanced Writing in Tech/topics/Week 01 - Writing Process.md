## Notes

### Reading the two together

Both readings attack the same target — the idea that a finished text tells you anything reliable
about how it was made — but from opposite ends.

**Lamott** works from the inside of one writer's process and argues that the fantasy of the
effortless first draft is the thing blocking people from writing at all. Her fix is
psychological: give yourself permission, separate generating from evaluating, get the down draft
out so the up draft and the dental draft have something to work on.

**Shipka** works from the outside and argues that the field's *picture* of writing — Brodkey's
"writer-writing-alone," the postcard of the Grand Canyon — is what's blocking research and
teaching. Her fix is methodological: widen the unit of analysis past the text and past the
individual writer to the whole distributed, material, multimodal activity.

> [!tip] The productive tension for Thursday
> Lamott is the postcard Shipka is complaining about — a lone writer at a desk, panicking, in
> a psychological drama with herself. **But read her against the grain and she's Shipka's best
> evidence.** Her own account is stuffed with the distributed, material stuff Shipka wants
> visible: the restaurant, the "opinionated, articulate friends" whose lines she writes down,
> the notes, the colored pen, the one-inch picture frame she stares into, the mirror, the phone
> calls, the kitchen, the monthly deadline, the mailing it in. Lamott narrates all of that as
> *interruption* — the stuff around the writing. Shipka would say it **is** the writing.
>
> The one-inch picture frame is the sharpest instance: a physical object on her desk that does
> the cognitive work of scoping the task. That's a mediated action, not a mood.

### Where they actually agree

- Product is a bad proxy for process. Lamott: the fantasy of the uninitiated. Shipka/Brandt:
  arriving at the party after everyone's gone home.
- Both are anti-mystification. Neither thinks good writing comes from talent or dictation from
  God (Lamott's Muriel Spark joke; Shipka's insistence on labor and "available means of
  production").
- Both want the process *described*, not idealized.

### Where they'd disagree

- Lamott offers a **universal sequence** — down, up, dental. Shipka reports the field's verdict
  on exactly that move: if all communication is "radically contingent, radically situational,"
  teaching *the* writing process is "misguided, unproductive [and] misleading" (Olson 1999), and
  a taught process tends to "inculcate[] a particular method of composing" set by the product
  the teacher expects (Harris 1997).
- Lamott's freewriting-adjacent advice is precisely the "introspective heuristic" Bawarshi calls
  "a privatized economy of invention" — it implies writing begins *in the writer* rather than in
  their relationship with the world.
- Verdict to argue in class: Lamott's advice is genuinely useful *as* a local intervention
  against paralysis, and it's a bad general theory of composing. Those aren't in conflict — it's
  the difference between a heuristic and a model.

### For "what is good writing?" (Wed)

Shipka gives the answer the course seems to want: the question "what makes a piece of writing
good (or purposeful, successful, appropriate)" stops being mysterious once you're looking at
social goals and situations rather than at an abstract quality of texts (28). Pair that with the
course's own rejection of "abstract or universal notions of good writing" — the field-example
document to bring in should be defended by *what it accomplishes for whom, under what
constraints*, not by how it reads.

## Key Takeaways

- Down draft → up draft → dental draft. The first draft's only job is to exist.
- Separating generation from evaluation is the whole mechanism; doing both at once is what
  produces Lamott's panic loop.
- "Multimodal" ≠ "digital." Handwriting, chalk, room layout, gesture, and smell are modes;
  new technology just makes the older materiality visible again.
- Writing is distributed labor across people, tools, places, and time — the text is the residue,
  not the event.
- The writer's memo required in this course is a metacommunicative move with a real history
  behind it: the communications approach was dismissed on the grounds that "writing comes first,
  consciousness-raising second."

## Open Questions

- Does Lamott's sequence hold in technical genres where structure is fixed up front (report,
  runbook, RFC, design doc)? Or does the down draft just become the outline?
- What's the honest boundary between a heuristic that unblocks a writer and a taught process
  that flattens everyone into one method?
- Software leaves an unusually complete process trace (commits, PRs, review threads). Is that
  the research site Shipka wants, or just a new "official side of writing" to get stuck on?
