# Reading — Schryer, "The Lab vs. the Clinic: Sites of Competing Genres"

Reading for [[Week 03 - Discourse Communities]] · [[Advanced Writing in Tech]]

> [!cite] Citation
> Schryer, Catherine F. "The Lab vs. the Clinic: Sites of Competing Genres." *Genre and the New
> Rhetoric*, edited by Aviva Freedman and Peter Medway, Taylor & Francis, 1994, pp. 105–124.

> [!abstract] One-line version
> A two-year ethnography of a veterinary college finds researchers and clinicians split into two
> camps by the two genres they write — the experimental article (IMRDS) and the medical record
> (POVMR) — and argues the genres aren't symptoms of the split but the machinery maintaining it,
> because one of them is ranked as producing real knowledge and the other isn't.

> [!tip] Read this as the rebuttal to Swales
> [[Reading - Swales, The Concept of a Discourse Community]] ends by admitting his model is
> "utopian" and free of the conflicts of an actual university. Schryer is inside that conflict.
> Same college, same staff, largely the same students — and genre keeps them "separate and apart."

## The Opening Move: Voices

She opens not with a thesis but with five unattributed quotations, arranged as a conversation
that never happened — the "voices" of a pathologist, two biomedical scientists, a clinician, and
a first-year student. The hostility is the data:

> *Dr W (pathologist):* "Personally I dread it when a clinician dumps a manuscript on my desk for
> my comments. I know it's going to be badly written."

> *Dr B (biomedical science):* "The clinicians are not scientists. They are practitioners... Such
> purely applied people can only write reviews. They lack the sort of curiosity needed for
> research."

> *Dr K (clinician):* "The traditional way, IMRDS... can lead to boredom, and it suggests that
> there is a finite amount of information which can be memorized by rote memory. But we're trying
> to emphasize problem solving."

The student, G, sees it clearly from below: "On the one side is the faculty and on the other side
are the clinicians... Both views are important, but the practical is more important."

This is a rhetorical choice worth stealing. She establishes that the conflict is *lived* before
she offers a word of theory, and she never tells you which voice to trust.

## Method

Ironically, the data came from a consulting job: the curriculum committee asked her whether their
students had a literacy problem. January–June 1988, 80 interviews with students, faculty and
practitioners, over 200 hours of participant observation in classrooms, labs and clinics, ten
reader protocols of faculty evaluating student papers, extensive document collection. Analysed
with Glaser and Strauss's grounded theory.

The finding was not the one she was hired to produce: "It was during this process that I became
aware of the tensions existing between researchers and clinicians/practitioners and the
relationship of discursive practices to each group."

## The Theory: Genre as Stabilized-for-Now

Her definition, which is the sentence people cite her for:

> a genre is a "stabilized-for-now or stabilized-enough site of social and ideological action"

It builds on Miller's "recurrent, significant action" and on Bakhtin, and the hyphenated hedge is
doing the work — genres look permanent and are not. From Bakhtin she takes the paradox that
genres are "sites of both stability and instability," and the notion of *addressivity*: an
utterance's boundaries are set by "a change of speaking subjects," so every genre has a built-in
attitude toward its audience. Genres "knit together with specific objects and with the belief
systems... peculiar to particular professions" and express "the tacit sets of shared values that
constitute common sense for particular groups."

The claim that gives the chapter its edge:

> "Most importantly, genres are inherently ideological; they embody the unexamined or tacit way
> of performing some social action. Hence they can represent the ways that a dominant élite does
> things."

Her five questions — a usable analytic kit for any genre, including the ones you write at work:

1. Where does this genre come from?
2. What are the **stabilizing** (recurrent conventions and structures) features?
3. What are the **destabilizing** features?
4. What values and beliefs are instantiated within the set of practices?
5. Who can, or cannot, use this genre — and how does it affect its users?

## The Hierarchy It Sits In

Schön's *technical rationality*: professional activity is "instrumental problem solving made
rigorous by the application of scientific theory and technique," and the resulting ranking runs
basic science → applied science → technical skills of day-to-day practice. Practitioners'
"artistry," their ability to handle "complex, 'messy'" problems, gets devalued because it isn't
rigorous in that sense. Across professions, "those disciplines which present themselves as
'rigorous practitioners of a science-based knowledge' generally have a higher status."

Schryer's warning is that Schön's fix is harder than he thinks: "his enterprise is more difficult
than he might imagine," because the division is held in place by genres, not just attitudes.

Latour's *Pasteurization of France* supplies the century-long precedent. Hygienists took up
Pasteur immediately; practitioners resisted, because they were "translators not of public health
but of a multitude of doctor–patient relationships." The battle was fought in journals — *Revue
Scientifique* against *Concours Médical* — and settled by a deal: the Pasteurians developed
diphtheria serums usable only *after* a physician had diagnosed, so doctors "could retain their
control over curing diseases." Dr Jeanne's 1958 lament is the sound of the losing side:

> "The ardor and skill of the champion of our old clinical methods were wasted, for the adversary
> advancing against him was not a theoretician, one of those dreamers who create a fashion... but
> it was a scientist, it was the experimental method, it was progress..."

## What It Looks Like on the Ground

**The ethics panel.** Undergraduates were troubled by live-animal surgical practice, especially
after an anaesthetic failed in the "bunny labs." Clinicians understood the concern but argued the
training prevents future errors. The researchers sympathized — and the shape of their sympathy is
the whole chapter in miniature:

> "I am very uneasy about the ethical implications of using animals to add to your own data
> banks. In research, it is more defensible since you are adding to information banks, not just
> improving your own expertise."

Schryer's gloss: it was ethical to sacrifice animals "if their deaths led to new knowledge, but
not appropriate if their deaths only served to enhance the students' surgical skills." Collective
knowledge outranks individual competence — as an *ethical* claim, not merely an epistemic one.

**"Overwhelmosis."** The students' own word for the inundation of memorized basics. Veterinary
students "have traditionally studied every biological entity except humankind."

**The insult exchange.** A pathologist "lamented that students were too influenced by the
clinicians, and that clinicians did not research or publish." A clinician's counter: students "can
memorize 'like stink'... but they are not lost in their books, and they cannot solve clinical
problems." The clinician described herself as "a clinician first, then a teacher, then an
administrator and finally a researcher," had board exams rather than a dissertation, and — the
telling detail — "may not get her tenure, since she may not publish enough to remain at the
college."

**The binary.** Schryer's own summary table of the competing values:

| Research | Practice |
| --- | --- |
| Science | Medicine |
| Information development | Case problem solving |
| Memorization of facts | Problem or case-oriented teaching |
| Communalistic | Individualistic |
| Long term | Short term, immediate |
| Validity | Ambiguity and complexity |
| Methods | Skills |
| Education | Training |

She immediately qualifies it — "it would be simplistic to suggest that all health professions
divide simply and easily along these lines" — and the Purdue reformulation report shows the
hierarchy stated out loud: "Education is the instruction in and cultivation of an understanding
of the system of ideas and conviction which are valued by society... Education is generalizing,
expanding," whereas "Training is the mastery of knowledge and skills by practice and
apprenticeship... Training is focused." As she notes: "The value system at work in these
definitions is self-evident."

## Genre 1 — IMRDS, the Experimental Article

Introduction (*why?*), Methods (*how?*), Results (*what?*), Discussion (*so what?*), Summary. On
the surface a neutral heuristic. The chapter's job is to show it isn't.

Bazerman: the research article evolved from narrative letters between experimenters into "a
logical and empirical juggernaut, with every step in the reasoning backed up with carefully
described experimental experiences precisely related to the formal proposition." Newton's
invention of the second-person method section "turns readers into virtual witnesses... compelled
to cooperate with the writer in reconstructing the experiment."

And it is a reconstruction. Knorr-Cetina found "the published account is a reversed version of
the actual research process." Prelli:

> "Research articles are thus more persuasive because the articles present an image of
> determinacy in science and obscure the influences of historical and situational circumstances
> and possibilities of alternate approaches and judgments."

Swales's own CARS moves show up here as what an introduction must do: establish the relevance of
the field, locate the project within it, and "occupy and defend a particular niche."

The handbook advice is where the ideology stops hiding. Lindsay's *A Guide to Scientific Writing*
tells novices the article is "no more and no less than an objective and accurate account" — and
then tells them how to build the hypothesis: "It is not a statement of fact, but a statement
which takes us just beyond known facts... A good hypothesis exists on the cutting edge between
the old and the new, past and future fact. It creates new space." On methods, Schryer's line is
the sharpest in the section: "the art of writing methods sections exists in knowing what one can
safely omit." Methods are "rhetorical enthymeme structures" — only the initiated recognize a
"guarded culture instrument." And on discussions Lindsay "is at his most overtly rhetorical":
guide the reader "by logical steps to see things from your point of view," so that "you can
manipulate the reader into ranking the priorities of your arguments in the same way as you have."

## Genre 2 — POVMR, the Medical Record

The Problem Oriented Veterinary Medical Record, a veterinary variant of Weed's 1969 POMR, which
replaced the older Source-Oriented Record. Weed's complaint about SOR was that it "encouraged
physicians to jump to diagnostic conclusions," produced records comprehensible "only to their
initial writers," and so could be used for neither evaluation nor research. He wanted records to
"mirror the medical problem solving process itself" — to become "audits of action."

Structure: **Data base** (signalment, complaints, history, physical exam) → **Problem list**
(numbered) → **Initial plan** (diagnostic, therapeutic, client education) → **Progress notes**
(numbered to the problem; data, assessment, plans) → **Discharge summary**. Students are taught
*not* to diagnose prematurely: configurations of symptoms are provisionally named "problems," and
the record cycles as data changes the assessment which changes the plan which produces more data.

> [!note] The detail worth remembering
> Human medicine's POMR uses **SOAP** — Subjective, Objective, Assess, Plan. The veterinary
> variant drops the S, because "animals cannot talk. So they only collect data." A genre
> deformed by the fact that the patient has no account of itself.

## The Comparison, Which Is the Point

| | IMRDS | POVMR |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kind | **Reporting** — "the writer goes back in time towards a set of events" | **Recording** — "as little time as possible must elapse between events and their recording" |
| Epistemology | Research is "an effort of 'bricolage'; yet IMRDS turns it into a work of 'engineering'" | "Variables cannot be eliminated; they can only be discovered, identified and managed" |
| Problems | Selected, simplified, controlled | Given — "'zebra' cases, cases with multiple and rare interacting problems" |
| Purpose | Persuade; "inherently argumentative" | Intervene — records "order treatments" and act "immediately to change phenomenological events"; and, "in today's litigious world, records are acts of justification" |
| Audience | Hyperaware of a critical, wide audience | Narrow, socialized insiders; prose "extraordinarily elliptical" |
| Structure | Moves visually signalled by headings | "never signalled in these kinds of texts with heading or with any other typographical feature" |
| References | Extensive bibliographies, aligning the lab with other labs | "rarely have extensive reference sections" |
| Status | "simply deemed more valid, more 'real' (i.e. scientific)" | — |

Law's funnel is the best image for what IMRDS does to a reader:

> "The mouth of such a funnel is broad in order to suck in as wide an audience as possible,
> focused on a particular series of points. Interests are thus channelled and flow along a course
> that is determined by the linked series of interessements. These are deployed by the authors in
> order to prevent escapes — to ensure that the reader reaches the approved conclusion and then
> moves with this back to the general once more."

The consequence at grand rounds: IMRDS-trained graduate students "always connected their cases to
wider interests," while clinical-area students "usually presented in the POVMR format, and rarely
connected their cases either to other cases or to wider research interests. As a result they were
often critiqued, even by clinicians. Their research was seen as unsubstantial and invalid."

## The "Data Banks" Problem

The phrase recurs and she flags it as a metaphor with consequences. Student-practitioners "were
referred to as 'data banks' or were directed to file information in their data banks." Her
objection:

> "The metaphor assumed that students were passive and that as practitioners they would not
> produce valid 'data' themselves. In fact, one of the problems this college faces is that after
> graduation most of its students do not contribute to the college's development."

The genre trained them out of producing knowledge, and then the college wondered why they didn't.

## Quotes Worth Keeping

> "Genres are inherently ideological; they embody the unexamined or tacit way of performing some
> social action. Hence they can represent the ways that a dominant élite does things."

> "Research articles are thus more persuasive because the articles present an image of
> determinacy in science and obscure the influences of historical and situational circumstances
> and possibilities of alternate approaches and judgments." (Prelli)

> "Research is essentially an effort of 'bricolage'; yet IMRDS turns it into a work of
> 'engineering'."

> "The art of writing methods sections exists in knowing what one can safely omit."

> "In research, it is more defensible since you are adding to information banks, not just
> improving your own expertise."

> "These forces can define groups and the work they can do; they can even keep members of
> apparently the same discourse community separate and apart."

> "Change, in my view, will come very slowly indeed to professions like medicine and engineering.
> In these professions, their genres, their ways of speaking, writing, reading and listening,
> deeply enact their ideology."

## Key Takeaways

- **Genre is not a container, it's an actor.** The split between lab and clinic isn't reflected
  by IMRDS and POVMR; it's reproduced by them, every time someone learns to write one.
- **"Stabilized-for-now"** is the whole theory in one hyphenated phrase — conventions that look
  like natural form are a truce, and truces can be renegotiated.
- **The hierarchy is enforced by which genre counts as knowledge.** Same college, same patients,
  same technical vocabulary; one genre gets you tenure and one doesn't.
- **Objectivity is a rhetorical achievement.** Virtual witnesses, reversed accounts, omitted
  methods, funnelled discussions. IMRDS earns its authority by hiding the bricolage.
- **Direct rebuttal to Swales.** Criterion 1 assumes broadly agreed common public goals. Schryer's
  college nominally has them and is still two communities — so genre can subdivide a discourse
  community below the level Swales's criteria can see.
- Schryer is not anti-IMRDS. Her recommendation is that both groups learn both genres, and she
  notes consultancy work as an emerging role needing "both sets of skills."

## Open Questions

- Her five questions are a general kit. Applied to the genres I actually write — a PR
  description, an incident postmortem, an ADR, a commit message — which is the IMRDS and which is
  the POVMR? The postmortem looks like a recording genre being graded as a reporting one.
- IMRDS "turns bricolage into engineering." Is that dishonesty or compression? Every discipline
  writes up a cleaned path; the question is whether the cleaning is a lie or a service to the
  reader, and Schryer doesn't quite decide.
- The POVMR drops Subjective because animals can't talk. What gets dropped from a genre because
  of who *can't* speak into it — and who notices the missing field once the form is standard?
- She thinks change will "come very slowly." IMRDS is thirty years older now and preprints, data
  availability statements, and registered reports have all pushed at it. Did the genre move, or
  did it absorb the pressure and stay stabilized?
- For [[Assignment 1 - Discourse Community Archive]]: if I archive a community, whose genre am I
  writing *in*? An archive with annotations is closer to POVMR than to IMRDS — and the
  Archivist's Note is the move that tries to make it IMRDS.
