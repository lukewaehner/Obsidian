# Reading — Shipka, "Rethinking Composition / Rethinking Process"

Reading for [[Week 01 - Writing Process]] · [[Advanced Writing in Tech]]

> [!cite] Citation
> Shipka, Jody. "Rethinking Composition / Rethinking Process." *Toward a Composition Made
> Whole*, University of Pittsburgh Press, 2011, pp. 18–38. JSTOR,
> https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt5hjqkk.5

> [!abstract] One-line version
> Writing has always been material and multimodal, so both what composition studies *counts as
> composing* and how it *researches composing processes* are too narrow — and the fix is not
> "add computers," it's attending to the full distributed, embodied, situated activity out of
> which any text emerges.

## The Setup: Handa's Two Classrooms

Shipka opens on Carolyn Handa's (2004) contrast between the old classroom (desks in rows,
podium, blackboard, the occasional overhead projector or film — visuals as "incidental props")
and today's "fully networked" one, where students' lives are "nothing if not visual."

She pushes back on both halves:

- **On the students.** Most of the students she's taught use email and social media, but "far
  fewer have experience using Flash, Photoshop, PageMaker, Dreamweaver, or Premiere Pro, and far
  fewer still have known how to 'write in JavaScript and HTML'" (19). Access ≠ fluency.
- **On the old classroom.** It was never *less* visual. Her 1995 undergrad course: the film, the
  overhead, the children's book the professor brought as a prop, lunches being eaten midday and
  their sounds and smells, the professor's clothing, gestures, tone of voice, facial expressions,
  desks in rows vs. rearranged into a circle, and — critically — handwriting legible enough that
  "little time was spent trying to crack that code" (19).

She's explicit that this isn't a tribute to a good teacher (20). The point is that all of those
were **viable communicative modes** carrying information students had to negotiate, and none of
them were ever the object of an assignment.

## Two Cautions

1. **Against a narrow definition of *technology*.** Handa's description doesn't appear to count
   the blackboards, podium, or desks as technologies — nor the "books, light switches,
   lightbulbs, floor and ceiling tiles, clocks, watches, water bottles, aluminum pop-top cans,
   clothing, chalk, pens, paper, handwriting" (20). Using *technology* to mean only the newest
   computers decides in advance "who and what it is that we empower or discount" (21).
2. **Against ignoring the multimodal dimensions of any communicative situation** — wired or not.
   "Classroom experiences certainly demand this of them, but so does driving, crossing a street,
   or running to the grocery store" (21).

> [!quote] The chiasmus at the center of the chapter
> Selber (2004): "a narrow definition of literacy . . . fails to encourage a situated view of
> technology."
> Shipka (31): "*a narrow definition of technology fails to encourage richly nuanced, situated
> views of literacy*."

Related: **Slatin (2008)** — the *invisibility of mature technologies* is why the field neglected
the material dimensions of writing; highly visible new tech is useful mainly because it directs
attention back to "the physical processes by which texts are brought into being" (168).
**Sloane (1999)** — new tools "throw into sharp relief" what was always there in paper-based
composing.

## Part 1 — Rethinking "Composition"

The disciplinary commitment to *written* discourse is too narrow.

- **Sean Williams (2001):** composition is "largely conservative" — it "cling[s] to the idea of
  writing about representation systems in verbal text because that's what we do in composition."
  Subject matter broadened over time; **form stayed fixed** at linear print.
- **Sirc (epigraph):** we "haven't really evolved an idea of writing that fully reflects the
  splendor of the medium." Our texts "are almost wholly determined by the texts that have gone
  before."
- **Jewitt and Kress (2003):** meanings are "made, distributed, received, interpreted and remade
  . . . through many representational and communicative modes — not just through language."
- **Wendy Bishop (2002):** "essay writing as usual" is the "backbone of program work."
- **Yancey (2004, CCCC chair's address):** yes, writing *is* words on paper — "*but* who also
  compose words and images and create audio files on Web logs (blogs), in word processors, with
  video editors and Web editors and in e-mail and on presentation software and in instant
  messaging and on listservs and on bulletin boards — and no doubt in whatever genre will emerge
  in the next ten minutes."
- **Odell and Prell (1999):** go further — *composition* is the misnomer. "Musicians composed;
  what we were doing was writing." They want attention to the "interanimation of words, visual
  images, and page (or screen) design."

### The buried history: the "communications" approach (1940s–60s)

Shipka's strongest move is showing that today's arguments were already made sixty years ago and
lost.

- CCCC formed in the late 1940s around "the problem of freshman English."
- **Harold Briggs (1947 NCTE paper, pub. 1948)** compared communication programs at Minnesota,
  Iowa, and Southern California to the "typical traditional freshman English program." Traditional
  programs are "pretty much satisfied with things as they are or as they used to be"; "the
  traditional mind is always a closed mind," while the communication teacher "must have an open
  and receptive mind."
- Communication programs **studied media as content** — newspaper, radio, moving picture,
  magazines, books — asking "what they communicate to us today and how they achieve this."
- **The unity-of-content complaint.** The traditional English course is "a hash of strange
  ingredients. One day one studies punctuation, the next day paragraphing, the next day an essay
  on jargon, or frying fish cakes." USC's answer: "our business is with language, spoken and
  written." Briggs: "in history, one writes on history; in economics, one writes on economics,
  [then] in English courses . . . one ought to write on some problem concerning the use of
  language." → **This predates Downs and Wardle's (2007) writing-about-writing argument by sixty
  years.**
- Communication treated as "a subject matter *and* as an act or process" (Dunn 1946), drawing
  content from psychology of language, philosophy, anthropology, neurology, and electronics, and
  requiring "continuous acquisition of new data." An **"experimental attitude"**: not "has it
  been tried?" or "what do the authorities say?" but "*Let's try it, and test it*" (Dow 1948).
- Traditional freshman English was blamed for teaching students that writing is "an impractical,
  useless, academic barrier to be surmounted with the least possible effort" (Dow 1948) — that
  "schoolbook English is a special variety of language found only in the English classroom and
  used only by English teachers" (Dean 1959).
- The alternative: students' **"language worlds"** (Dunn 1946) — the dining room, the dance
  floor, the church, sports pages, comic sheets, ads, editorials, "the sounds emanating from the
  radio" — treating "the communicative process as a dynamic whole" and making students aware of
  how "one is conditioned to language symbols by muscle sets, gestures, tone and the words
  themselves." Writing, reading, speaking, and evaluating as "a function of place, time, sex,
  age and many other elements of life" (Malstrom 1956). Outcome: **"more flexible and reflexive
  communicants."**

### Why it lost

- Charged with being **overly ambitious** — "there was not time enough to teach students what
  they needed to know about writing in order to improve that skill set."
- **George and Trimbur (1999):** the rationale for staying with written discourse rested on
  students' "woeful preparation in writing and the implied warrant of a generalized literacy
  crisis." The metacommunicative/reflective aims were dismissed: "**writing comes first,
  consciousness-raising second**."
- 1959 Gerber Committee report → proposal to amend CCCC's purpose to "*especially in written
  discourse*"; a Committee on Possible Name Change floated "Council on College Writing." The
  amendments never passed, but the outcome held anyway: "an emerging structure of feeling that
  composition, as a discipline devoted to the study and teaching of written discourse, was at
  the core of CCCC's identity."
- **"Whatever happened to the 4th C?"** The fourth *C* (communication) pointed at "the worldly,
  the actual, the material," reminding us that "writing, like other types of composition
  (musical, graphic, handicraft, engineering design) [is] an act of labor that quite literally
  fashions the world" (George and Trimbur 1999, 697).

### Don't just study artifacts

- **DePew (2007):** digital writing studies tend to "eliminate or de-emphasize the human feature
  of digital writing"; examine "more features of the communicative situation rather than merely
  an artifact it produces" — composer's intentions, audience response, local contexts of
  production and reception.
- **Brandt's party analogy (1990, 76):** reading meaning off a static text is like "coming upon
  the scene of a party after it is over and everybody has gone home, being left to imagine from
  the remnants what the party must have been like."

## Part 2 — Process Revisited

Composition was transformed circa 1971 (Emig, *The Composing Processes of Twelfth Graders*)
through the early 1980s by asking "what really happens when people write?" Then process research
faded — by the mid-1980s far fewer studies, by the 1990s fewer still. Four critiques did it:

| Critique | Charge | Sources |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Micro-theory** | Lab-like settings and think-aloud protocols obscured the real settings and conditions writers work under | Reither 1994; Matsuhashi 1987 |
| **Individualist / expressivist** | Framed the writer as "a primary site and agent of writing," cut off from the world; overlooked the texts, participants, and activities in play "before the impulse to write is even possible" | Bizzell, Cooper, Heath, LeFevre, Reither; Bawarshi 2003 |
| **Process "en masse"** | Classrooms "inculcate[d] a particular method of composing" set by the product teachers expected; freewriting/brainstorming created "a privatized economy of invention," implying writing starts in the writer, not in a relationship with the world | Couture 1999; Harris 1997; Bawarshi 2003 |
| **Generalizability** | If all communication is "radically contingent, radically situational," teaching *the* writing process is "misguided, unproductive [and] misleading" | Russell 1999; Faigley 1994; Olson 1999 |

> [!quote] Brodkey (1996) on the "writer-writing-alone" picture
> "Having seen so many postcards of the Grand Canyon, we can hardly look at it, much less
> remember it, as anything other than glossy three-by-fives. It is likewise difficult to see or
> remember writing as other than it is portrayed in the scene of writing if that picture frames
> our experience and governs our memories."
>
> Her fix is not to swap in a noisier picture but to "**tell new stories about the old picture,
> and to add pictures that tell altogether different stories about writers and writing**" (58).

**The second generation** got more social and situated — and overcorrected into fixity. Even
naturalistic studies "overlooked the messy, multimodal, and highly distributed dimensions of
writers' processes," staying "firmly fixed on the official side of writing" (Prior and Shipka
2003): the planned, responded-to texts, and not the other times, places, and resources involved.
They missed writers' "multiple external connections" (Drew 2001), and the everyday nonlinguistic
sign systems — "labels on cereal boxes, traffic signs, telephone book yellow pages" (Witte 1992).
**Syverson (1999):** "we have continued to focus on readers, writers, and texts as independent
objects."

### What a better framework has to do

- **Trace the material and the labor.** Trimbur (2004): a "thoroughgoing reconceptualization of
  the writer at work" locates the composer "in the labor process, in relation to the available
  means of production" — writers as "makers of the means of producing meaning out of the
  available resources of representation."
- **Account for place.** Reynolds (2004): attend to the "*where* of writing" — not just physical
  location but "the sense of place and space that readers and writers bring with them to the
  intellectual work of writing."
- **Treat writers as travelers, not students.** Drew (2001): "Naming the writers in our
  classrooms 'students' is one way of confining them, reducing them to knowable objects, by
  intimating that one aspect of their discursive and intellectual lives is accurately
  representative of the whole." The classroom is one space among many they move through.
- **Attend to embodied activity and co-practice.** Prior and Hengst (2010): writers "are never
  just talking, just reading, just writing."

> [!example] Shipka's conference-paper example (36–37)
> Writing the talk also means: consulting the CFP, previous publications, outlines, sketches,
> to-do lists; drawing on prior experience with the genre; talking about it with family, friends,
> and current students; rereading and gesturing at passages; moving bits of text around; tweaking
> line spacing, margins, and fonts so it's easy to see and read while delivering; and deciding
> when, where, and for how long to work — around eating, sleeping, working, working out,
> cleaning, seeing friends, and hobbies.

### What the point is *not*

Not to recover the whole truth about *the* composing process. Reynolds (2004): "Crossing a
street or skimming a newspaper are acts contingent upon a multitude of variables that can never
be neatly isolated." Not to teach novices to compose like experts, and not to prove a
cause-and-effect between a text's quality and its site of production. The point is **"to make the
complex and highly distributed processes involved with the production, reception, circulation,
and valuation of texts more visible"** (38).

Chapter 2 turns to **mediated activity theory** as the framework for doing that.

## Key Takeaways

- "Multimodal" is not a synonym for "digital." Chalk, handwriting, gesture, room layout, and
  smell were always modes; new tools make the old materiality visible, they don't create it.
- Naming shapes the field. Dropping the fourth *C* wasn't a vote — it was a slow settling of
  identity onto written discourse, and it cost the discipline a whole research program.
- Process research died of being too narrow twice: first too individual and lab-bound, then too
  fixed on the official, planned, textual side of situated activity.
- The unit of analysis is the wrong size. Not the text, not even the writer — the distributed
  activity across people, tools, places, and time out of which the text emerges.
- Studying the artifact alone is arriving after the party.

## Open Questions

- Shipka wants process made visible, but she's explicit there's no complete account. What's the
  stopping rule for a *practically useful* description of a composing process?
- Software has the richest process trace of any writing practice — commit history, PR threads,
  CI logs, issue trackers. Does that make engineering an unusually good site for the research
  she wants, or does the trace just create a new "official side of writing" to get fixed on?
- If we take Drew's "travelers" seriously, what does assessment look like when the classroom is
  only one of the spaces the composing happened in?
