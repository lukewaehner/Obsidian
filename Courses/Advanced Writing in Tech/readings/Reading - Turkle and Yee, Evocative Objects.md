# Reading — Turkle and Yee, *Evocative Objects* (excerpt)

Reading for [[Week 03 - Discourse Communities]] · [[Advanced Writing in Tech]]

## Turkle — The Memory Closet

She opens autobiographically, which for an academic introduction is itself the argument. Her
grandparents' Brooklyn apartment had a kitchen closet set high near the ceiling holding all the
family keepsakes. From six to thirteen or fourteen she climbed onto the kitchen table every
weekend to reach it. "The rules were that I was allowed to look at anything in the closet, but I
was always to put it back. The closet seemed to me of infinite dimensions, infinite depth."

Her biological father had left when she was two and was unmentionable. She was searching without
knowing it:

> "At the time I didn't know what I was looking for. I think they did. I was looking, without
> awareness, for the one who was missing."

> "Once I found a photograph of a man standing on a boardwalk with his face cut out of the
> picture. I never asked whose face it was; I knew. And I knew enough never to mention the
> photograph, for fear that it too would disappear. It was precious to me. The image had been
> attacked, but it contained so many missing puzzle pieces. What his hands looked like. That he
> wore lace-up shoes. That his pants were tweed."

Then Paris, the late 1960s, and Lévi-Strauss's **bricolage** — "a way of combining and
recombining a closed set of materials to come up with new ideas." The pun she keeps: "Material
things, for Lévi-Strauss, were goods-to-think-with and, following the pun in French, they were
good-to-think-with as well." But the theory arrived "in the cool, cognitive light of French
intellectual life," while her own childhood version "had been clues for tracing my lost father, an
experience of bricolage with a high emotional intensity." Hence her correction to the theory:
"I began to consider bricolage as a passionate practice."

## The Thesis

> "We find it familiar to consider objects as useful or aesthetic, as necessities or vain
> indulgences. We are on less familiar ground when we consider objects as companions to our
> emotional lives or as provocations to thought. The notion of evocative objects brings together
> these two less familiar ideas, underscoring the inseparability of thought and feeling in our
> relationship to things. **We think with the objects we love; we love the objects we think
> with.**"

The essayists' brief was never the object's instrumental power — "not on how fast the train
travels or how fast the computer calculates — but on the object as a companion in life
experience."

## The Argument Underneath: Against the Rank Order of Knowing

This is the part that connects to Schryer and is easy to skim past.

> "Behind the reticence to examine objects as centerpieces of thought was the value placed, at
> least within the Western tradition, on formal, propositional ways of knowing. In thinking about
> science, certainly, abstract reasoning was traditionally recognized as a standard, canonical
> style; many have taken it to be synonymous with knowledge altogether."

And the twist — the two thinkers who rehabilitated concrete thinking also demoted it:

> "It is poignant that Claude Lévi-Strauss and the psychologist Jean Piaget, who each in their
> way contributed to a fundamental revaluation of the concrete in the mid-twentieth century, also
> undermined the concrete thinking they promoted."

Piaget made children's "close-to-the-object" reasoning "a stage in a progression to a formal
thinking style." Lévi-Strauss preferred to call bricolage "prior" rather than "premature" — "yet
it was not fully equal." The rescue came from the 1980s onward, and notably from inside science
itself: laboratories "were shown to be places where discoveries are made in a concrete, ad hoc
fashion, and only later recast into canonically accepted formalisms," Nobel laureates described
relating to their materials "in a tactile and playful manner," and feminist scholars documented
contextual reasoning across domains.

> [!note] Same claim, two courses apart
> "Discoveries are made in a concrete, ad hoc fashion, and only later recast into canonically
> accepted formalisms" is Schryer's "research is essentially an effort of 'bricolage'; yet IMRDS
> turns it into a work of 'engineering'" — arrived at independently, thirteen years apart. Both
> say the write-up reverses the work.

## Turkle's Vocabulary Worth Stealing

- **Boundary objects** and "the general principle that objects are active life presences."
- **The uncanny.** Freud's "known of old yet unfamiliar." Turkle's gloss is better than the
  original: "The uncanny is not what is most frightening and strange. It is what seems close but
  'off,' distorted enough to be creepy." Her example is a museum mummy as an author's double.
- **Liminality** (Victor Turner). Objects associated with transitional, threshold times are "rich
  with creative possibility" — she follows a young man boarding the Melbourne train out of the
  Australian outback, "poised between states of being," where "everything solid and known can be
  called into question."
- **The biography of things** (Kopytoff) — how "a new car becomes a new skin."
- **Objects as parts of the self.** "A young child believes her stuffed bunny rabbit can read her
  mind; a diabetic is at one with his glucometer." Extended to "the glowing screens of our
  laptops, our iPods, and our BlackBerries."
- **"When objects are lost, subjects are found."** Freud: "the shadow of the object fell upon the
  ego."

> "Evocative objects bring philosophy down to earth. When we focus on objects, physicians and
> philosophers, psychologists and designers, artists and engineers are able to find common
> ground in everyday experience."

She closes on Whitman: "A child went forth everyday/ and the first object he look'd upon, that
object he became."

## Yee — "The Archive"

Mid-1990s, Fondation Le Corbusier in Paris, housed in Le Corbusier's own Villa La Roche and Villa
Jeanneret. Her task: build a virtual model of his unbuilt Palace of the Soviets. The pleasure is
in the mess of the material — letters, a datebook, "hand-scrawled calculations in the margins of
sketches" she did the math along with. And a newspaper clipping critiquing his design, on which he
had written "Idiote":

> "I could trace the precision and force of the incision into the newsprint. I felt his
> frustration, his spirit."

**The drawing.** A scroll so large it spilled over the table and she had to walk around it. She
expected gloves and wasn't given any.

> "I didn't know if I could or should touch it. And then the curator touched it, so I went ahead
> and touched it too with my bare hands. All I could think about was that this was Le Corbusier's
> original drawing. It was meticulously hand-drawn, but the drawing was dirty. There were marks on
> it, smudges, fingerprints, the marks of other hands, and now I added mine."

**The paper squares.** A little parchment bag of colored paper squares — the elements Le Corbusier
used to program the building, one color per function. "I imagined how he fiddled with these little
bits of paper until he found a programmatic configuration that pleased him; I fiddled with them
too." That sentence is the whole of Turkle's thesis enacted: thinking *with* the object, by
repeating its maker's gesture.

**The turn.** On her last day the curator delivers the news as good news:

> "Oh, you'll love what we're doing now. You won't ever have to come here! You won't ever have to
> look at these drawings anymore! We're putting them all in a digital database!"

The same drawing appeared as a small icon; click and it got larger.

> "If I had accessed this drawing from home, I would never have grasped its dimensions, I would
> never have known that it was stored separately, carefully rolled, that it was dirty with smudges
> and fingerprints. The scans for the Web site gave me nothing to touch. I felt no awe about the
> scale of the drawings."

And the moment of self-recognition — sitting at the curator's machine in Paris, she idly links to
MIT: "Feeling like a saddened citizen of the information world, I felt transported to MIT through
the link. I had a moment of shame."

**The frame she borrows from Turkle** — instrumental vs. subjective technology, "what technology
does for us and what it does to us as people":

> "It allowed me to do things that I could not do before. I could search it, manipulate it, copy
> it, save it, share it. But what did it do to me? It made the drawings feel anonymous and it made
> me feel anonymous."

She resists nostalgia, though. She calls herself "fortunate to be in a generation of designers
that straddles both physical and digital worlds," and her ending is questions rather than a
verdict:

> "Will future students be satisfied to simply understand the algorithms that generated their
> designs? Will we still crave some pilgrimage such as the one I took to Paris? But there will be
> no place to go; it will all be on a collection of servers... Will we be able to feel the human
> connection through digital archives? Will we care?"

## Piaget — The Coda

The excerpt the editors pair with Yee, and it retroactively explains why touching the drawing
mattered:

> "I think that human knowledge is essentially active. To know is to assimilate reality into
> systems of transformations... knowing an object does not mean copying it—it means acting upon
> it. It means constructing systems of transformation that can be carried out on or with this
> object."

And the last line is the one to keep, because it answers Yee's complaint precisely: when we act on
an object, we can abstract from the action itself — "the abstraction is drawn not from the object
that is acted upon, but from the action itself." Yee lost the *action*, not the image. The scan
preserved the object and destroyed the operation.

## Key Takeaways

- **Objects carry arguments.** Turkle's licence for the archive assignment: an annotated object is
  not an illustration of a claim, it is the claim's evidence and often its origin.
- **The method is: choose an object and follow its associations.** "Where does it take you; what do
  you feel; what are you able to understand?" That is literally the annotation prompt.
- **The concrete has been ranked below the abstract, and that ranking is contingent.** Turkle's
  intellectual history pairs exactly with Schryer's hierarchy of basic science over practice —
  both are describing the same prejudice from different disciplines.
- **Yee's essay is the model annotation.** One object (a drawing), a specific sensory detail
  (smudges, the incision of "Idiote"), an emotional register, and a general claim about
  digitization. Roughly the 150–200 words the assignment asks for, done well.
- **Instrumental vs. subjective is the reusable pair.** For any tool: what does it do *for* me,
  and what does it do *to* me? The second question is the one nobody in the room asks.
- **Digitization is a genre change, not a format change.** The database preserved every drawing
  and destroyed the scale, the rolling, the dirt, the walking around it — i.e. everything that was
  not information.

## Open Questions

- Yee's loss is real, but her access was a privilege — a funded MIT trip to Paris. The curator's
  database serves everyone who can't fly. Is "I had a moment of shame" self-aware about that, or
  is it shame at her own convenience rather than at the exclusion the physical archive enforced?
- Turkle wants objects taken seriously as thinking partners, and then the entire book is made of
  *prose about* objects. Does writing an evocative object down re-abstract it — the same reversal
  Schryer catches IMRDS doing to bricolage?
- If knowing is acting on an object, what counts as an action in a born-digital archive? Version
  history, diffing, forking, running the code — these are actions on the object. Is Yee mourning
  physicality, or mourning a set of actions that simply have not been invented for the new medium
  yet?
- "We think with the objects we love" — the essayists all chose objects they had feelings about.
  What about objects that shaped your thinking and that you resent? The archive of a discourse
  community probably contains more of those.
- For [[Assignment 1 - Discourse Community Archive]]: seven objects with a cohesive theme. Do the
  objects need to be things a member would recognize as significant, or is the archivist allowed
  to pick what the community itself overlooks — which is where Swales's invisible lexis would
  live?
