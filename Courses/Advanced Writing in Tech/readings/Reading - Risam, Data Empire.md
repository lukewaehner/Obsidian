## Introduction — The Counts We Keep

**The hook.** As a child she slept under a poster of the Lascaux bull, which she took to be cave
art. In 2023, Ben Bacon and UCL researchers argued that its dots and Y-marks encode lunar months
and breeding cycles, a record of when to hunt. "It was art as information."

**We are the data species.** The oldest known dataset is the **Lebombo Bone**, a 43,000-year-old
baboon bone from the South Africa–Eswatini border with 29 deliberate notches. It resembles the
calendar sticks San peoples use today. Similar tally bones turn up in the Congo (possibly tracking a
menstrual cycle), in Czechia (a wolf bone, possibly a double lunar
period), and in France. "Long before we wrote stories, we kept score." "Our first archives were
captured in bones and stones, not words."

**The pivot from memory to power.** At first, marks served the group by telling it when to hunt
and when to plant. "But the same signs could just as easily fix a debt, define a boundary, name an
enemy."

**Records were acts.** Before the twentieth century, nobody separated data, information, and
knowledge. "An exchange of shells meant a deal was done… these were at once records and acts." The
data → information → knowledge hierarchy comes from how computers process data.

**"Nothing was ever given."** *Data* comes from Latin *datum*, "that which is given," and she
traces the same sense through Sanskrit, Greek, Dutch, Hebrew, Mandarin, and Arabic. Against it, she
quotes Lisa Gitelman: "raw data is an oxymoron." "A notch in bone—someone carved it. A census
return—a person filled it out."

**Her Social Security story.** A clerk found her coded as a noncitizen in one cell of a government
database, decades after she was naturalized at 16. Fixing it cost $150. After 2025, when those same
flawed records were used to target people for deportation, that one cell looked very different: "A
single record can redraw the borders of a life."

**Thesis.** "Our first technology of power was not wheat or the wheel but the ability to record."
Once records outlive their makers, other people can take and use them. The turn "from keeping time
to keeping score" is the root of tax ledgers, biometric databases, and predictive algorithms. "We
created records to remember, and they stayed to govern."

## Chapter 7 — Subjects of Calculation, 1760–1907

Subtitle: *how data claimed the mantle of science, making classification a governing force.* The
chapter is a string of cases, each showing the same move from a different angle.

### 1. Tupaia and the Endeavour: translation as extraction
Tupaia was a Ra'iatean navigator and priest whose knowledge "moved not through books but through
bodies, taught in chants." He sailed with Cook and Banks, drew a chart of more than 70 islands
organized by star paths and sailing times rather than latitude, and taught Banks more than 1,000
plant names. Banks turned the plant names into Latin classifications "that extracted the plants
from their living contexts." "Every star path he recited… became data." The British "did not absorb
Tupaia's knowledge so much as disassemble it," keeping what they could name and measure and
discarding the relationships that held it together. Tupaia died in Indonesia and "became a footnote
to their history," yet the Māori still shouted his name at Cook in 1773.

### 2. Caldas and the Royal Botanical Expedition: the tools turn
Spain funded the New Granada expedition to catalogue exploitable plants. The head of the Botanical
Garden boasted that twelve naturalists would do more for the empire "than could one hundred thousand
soldiers." But the Creole scientists who did the work came to know the land intimately, and "their
expertise began to feel like prerogative." Several joined the revolutionary government, and the
expedition's artist fought with Bolívar. Spain executed Caldas and others in 1816. Mastering the
empire's instruments had taught them to see themselves "as rightful owners of the land."

### 3. Sarah Baartman: the body as a dataset
Baartman was a Khoekhoe woman exhibited in London as the "Hottentot Venus." Georges Cuvier measured
and drew her, and after her death in 1815 he dissected her and displayed her remains, which stayed in
a Paris museum until 1974 and were repatriated nearly three decades later. Her case belongs to a
wider practice of "living dioramas" at world's fairs, and of Ota Benga in the Bronx Zoo in 1906. It
"treated bodies not as human lives but as data."

### 4. Morton, Nott & Gliddon: bad measurement with authority
Samuel Morton collected about 1,000 skulls, many grave-robbed, and filled them with seed or lead shot
to "measure" intelligence. His method was full of errors: the result depended on the filling
material, and women and adolescents weren't separated from adult men. Nott and Gliddon's *Types of
Mankind* (1854) went through 10 editions by 1871 and was used to defend slavery and the taking of
Native land. Frederick Douglass called it "scientific moonshine." Risam's point: "In its time… [this]
was not pseudoscience—it was mainstream science." Darwin's *Descent of Man* (1871) weakened the
theory but didn't end it.

### 5. Quetelet and Galton: the average and the deviant
Quetelet's *l'homme moyen*, the "average man," borrowed from astronomy's practice of averaging
error. "Governing meant managing deviation." Galton's Anthropometric Laboratory in South Kensington
(1884) charged threepence for a 17-measurement scorecard, and the public paid to become data. From
those numbers Galton developed **correlation, regression, standard deviation, and normal
distributions**. He also coined the word **eugenics**. "Formulas that turned prejudice into
probability."

### 6. Dewey: classification as hierarchy
The Decimal Classification (1876) gave Christianity the range 220–289 and put Islam, Judaism,
Buddhism, and Hinduism together in 290. It had no place for Indigenous cosmologies or oral
traditions. By 1927 it was used in 96% of US public libraries. "Cataloguers applied its codes by
rote. Readers absorbed its worldview as fact." Dewey's Library Bureau then sold the index cards and
filing systems that made "sortable units" physical.

### 7. Hollerith: identity becomes machine-readable
The 1880 census took eight years to tabulate. Taking ideas from railroad tickets that were punched
to mark passenger features, and from Jacquard loom cards, Hollerith built a punch-card tabulator,
and the 1890 census took three years. His company became **IBM** by 1924. "He had made human
identity machine-readable."

### 8. The Dawes Rolls: classification as dispossession
The Dawes Act of 1887 broke communal tribal land into allotments. The Dawes Commission, made up of
non-Native census takers and stenographers, decided who belonged to each tribe, often by looking at
people. **Blood quantum** was a new identity datum invented by the US government: "Kinship systems
re-emerged as percentages, treaties as land ledgers." The Perry brothers had to prove in court that
they were Chickasaw. More than 90 million acres were taken. People resisted by refusing to enroll,
filing appeals to delay decisions, and choosing which parent's lineage to emphasize.

### The close
"Data itself was beginning to govern—not metaphorically, but materially." "To be counted was now to
be categorized. To be categorized was to be calculated, and then constrained. To be left out was to
be erased." "Data masqueraded as destiny."

## Key Takeaways

- **Data is made, not given.** Every record encodes someone's choice about what matters. Gitelman:
  "raw data is an oxymoron."
- **Classification is power.** Whoever controls the categories (Dewey's decimals, Banks's Latin,
  the Dawes Rolls' fractions) controls what is visible and who counts.
- **Translation strips context.** Tupaia's knowledge was relational, and turning it into European
  data kept the fragments and discarded the relationships.
- **Measurement borrows authority it hasn't earned.** Morton's method was bad, but the numbers made
  it look like science.
- **Records outlive intentions.** Tallies made to remember end up used to govern.
- **The tools can turn.** The Creole naturalists learned to see through the empire's instruments,
  and Dawes enrollees used the system's contradictions to resist it.

  frightening version of the membership threshold, where an outside body decides who belongs to a
  community.

## Open Questions

- Is there any classification system that doesn't distort what it classifies, or is the goal just
  to make the classifier's choices visible?
- Risam uses Galton's statistics as evidence of how data went wrong, but those tools are still the
  basis of the field. Can you separate a method from where it came from?
- Where does software sit in this story? Schema design, database enums, and required form fields
  are all Dewey-style choices about what can be recorded.
