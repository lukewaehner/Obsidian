# Reading — Lamott, "Shitty First Drafts"

Reading for [[Week 01 - Writing Process]] · [[Advanced Writing in Tech]]

> [!cite] Citation
> Lamott, Anne. "Shitty First Drafts." *Language Awareness: Readings for College Writers*,
> edited by Paul Eschholz, Alfred Rosa, and Virginia Clark, 9th ed., Bedford/St. Martin's,
> 2005, pp. 93–96. Originally from *Bird by Bird* (1994).

## Thesis

Nobody writes a good first draft, including the writers you admire. The only reliable route to
a good second and third draft is permission to write a bad first one. Lamott is arguing against
a *product* model of writing — where the page is supposed to arrive finished — and for a
*process* model where the first pass is a disposable means, not a performance.

## The Argument in Sequence

1. **The fantasy of the uninitiated** (¶1). Non-writers imagine published writers sitting down
   confident and typing "fully formed passages as fast as a court reporter." False. The one
   writer she knows who does write elegant first drafts is disliked on principle.
2. **Writing is not rapturous** (¶2–3). "Very few writers really know what they are doing until
   they've done it." The right words don't "come pouring out like ticker tape." Even the writers
   whose prose reads most natural and fluid feel like they're pulling teeth.
3. **The child's draft** (¶4). Let it pour out and romp all over the place, knowing no one will
   see it and you can shape it later. The permission is what makes discovery possible — the
   thing you actually needed to say is often buried in the last line of the last paragraph of
   six crazy pages, and "there was no way to get to this without first getting through the first
   five and a half pages."
4. **The food-review case study** (¶5–9). Two days per review. Day one: go eat, take notes,
   write down everything funny anyone says. Day two: panic ("I'm toast"), stall, study her teeth
   in the mirror, make phone calls, come back, write a terrible bloated draft with a lead a full
   page long. Next day: colored pen, cut everything possible, find the real lead on page two,
   find a "kicky place to end it," write draft two. "It always turned out fine."
5. **The three drafts** (¶10). The framework worth memorizing.

## The Three Drafts

| Draft | Name | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | **Down draft** | Just get it down. Volume over quality, no editing. |
| 2nd | **Up draft** | Fix it up. Say what you mean more accurately; cut; relocate the lead and the ending. |
| 3rd | **Dental draft** | Check every tooth — loose, cramped, decayed, or healthy. Sentence-level. |

## Quotes Worth Keeping

> "Almost all good writing begins with terrible first efforts. You need to start somewhere.
> Start by getting something — anything — down on paper." (¶10)

> "The first draft is the child's draft, where you let it all pour out and then let it romp all
> over the place, knowing that no one is going to see it and that you can shape it later." (¶4)

> "Very few writers really know what they are doing until they've done it." (¶2)

## Discussion Questions

The three questions printed with the reading, with a starting position for each:

1. **What does "the fantasy of the uninitiated" (¶1) mean?**
   The belief that fluency on the page reflects fluency in the head — that a polished text is
   evidence of a smooth process. It is a misreading of *product* as *process*: readers only ever
   see finished drafts, so they infer the drafting looked like the result. It's the same error as
   inferring from clean merged code that nobody wrote three broken versions first.
2. **Why does she "trust the process — sort of, more or less" (¶7)?**
   Because trust in the process is retrospective, not felt in the moment. She has the evidence
   ("it always turned out fine") but panic returns with every new assignment (¶9 — the whole
   cycle repeats a month later). The hedge reads as generalizable, not personal: knowing the
   process works does not make the bad draft feel less bad while you're in it.
3. **Is a first draft about the product or the process?**
   Process. The draft is not a bad version of the finished thing; it's a *different kind of
   object* — raw material whose value is that it exists and can be cut. Judging it as product is
   the specific mistake that produces the paralysis she describes.

## Key Takeaways

- Separate generating from evaluating. The panic in ¶5 comes from trying to do both at once —
  she writes two sentences and X's them out, over and over, and gets nowhere.
- Cut *forward*: the real lead was on page two, and the real ending needed to be found, not
  planned. That only works if there's a bloated draft to find them in.
- Deadline pressure and stalling (mirror, phone calls, kitchen) are part of the process, not a
  failure of it. She still ships every month.
- The essay is written as advice but doubles as a *description* of one writer's actual composing
  process — which is exactly what [[Reading - Shipka, Rethinking Composition|Shipka]] argues the
  field stopped studying carefully.

## Open Questions

- Does the down/up/dental sequence survive in genres with hard structural conventions — a
  technical report, an API doc, an RFC — where the outline is largely given before you start?
- Lamott is writing about prose written alone. What happens to the down draft when the draft is
  in a shared doc, or a PR, and someone *is* going to see it?
