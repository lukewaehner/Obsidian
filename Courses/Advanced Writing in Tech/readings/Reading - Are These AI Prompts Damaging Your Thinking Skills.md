## Thesis

A news feature, not an argument — it assembles four studies and three interviewees around one
question: does offloading cognitive work to a chatbot degrade the thinking skills you were
supposed to be building? The evidence it gathers leans yes, and the framing it lands on is
**"their outputs are better but actually their learning is worse."** The piece never resolves the
question; it stages the disagreement between a researcher (Holmes) and a vendor (OpenAI).

## The Evidence It Assembles

| Study | Population | Finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **MIT**, published earlier that year | 54 participants from MIT and nearby universities, EEG-monitored | Those who used ChatGPT to write essays showed **less activity in brain networks associated with cognitive processing**, and couldn't quote from their own essays as easily as the non-AI group. Researchers cited "the pressing matter of exploring a possible decrease in learning skills." |
| **Carnegie Mellon + Microsoft** | 319 white-collar workers using AI weekly; 900 task examples | **Higher confidence in the tool's ability was associated with less critical thinking effort.** GenAI "can inhibit critical engagement with work and can potentially lead to long-term overreliance ... and diminished skill for independent problem-solving." |
| **Oxford University Press**, published in October | UK schoolchildren | **Six in ten** felt AI had negatively impacted their schoolwork skills. But **nine in ten** say it helped them develop at least one skill (problem-solving, creativity, revision), and about a quarter say it made the work too easy to do for them. |
| **Harvard Medical School**, published the prior year | Radiologists reading X-rays | AI assistance **improved some clinicians and damaged others**, for reasons researchers don't fully understand. Authors called for work on how to use AI so it "boost[s] human performance rather than hurt[s] it." |

Note the MIT prompts studied: summarising essay questions, tracking down sources, refining grammar
and style, and generating/articulating ideas — though participants felt AI was weak at that last
one.

## The Three Positions in the Piece

- **Prof. Wayne Holmes (UCL)** — the skeptic, and the strongest line in the article: "Today there
  is no independent evidence at scale for the effectiveness of these tools in education, or for
  their safety, or even for the idea they have a positive impact." He points to **cognitive
  atrophy**, wants far more research *before* students are encouraged to use these tools, and
  insists it "is not just the latest iteration of the calculator." His actual practice is not
  prohibition: "I never say to my students, you shouldn't use AI… But what I do try to say is
  look, we need to understand all these different things about it so that you can make informed
  decisions."
- **Dr. Alexandra Tomescu (OUP)** — the nuance position. Not necessarily decline; "quite a nuanced
  picture," and many pupils want more guidance on how to use AI.
- **Jayna Devani (OpenAI, international education)** — the vendor. "We definitely don't think
  students should be using ChatGPT to outsource work." Her frame is **tutor, not answer-provider**
  — study mode, back-and-forth decomposition of a question, the midnight-before-a-presentation
  case where you can't email your tutor. "The potential is truly there for ChatGPT to accelerate
  learning when it's used in a targeted way."

Context worth holding onto: OpenAI published 100 student prompts, and Oxford began giving students
and staff free ChatGPT in September. Devani helped secure that deal. The piece places her quote
next to that fact without comment.

## Reading It as Genre

This is the **news-feature** version of the week's question, and the contrast with the other three
readings is the point. It has no thesis of its own, sources every claim to an institution, quotes
a critic and a vendor at roughly equal length, and hedges its own headline into a question mark.
The balance is a genre convention, not a judgment — note that the "both sides" structure gives a
company with a commercial stake the same standing as the peer-reviewed studies, and gives the
article's last word to a researcher rather than to evidence. Compare: Mazzucato asserts and
prescribes, Slater et al. argue from a mechanism, the ethics page briefs without concluding, and
this one reports.

## Key Takeaways

- The measurable harm found isn't wrong answers — it's **reduced encoding**. The MIT participants
  couldn't quote essays they had just submitted.
- The Carnegie Mellon finding is the one that generalizes past school: **trust in the tool is
  inversely related to critical effort.** That's a mechanism, and it applies to code review and
  design docs as much as to essays.
- The Harvard radiology result blocks the simple story in both directions — same tool, opposite
  effects on different clinicians, cause unknown.
- OUP's numbers are genuinely two-sided (6/10 harmed, 9/10 helped). Anyone citing only one half is
  cherry-picking.
- Holmes's evidentiary claim — *no independent evidence at scale* — is the cheapest and most
  defensible position in the whole week's reading.

## Open Questions

- Is the calculator analogy actually wrong, or just premature? Calculators were also accused of
  atrophy and the answer depended on what got assessed afterward. Holmes rejects it by pointing to
  breadth of capability, not to a difference in the atrophy mechanism.
- Does "AI as tutor" survive contact with incentives? Study mode is slower than asking for the
  answer, and nothing makes a student pick the slow path at midnight.
- MIT's n=54, drawn from MIT and neighboring universities, is a small and very unrepresentative
  sample for a headline claim about brains. How much weight can it hold?
- If reduced encoding is the harm, the fix might be assessment design rather than tool policy —
  which is the bridge to Wednesday's policy session.
