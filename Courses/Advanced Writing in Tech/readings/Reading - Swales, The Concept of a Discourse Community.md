# Reading — Swales, "The Concept of a Discourse Community"

Reading for [[Week 03 - Discourse Communities]] · [[Advanced Writing in Tech]]

> [!cite] Citation
> Swales, John. "The Concept of a Discourse Community." *Genre Analysis: English in Academic
> and Research Settings*, Cambridge UP, 1990, pp. 21–32. Reprinted in *Writing about Writing*,
> pp. 466–480.

> [!abstract] One-line version
> "Discourse community" had become a warm, vague, circular term, so Swales replaces it with a
> deliberately narrow six-part test — goals, intercommunication, feedback, genres, lexis,
> expertise — that is meant to *exclude* most groups you'd loosely call communities, and then
> proves it works by applying it to a stamp-collecting club rather than to a university.

> [!tip] Why this one is the load-bearing reading
> The six criteria are the vocabulary for [[Assignment 1 - Discourse Community Archive]] — the
> terms you'll be bolding. Read it as a checklist you have to apply, not as an argument you
> have to agree with.

## The Problem He's Solving

The term arrived through the "social view" of writing (Faigley 1986) with a genealogy in
Perelman, Kuhn, Fish, Foucault, Rorty, Geertz, and Wittgenstein's language games. Swales quotes
Herzberg's summary of the received "cluster of ideas" — that language use in a group is a form
of social behavior, that discourse maintains and extends the group's knowledge, and that
discourse is epistemic.

Then he makes the move the whole chapter turns on: that cluster is **consequential, not
criterial**. It tells you what follows *once you already have* a discourse community; it gives
you no way to recognize one.

> "Irrespective of the merits of this 'cluster of ideas', the cluster is, I suggest,
> *consequential* of the assumption that there are indeed entities identifiable as discourse
> communities, not *criterial* for establishing or identifying them."

Even sympathizers conceded the mess. Herzberg admitted the idea is "not well defined as yet."
Porter (1988) asked whether communities should be determined "by shared objects of study, by
common research methodology, by opportunity and frequency of communication, or by genre and
stylistic conventions?" And Fennell et al. (1987) caught the circularity — definitions where
community involves discourse and discourse involves community "suffer the uncomfortable fate of
ending up circular."

His stated remedy is unfashionably blunt:

> "We need then to clarify, for procedural purposes, what is to be understood by discourse
> community and, perhaps in the present circumstances, it is better to offer a set of criteria
> sufficiently narrow that it will eliminate many of the marginal, blurred and controversial
> contenders."

Note the word *procedural*. He is not claiming to have found the essence of the thing; he's
building a tool that produces usable answers. A "strong" list also dodges the circularity
problem, because it follows that not all communities are discourse communities and not all
discourse activity builds one.

## Discourse Community ≠ Speech Community

Three reasons to keep them apart, and the second is the one that actually matters:

1. **Medium.** "Speech" won't do as the modifier for heavily literate groups. "Literacy takes
   away locality and parochiality" — members communicate with distant members and respond to
   writing from the past.
2. **Sociolinguistic vs. sociorhetorical.** In a speech community the communicative needs *of
   the group* — socialization, solidarity — drive its discourse. In a discourse community the
   needs *of the goals* do. The determinants are functional, not social: people "link up in
   order to pursue objectives that are prior to those of socialization and solidarity."
3. **Direction of pull.** "Speech communities are centripetal (they tend to absorb people into
   that general fabric), whereas discourse communities are centrifugal (they tend to separate
   people into occupational or speciality-interest groups)." You inherit a speech community "by
   birth, accident or adoption"; a discourse community "recruits its members by persuasion,
   training or relevant qualification."

His summary tag: "an archetypal discourse community tends to be a Specific Interest Group."

## The Six Criteria

Proposed as **necessary and sufficient**, which is a much stronger claim than a list of family
resemblances.

| # | Criterion | The teeth in it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A broadly agreed set of **common public goals** | *Public*, "because spies may join speech and discourse communities for hidden purposes of subversion." Commonality of goal is criterial, not shared object of study |
| 2 | **Mechanisms of intercommunication** among members | The stringent one — it is what kills the Café Owner Problem |
| 3 | Uses those mechanisms primarily to **provide information and feedback** | Membership implies *uptake*. Paying dues without reading anything doesn't count |
| 4 | Utilizes and hence possesses **one or more genres** | "Genres are how things get done, when language is used to accomplish them" (Martin 1985) |
| 5 | Has acquired some specific **lexis** | Usually abbreviations and acronyms; specialized senses of ordinary words |
| 6 | A threshold level of **members with relevant content and discoursal expertise** | Survival "depends on a reasonable ratio between novices and experts" |

**Criterion 1's sharp edge.** Shared goals are not the same as a shared subject. "The fact that
the shared object of study is, say, the Vatican, does not imply that students of the Vatican in
history departments, the Kremlin, dioceses, birth control agencies and liberation theology
seminaries form a discourse community." Adversarial members are fine — a parliament still shares
the objective of improved government — but if agreement falls far enough, "communication breaks
down and the discourse community splits."

**The Café Owner Problem** (Najjar) is the best stress test in the chapter. Café owners A, B and
C occupy the same professional role, serve the same clienteles, exchange the same kinds of
messages for the same purposes, and have similar genre skills — but they work long hours in
their own establishments, aren't in the Chamber of Commerce, and never interact. Criterion 2
says no. Swales distinguishes them from lighthouse keepers, missionaries, and "neglected consular
officials in their rotting outposts," who also never meet each other but do have "lines of
communication back to base" and acquired membership in training. Bizzell objected that café
owners share class- and ethnicity-based discursive practices; Swales's reply is a logical one —
that "does not resolve the logical problem of assigning membership of a community to individuals
who neither admit nor recognize that such a community exists."

**Criterion 5's test** is the one you can actually run on a group in an afternoon:

> "It is hard to imagine attending the convention of some group of which one is an outsider and
> understanding every word. If it were to happen — as might occur in the inaugural meeting of
> some quite new grouping — then that grouping would not yet constitute a discourse community."

## The Hong Kong Study Circle

He picks a philatelic hobby group *on purpose*, to avoid "creating a false impression that such
communities are only to be associated with intellectual paradigms or scholarly cliques." About
320 members worldwide, a third non-native English speakers, about a fifth women. Members include
a stamp dealer, a Dean at Yale, a retired Lieutenant-Colonel, and — as the greatest authority on
nineteenth-century Hong Kong mail — a retired British Rail signalman. They share "nothing in
common except their shared hobby interest."

The forum is a bi-monthly Journal/Newsletter, No. 265 at time of writing. Meetings exist but
"rarely more than a dozen members attend." His verdict: "without the Journal/Newsletter I doubt
the discourse community would survive."

The lexis demonstration is an auction catalogue line:

> "1176   1899 Combination PPC to Europe franked CIP 4 C canc large CANTON dollar chop, pair HK
> 2 C carmine added & Hong Kong index B cds. Arr cds. (1) (Photo) HK $1500."

Even decoding PPC as picture postcard and CIP as Chinese Imperial Post, "an outsider would still
not be in a position to estimate whether 1500 Hong Kong dollars would be an appropriate sum to
bid." Insider/outsider "is not absolute but consists of gradations."

**The best passage in the chapter is his own failure to get in.** He published an article using
frequency analysis from applied linguistics to solve a known HKSC puzzle:

> "The only comments that this effort to establish credibility elicited were 'too clever by
> half' and 'Mr Swales, we won't change our minds without a chemical analysis.'"

> "I have also had to learn over time the particular terms of approval and disapproval for a
> philatelic item (cf. Becher, 1981) such as 'significant', 'useful', 'normal', and not to
> comment directly on the monetary value of such items."

That is criterion 5 from the inside: he had the content expertise and still failed on lexis and
on the tacit rule about what one does not say.

## What He Concedes at the End

- **Discourse need not colonize your world-view.** Against the Whorfian reading, he insists
  conventions *can sometimes* be used "in a detached, instrumental way," and reaches for spies
  ("if they also *assimilate* they cease to be single spies but become double agents") and, more
  cheaply, a prospective son-in-law faking enthusiasm for bridge.
- **People belong to many, and vary in how many.** From "'Cooped up in the house with the
  children all day'" at one extreme to "skilled professional journalists with their
  chameleon-like ability to assume temporary membership of a wide range of discourse
  communities" at the other.
- **Personal involvement is not criterial.** The HKSC succeeds "despite a very low level of
  personal involvement."
- **Same department, different communities.** Herrington's study of two senior chemical
  engineering courses found the Lab course and the Design course were distinct communities —
  same department, same institution, same staff, largely the same students. Lab was
  *norm-developed* and served the "display familiarity" macro-act of college assignments; Design
  was *norm-developing* and served the persuasive reporting of the professional world. In Lab,
  "students and faculty were all too aware that the conceptual issue in the assignments was
  *not* an issue for the audience — the professor knew the answers."
- **What fails the test:** "shareholders of General Motors, members of the Book of the Month
  Club, voters for a particular political party, clienteles of restaurants and bars (except
  perhaps in soap-operas), employees of a university, and inhabitants of an apartment block."
- **Your class is not one yet.** "Except in exceptional cases of well-knit groups of advanced
  students already familiar with much of the material, an academic class is unlikely to be a
  discourse community at the outset. However, the hoped-for outcome is that it will form a
  discourse community."

And the honest last word, quoting Harris against himself:

> "The account I have provided of discourse community, for all its attempts to offer a set of
> pragmatic and operational criteria, remains in at least one sense somewhat removed from
> reality. It is utopian and 'oddly free of many of the tensions, discontinuities and conflicts
> in the sorts of talk and writing that go on everyday in the departments and classrooms of an
> actual university'."

## Key Takeaways

- The six criteria are **necessary and sufficient**, and the point of the list is exclusion. If
  you apply it and everything passes, you've applied it wrong.
- **Goals, not topics.** Criterion 1 is where most casual claims about a "community" die.
- **Criterion 2 is the stringent one.** No mechanism of intercommunication, no community — which
  is why the café owners fail and the scattered stamp collectors pass. Swales's community is held
  together by a newsletter, not by proximity.
- **Lexis is the fastest diagnostic.** Understanding every word means you're not looking at one.
- Swales's own HKSC humiliation shows the thing the criteria list can't: membership is granted by
  *the community*, on its terms, and content expertise won't buy it.
- He concedes his own model is utopian — it has no room for the conflict inside real groups. That
  concession is exactly the gap [[Reading - Schryer, The Lab vs the Clinic]] walks into.

## Open Questions

- Criterion 2 makes the café owners fail on a technicality of contact. But developers who have
  never met, sharing a Stack Overflow tag and a set of conventions, clearly *are* something.
  Does async, many-to-many, public-archive communication satisfy "mechanisms of
  intercommunication," or does it dissolve the criterion by making it trivially easy to meet?
  Swales wrote in 1990 and the test was expensive to pass then.
- If the criteria are necessary *and sufficient*, Herrington's Lab-vs-Design split should be
  impossible — the two courses share goals, mechanisms, genres, lexis and expertise ratios, yet
  Swales accepts them as distinct communities. Which criterion actually separates them?
- He wants participation without assimilation, and reaches for spies and double agents to prove
  it. Is the instrumental-use claim doing real work, or is it a convenience that lets him keep
  international students from being described as assimilated?
- For [[Assignment 1 - Discourse Community Archive]]: if my class isn't a discourse community
  yet, can I archive one I'm actually inside — and does being inside it make the lexis invisible
  to me exactly when I need to notice it?
