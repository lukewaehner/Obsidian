## The Argument in Five Moves

1. **The field's gap.** Technical and professional communication (TPC) has been calling for social-justice, culturally sensitive, community-honoring research, and for attention to localization by global users. Hip Hop has been doing exactly that for 40 years and is absent from the conversation.
2. **The definitional move.** If the value of a technical communicator is "their ability to make
   information more useable and accessible" (the Society for Technical Communication's
   definition), then "within a Hip Hop context, the DJ is almost entirely responsible for being
   the technical communicators of their culture."
3. **The DJ's position.** DJs sit in the "in-between space" of consumer and producer. They consume other people's records constantly, and they produce a text of their own: the *selection*, the *order*, and the *style*. Crowds consume the curation as well as the songs.
4. **The case: DJ Screw, Houston, 1990–2001.** Screw slowed records down and repeated phrases so that you "can hear everything and feel everything." That is localization for a specific listening practice: long, hot, slow drives across Houston's highways with a cassette deck.
5. **The documents.** Handwritten request playlists in the archive look like bad technical writing, but they work. They leave information out because the community already shares it, and that shared context is what makes them effective.

## Hip Hop's Origin as a Communication Story

- The **four elements** (DJing, breaking, graffiti, emceeing) can each exist outside Hip Hop.
  What makes them Hip Hop is "a relationship to community." Without that tie, an element "on its own does not represent Hip Hop."
- Early 1970s South Bronx: Hip Hop grew as "a response to the lack of community resources" in education, housing, representation, and employment. DJs were credited as the founders "because they had the ability to create and hold space."
- **Kool Herc's looping** is the founding example of user-driven design. He noticed that break
  dancers waited for the instrumental break, so he bought records for a fifteen-second solo and used two turntables and a crossfader to extend the break "for as long as he wanted." Del Hierro calls this "one of the first places we see Hip Hop practitioners convert texts for better
  accessibility and usability for local users."
- Tricia Rose's frame: sampling and turntablism are "at once destructive... and recuperative." They take recordings apart and recontextualize them to create "new meanings for cultural sounds that have been relegated to commercial waste bins."

## The DJ as Technical Communicator

Two scholars carry the theory:

- **Natasha N. Jones (2016):** technical communicators "must be aware of the ways that texts and technologies that they create and critique reinforce certain ideologies." The technical communicator mediates between the information that needs to be communicated and what actually gets communicated and consumed.
- **Adam J. Banks (2010), *Digital Griots*:** DJs are not "ventriloquist[s], playing other people's stories for us; rather [DJs] are arranging, layering, sampling, and remixing… keeping the culture, telling stories and binding time as they move the crowd."

Del Hierro's synthesis is **call and response** as a user-feedback loop. The DJ reads the crowd
live and decides when to *preserve* and when to *expand*. For example, a DJ might play a local
artist, then follow with an out-of-town artist in a similar style. The relationship is "perpetual
and reciprocal." He offers it as the model TPC wants: balancing user needs while acting as "an
agent for social change through an added attention to curation."

## Method: Hiphopography

- **Hiphopography** (Spady 1991; Alim 2006) integrates "ethnography, biography, and social,
  cultural, and oral history to arrive at an emic view." It insists that the cultural creators
  take part in telling the culture's story.
- Screw died in 2000 (the paper says both 2000 and 2001), so Del Hierro adds **archival
  research**. He spent three visits and 25 hours in the University of Houston's Houston Hip Hop
  Archive, kept in contact with curator Julie Grob, bought two Screw Tape CDs, listened to 10 more online, and read Hip Hop journalism.
- The tapes count as data because Screw "continues to speak through his performances," both verbally and through his turntablism.

## DJ Screw and the Screwed-and-Chopped Style

Julie Grob's technical description: Screw slowed records with the turntables' pitch controls
("screw") and played two copies of one record a beat apart, switching between them to repeat
sounds ("chop").

- More than **300 officially released tapes**, 1990–2001. The style became synonymous with
  Houston and pulled Southern rap toward slower tempos and more melodic rapping.
- Distribution started **from his front door**, then moved to a storefront. His home became the
  hub where people bought tapes, made requests, and recorded verses.
- The design worked so well that record labels began **sending him their artists' music**
  despite his use of copyrighted material. Labels still release chopped-and-screwed versions for
  Southern markets.
- Big Demo (Screwed Up Click): **"every Screw tape is somebody's personal tape."** Every tape came
  out of a relationship, either a friendship or a request.

### The Workflow (Figures 1–2)

A cycle with **no starting point**:

```
listener (headphones) → writes a request playlist → DJ Screw (turntables)
        ↑                                                  ↓
        └──────────── cassette tape ◄──────────────────────┘
          + wider Hip Hop (boombox, Fig. 2) feeding new material in
```

Information changes form as it moves: sometimes it is a static artifact (the list, the tape),
sometimes a practice (listening, writing, DJing). Del Hierro calls it "public deliberation in
communication design," with built-in ways for the community to take part in making knowledge.
Figure 2 adds the point that listeners *had* Hip Hop outside of Screw and kept coming back to the
screwed version anyway.

## The Playlists: "Incomplete" on Purpose

This is the core of the paper, and the part most useful for the archive assignment.

- The original Screw Tapes were grey **Maxwell cassettes with almost no packaging text**. Figure 4
  shows a tracklist card left blank. Listeners knew only "DJ Screw" and the style, and learned
  everything else by listening.
- Three list types compared:
  - **Table 1:** the CD back cover of *3 In Da Mornin'*, written *after* release for retail. It
    has its own [sic] errors: "Parliment," "Ally."
  - **Figure 5:** "Screw tape list for Steve," torn from a notebook, 20 songs in black ink with a
    blue-ink correction to track 18.
  - **Figure 6:** "Screw tape list for Keke," red ink on the back of a folded flyer, numbered 1–20
    with only 13 songs filled in.
- "In a neutral setting—devoid of context—these lists might be considered incomplete or bad
  examples." Across all of them, though, **incomplete information is the common trait**, "implying
  that this type of omission is a purposeful act."
- **The Juvy example.** Steve's list, the most "complete" of the three, reads "Back tha Ass Up
  (Juvy)." The official credit is Juvenile, "Back that Azz Up featuring Mannie Fresh & Lil Wayne."
  It works because the writer and Screw had both *already listened* to the track. Listening comes
  before writing, and the list only has to be unambiguous to people who share that listening.
- **Hip Hop Nation Language (HHNL)** (Alim 2004) is the shared code, "suitable and functional for
  all communicative needs." It conveys essential information through efficient writing.
- **Figure 7: playlist ≈ or ≠ tape.** A list and a tape could hold identical songs and still be
  different documents, because each medium has its own "limitations or affordances." Written
  lists are "placeholders or bookmarks," and "like an outline, these written lists are not meant
  to be authoritative." Alphabetic writing is "one part of the process in Hip Hop but hardly ever
  the end product."

> "The various versions of track-lists/playlists all show how one can have their own style while
> participating in a larger discourse."

## Implications (His Three Bullets)

1. **Hip Hop is an important site** for TPC research and practice: 50 years of community-based
   communication spreading globally through local practitioners.
2. **The Hip Hop DJ is a community-engaged technical communicator**, answering Potts's (2009) call
   to study "people-powered spaces where participants are making do with the tools available."
3. **Community should play a role in designing communication.** Center community to get
   localization and accessibility, and stay aware (Jones) of the ideologies your documents pass on.

Current examples he points to: Bun B's "Trill Moji" emoji keyboard and the mixtape repository
Datpiff.com.

## Key Takeaways

- **Redefinition by function.** Del Hierro doesn't argue that DJs *resemble* technical
  communicators. He takes the field's own definition (making information usable and accessible)
  and shows that DJs meet it. That is why the claim is hard to dismiss.
- **Localization is design for a use context.** Screw slowed tracks for how Houston listened: in
  cars, in traffic, in heat. "Hear everything" is legibility, and "feel everything" is
  engagement.
- **Curation is authorship.** Choosing what gets preserved and what gets expanded is the
  technical communicator's ideological power, which is Jones's warning restated as DJ practice.
- **Judge a genre inside its community.** The playlists fail by a neutral standard and succeed by
  the community's. This is Swales's criterion 5 (lexis) and criterion 4 (genres) at work: to an
  outsider, "Juvy" is an error, and to an insider it is enough.
- **Writing is one mode in a multimodal workflow.** The list is an outline of the tape, not the
  thing itself.

## Connections

- **[[Reading - Swales, The Concept of a Discourse Community]].** Screw's scene passes Swales's
  test: public goals, intercommunication through the tapes and the front door, feedback through
  requests, genres (playlists, tapes), lexis (HHNL), and a ratio of experts to novices. Swales
  says discourse communities recruit by persuasion and skill, not birth. Del Hierro's Hip Hop is
  defined by "relationship to community," which may be closer to Swales's *speech* community.
- **[[Reading - Schryer, The Lab vs the Clinic]].** Genre as "stabilized-for-now or
  stabilized-enough." The three list styles are one genre that tolerates individual variation and
  is still stable enough for Screw to act on. Schryer's medical record (POVMR) is the
  institutional opposite: a record genre that enforces one format.
- **[[Reading - Turkle and Yee, Evocative Objects]].** The worn notebook page and the flyer's back
  are evocative objects. The archive stores the relationship along with the song titles.
- **[[Reading - Shipka, Rethinking Composition]].** Multimodality: the playlist ≈/≠ tape figure is
  Shipka's argument that the finished artifact isn't the whole composition.

## For Monday's Class

The Del Hierro and DJing slides (`week-4/Del Hierro and DJing.pdf`) run in this order:

1. **Freewrite:** your most memorable experience at a live event, and what made it memorable.
   Del Hierro's answer would be the DJ holding the space and responding to the crowd. Have one
   concrete example ready.
2. **Discussion:** "What do you think of Del Hierro's ideas here?" and "How does technology shape
   technical communication in your field?"
3. **Activity, "record player telephone":** Group 1 makes sounds on a turntable and writes a
   description. Each later group goes into the hallway, tries to recreate the previous group's
   sound, and writes a new description. At the end, the class compares descriptions and
   recordings. It is a live demonstration of the playlist ≠ tape point: information shifts each
   time it passes through a medium and a person.

A software answer for the technology prompt: READMEs, commit messages, and API docs are
localized for their readers too. A terse commit log works the way the Juvy list does, because
it assumes readers who already share the context.

## Open Questions

- Del Hierro treats omission as purposeful because it *works*. But working for insiders is also
  how a genre excludes outsiders. Is the "incomplete" playlist accessible, or accessible only to
  people already in the community? He uses "accessibility" for both, and they pull in opposite
  directions.
- The argument maps a subculture onto a professional field to legitimize it. Does calling DJs
  "technical communicators" honor the practice, or absorb it into TPC's terms? He wants TPC
  "allies" to promote Hip Hop, but the benefit seems to flow mostly to the field.
- The Screw Tapes built a community around copyrighted material until labels started sending him
  music. At what point does localization become distribution, and does the community-first framing
  survive commercial success?
- For [[Assignment 1 - Discourse Community Archive]]: which of my 7 objects are insider documents
  that look "incomplete" from outside? The annotation is where I translate them for an outsider
  reader without flattening what makes them work.
