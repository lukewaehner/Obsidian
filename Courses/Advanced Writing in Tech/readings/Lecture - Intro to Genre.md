### Terms for genre

| Term | Definition (handout wording) |
| --- | --- |
| **Genre** | Typified social action |
| **Exigence** | The social motivation for writing |
| **Addressivity** | The way language is directed |
| **Utterance** | Language with direction — the building block of genres |
| **Chronotope** and **kairos** | The social and cultural moment that forms writing, and the social and cultural moment that makes writing most effective |
| **Uptake** | The ways we respond to a piece of writing |
| **Discourse community** | Groups that share genres, language, etc. |
| **Antecedent genre knowledge** | Genre knowledge that gets used to help compose in new and unknown genres |
| **Metagenre** | The larger structures of knowing and understanding genres, *or* a genre that explains how to write in another genre |

### Freadman terms

Freadman's "Anyone for Tennis?" uses a game as its metaphor for genre. The reading itself comes
later in the term.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Ball** | Language in general, with no addressivity |
| **Shot** | An utterance, meaning language with direction. "We don't exchange balls but shots." Knowing which shots work under the rules is your knowledge of uptake |
| **Rules** | Genre conventions, which make the game possible and decide whether a shot "counts" |
| **Ceremonials** | The chronotope and kairotic moment of the game. Pick-up tennis and Wimbledon are the same game with very different stakes, and the same genre can carry different meanings depending on where and when it's written |

## The Lecture, in Order

### From classification to social action

For most of its history, genre was treated like pre-genetics taxonomy: things were grouped by
their external features. The standard counterexample is **historical letter collections vs.
epistolary novels**, which have nearly identical features but work differently and are read
differently.

**Carolyn Miller, "Genre as Social Action" (1984)** broke this: "a rhetorically sound definition
of genre must be centered not on the substance or the form of discourse but on the action it is
used to accomplish… genre can be said to represent typified rhetorical action" (151). Genres are
typified responses to recurrent rhetorical situations. Bakhtin and Kathleen Jamieson reached
similar conclusions. The field that grew from this is **Rhetorical Genre Studies (RGS)**. Its
sister fields, Systemic Functional Linguistics (the "Sydney School") and English for Specific
Purposes, teach genre explicitly so that students can access genres of power.

### Bakhtin: utterance and addressivity

- An **utterance** is language directed somewhere, and it is the building block of genre.
- **Addressivity** is who the language is written for or to. "Each speech genre in each area of
  speech communication has its own typical conception of the addressee, and this defines it as a
  genre" (95).
- Addressivity makes writing **dialogic**: it is part of a larger conversation, and that is what
  makes uptake possible.

### Exigence, chronotope, kairos

- **Exigence**, per Miller: "neither as a cause of rhetorical action nor as intention, but as
  social motive" (158). Per Bitzer: "an imperfection marked by urgency… a thing which is other
  than it should be."
- A **rhetorical situation**, per Miller citing Bitzer, is "a 'complex of persons, events, objects
  and relations' presenting an 'exigence' that can be allayed through the mediation of discourse."
- The **chronotope** (Bakhtin, later developed by Schryer) is the space and time in which action
  is available: the culture, time, and place that allow certain genres to exist. Her example is
  that you write essays because you're in the chronotopic space of homework, not for fun.
- **Kairos** is the right moment, when an utterance is most effective.
- Her worked example is the SOP. A history of people botching a complex procedure creates the
  exigence, and the exigence produces the conventions (numbered steps, very clear language).
  **Conventions exist because they fill the gap, not because they're rules.**

### Uptake (Freadman, Dryer)

- The idea comes from J. L. Austin's speech-act theory, where some speech *is* action (marriage
  vows).
- **Uptake** is the relationship between a text and the response to it. The response helps define
  the genre, and the responses available shape how you write. Her example is the assignment
  prompt: you answer it with an essay, not a methods section, because a methods section isn't an
  effective uptake of a discourse-analysis prompt.
- Per Freadman, no genre guarantees its uptake, there is no ideal uptake, and uptake
  **stabilizes and destabilizes** at once (560). Compare Schryer's "stabilized-for-now."
- **Dryer, "Disambiguating Uptake"**, adds a vocabulary:
  - **uptake residues**: the memories carried inside an uptake
  - **affordances**: the conventions that shape an uptake
  - **artifacts**: "the genres that result from uptake"
  - **captures**: the aftereffects of repeated encounters with a genre
  - **enactments**: the production of a textual uptake
  - **disruptakes**: deliberately mishandling an uptake as a form of resistance

### Genre ecologies

"No genre is an island." Genres connect in **ecologies**. She prefers that word to "sets" or
"assemblages" because genres are "a little wild." Engineering's genre ecology overlaps with
public science's (Bill Nye) at its edges.

### Discourse communities: Swales, compressed

She admits the Swales article "is pretty dry." Her six marks:

1. Common public goals
2. Methods of communicating among members
3. Participatory communication methods
4. Genres that define the group
5. A lexis
6. A standard of knowledge needed for membership

Two points she adds that aren't obvious from the reading alone. First, Swales ties discourse
communities to **power**: how well you fit the community's knowledge decides whether it lets you
belong. Second, the goals can be mundane, and her example is literally *playing a game of
Dungeons and Dragons together*. That is direct licence for a gaming community in Assignment 1.

### Activity theory (CHAT)

- **Cultural Historical Activity Theory** is an RGS uptake of Vygotsky and Leont'ev, who were
  education theorists, not rhetoricians.
- **Vygotsky's triangle**: subject, object, and **mediating artifact**, producing an outcome.
  **Leont'ev** added **rules**, **community**, and **division of labor**.
- A subject can't reach the outcome without a mediating artifact, and an artifact means nothing
  without a subject using it. If a community has no word for something, it has to invent one or
  the idea goes unexpressed.
- Her Neopets/MySpace example: those interfaces allowed HTML and CSS, so people composed in HTML
  (she learned it that way). Facebook doesn't allow it, so nobody does. **Interfaces push you
  toward certain kinds of writing.**
- She notes that most people misread Vygotsky, whose real interest was children's inner and outer
  speech, but "these mis-interpretations have a life of their own now."

### Transfer and antecedent genre knowledge

- **Antecedent genre knowledge**: the genres you already know shape how you approach a new one.
  Jamieson: "discourses bear the imprint of ancestral genres."
- Knowing about a genre ≠ being able to write in it (Artemeva and Fox). Students comfortable in
  their antecedent genres can become **"boundary guarders"** who apply the old genre rather than
  learn the new one.
- Transfer is real but slow, taking 2–5 years. It is "like building a muscle group." It comes
  from treating genres as flexible rather than right-or-wrong.

### Metagenre

This has two meanings. One is a genre that tells you how to write another genre, such as her
assignment prompts. The other is a large-scale structure of thinking, such as empirical
thinking.

### Closing caveat

The classification view of genre isn't *wrong*. Use it when you need a taxonomy, and use RGS when
you need to think about how genres are fuzzy, unstable, social, and interconnected.

## Key Takeaways

- **Genre = typified social action.** Define a genre by what it does and for whom, not by how it
  looks.
- Conventions exist to fill an exigence, which is why the same features can mean different things
  in different ceremonials.
- **Uptake** is the concept that links genres together: every text responds to one and invites
  others.
- CHAT's mediating artifacts and Dryer's "artifacts" give an object-based archive a genre-theory
  vocabulary, not only the Turkle and Yee one.

## Open Questions

- Is a commit message or a PR description a genre defined by its uptake (the reviewer's approval)
  more than by its form?
- Where's the line between an "artifact" in Dryer's sense, a genre produced by uptake, and an
  artifact in the archive-assignment sense, a physical object?
