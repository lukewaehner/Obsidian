# Reading — Mazzucato, "The Ugly Truth Behind ChatGPT: AI Is Guzzling Resources at Planet-Eating Rates"

Reading for [[Week 02 - AI Ethics and Genre]] · [[Advanced Writing in Tech]]

> [!cite] Citation
> Mazzucato, Mariana. "The Ugly Truth Behind ChatGPT: AI Is Guzzling Resources at Planet-Eating
> Rates." *The Guardian*, 30 May 2024,
> www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/article/2024/may/30/ugly-truth-ai-chatgpt-guzzling-resources-environment.
> Filed under Opinion › Climate crisis. Mazzucato is professor in the economics of innovation and
> public value at UCL and founding director of the UCL Institute for Innovation & Public Purpose.

## Thesis

The tech industry's environmental footprint is invisible by design — we picture apps and
browsers, not the datacentres — and that invisibility has let the industry avoid accountability
for it. Mazzucato's real argument is not "AI uses a lot of power." It is a **policy** argument:
because solving one climate goal (off fossil fuels) can wreck another (water security, grid
capacity, human rights in mineral supply chains), governments must stop "picking winners" and
start **picking the willing** — making public support conditional on disclosure and direction of
travel.

## The Argument in Sequence

1. **The materiality move** (¶1). You think of the tech industry as things that don't exist in
   physical space. The "cloud" is a building that draws power. Its infrastructure accounts for
   more global greenhouse emissions than commercial flights.
2. **Scale, made concrete.** The 5bn YouTube plays of *Despacito* in 2018 used the energy it
   would take to heat 40,000 US homes for a year. Training GPT-3 at Microsoft's facilities could
   have consumed ~700,000 litres of water for cooling.
3. **The silence is strategic.** Companies producing these models "have stayed remarkably quiet
   about the amount of energy they consume — probably because they don't want to spark our
   concern." This sits alongside the industry's other obscured uglier sides: tax avoidance,
   privacy invasion, attention exploitation.
4. **Expansion makes it worse.** Google's global datacentre footprint and Meta's AI Research
   SuperCluster raise consumption. Chasing cheap electricity pushes datacentres toward regions
   like the southern US — exacerbating water stress in already dry places.
5. **The supply chain.** Lithium and cobalt aren't just automotive; datacentre batteries need
   them. Extraction is water-intensive, polluting, and "often linked to human rights violations
   and poor labour standards."
6. **Competition for the grid.** Energy routed to tech competes with residential supply. The UK's
   outdated electricity network is already holding back housing — plans for 7,000 homes in
   Bicester were paused for lack of network capacity. This worsens as households electrify.
7. **The policy prescription** (the actual payload). Evaluate the organisations government funds
   and partners with on whether their actions produce "concrete successes for people and the
   planet." Don't pick sectors or technologies as winners — **pick the willing**, with support
   conditional on moving the right way. Make environmental disclosure a condition of public
   support; extend the same conditionality to mineral supply chains and human rights compliance.
8. **Systemic, not piecemeal** (¶closing). Flexible strategies integrating knowledge from local
   to global, not outdated top-down methods. With 1.5C expected to be exceeded, "it's time we
   approach today's grand challenges systemically, so that the solution to one problem does not
   exacerbate another."

## Reading It as Genre

A newspaper op-ed by an academic economist, and the form shows: no citations, one concrete number
per claim, a physical image to anchor the abstraction (a lithium mine in the Atacama, in the
photo), and roughly 800 words. Compare to what the same argument would look like as an IIPP
working paper. **Useful for Thursday:** the *Despacito* comparison and the Bicester housing
example are doing rhetorical work that a footnote cannot — they translate an unimaginable
aggregate into a household unit. Also note the frame: it is filed as *Opinion › Climate crisis*,
not as tech reporting, which tells the reader which conversation to slot it into before they read
a word.

## Where It's Vulnerable

- The two headline figures (cloud > aviation emissions; 700,000 L for GPT-3) are asserted without
  sourcing in the op-ed itself — the genre permits it, but both are contested and the water figure
  is a modeled estimate, not a measurement. Worth chasing to the source before reusing.
- "Research suggests," "could have been used," "potentially exacerbating" — the hedging is heavier
  than the headline's "planet-eating" implies. The head and the body are not making the same
  strength of claim, which is itself a genre artifact (headlines are written by editors).

## Key Takeaways

- The environmental cost of AI is a *siting and supply-chain* problem, not only an energy-total
  problem. Where the datacentre goes determines whose water it drinks.
- Climate goals conflict with each other. Decarbonising via electrification collides with water
  security and with mineral extraction ethics.
- Her lever is procurement and conditionality, not consumer restraint. That is a different
  political theory of change than "use AI less."
- Opacity is the enabling condition — you cannot regulate consumption nobody discloses.

## Open Questions

- Where does this land on individual use? She aims entirely at state and corporate levels, which
  leaves my own per-prompt footprint an open question — possibly deliberately.
- How does this cut against the Scientific American piece? Slater et al. want blame on users and
  programmers; Mazzucato wants it on funders and policymakers. Are those complementary or
  competing accounts of responsibility?
- If disclosure became mandatory, what unit would it even be in — per query, per training run,
  per datacentre-year? The measurement problem may be the actual bottleneck.
