## Audience and Stance — read this first

This is **written for instructors, not students**, and it opens by refusing to do the thing you'd
expect an ethics page to do. It explicitly declines to draw boundaries around "ethical" and
"unethical" uses, and declines to encourage or discourage any practice. Its stated goal is "simply
to recap a few of the major issues raised by AI, to help instructors make informed and nuanced
decisions." That refusal is the rhetorical move of the whole document — it is a **briefing**, not
an argument, and reading it as though it had a thesis will misread it.

## The Four Issues

### 1. Copyright and Intellectual Property

- Generative tools synthesize publicly available online content, so "any actual substance in
  AI-generated writing is really a pastiche of ideas expressed by human writers elsewhere on the
  internet." The page's sharpest phrase: this arguably makes the output **mosaic plagiarism**.
- The concealment is deliberate and is what makes this easy to forget in practice — "the
  algorithms that power these tools deliberately conceal the sources they draw from in order to
  make the writing they produce read like original content."
- Legally unresolved; cases are only now entering the courts.
- The pedagogical worry is not really the law: it's that AI discourse "can complicate or even
  undermine the ways that students understand their obligations to engage and document their
  sources."

### 2. Availability and Equity — the argument that cuts both ways

The optimistic case: AI *editing* tools (proofread and correct, don't generate) could level the
field for English language learners, students from underprivileged educational backgrounds
(statistically more likely to be BIPOC and/or low-income), and students with dyslexia or ADHD —
who otherwise face a steepened difficulty curve, mastering college-level written English and
college-level content simultaneously.

The counter, which the page thinks is the stronger one:

- **AI tools are rarely free.** Subscription models mean the good tools go to students who can
  pay. So availability may *widen* the gap rather than close it.
- Unresolved: if students use AI editors, "to what extent is their writing their own," and will
  they ever acquire higher-order writing skills?
- The instructor-level question it poses is genuinely uncomfortable: **how much of your grade
  rests on surface-level grammar, diction, and tone — i.e., on exactly the layer a paid tool can
  buy?** The page refuses the easy out, noting it's "neither desirable nor realistic" to declare
  that surface qualities don't matter.
- Institutional version: should the college fund AI access for low-income students?

### 3. Privacy and Data Mining

- All major tools require accounts, so personal information goes to the vendor and to whoever
  they share data with. Account friction varies — email only vs. verified phone and credit card,
  even for free tiers. This matters if an assignment *requires* AI use.
- Many vendors stipulate that both user inputs and generated outputs are theirs to use — for
  training, and potentially for sale to third parties.
- **The instructor-facing twist is the interesting part:** an instructor who feeds their own
  writing prompt into a tool to demo it is "handing their own intellectual property — i.e. the
  writing prompt — over to the AI's owners, while simultaneously teaching the AI to generate
  better and more 'authentic' responses to that assignment." The page notes some of its own
  suggested assignments do this.

### 4. Bias

- Remixing internet content replicates implicit and explicit bias in it.
- The asymmetry with human writers is the key claim: humans "can make their writing and research
  methods more transparent than an AI can," which makes bias findable and arguable. With AI,
  "both the writing and the 'research' ... happens inside the machine," so users see results with
  no insight into what was used or overlooked.
- **The objectivity trap:** because an AI is technically incapable of having an ideology, readers
  assume its text is more likely to be unbiased — and therefore scrutinize it *less* than they
  would a human author. Ideological incapacity gets misread as neutrality.
- **Detection cuts the same way:** a Stanford study found AI detectors like GPTZero have an
  alarmingly high false positive rate on writing by non-native English speakers. A "likely AI"
  report is not proof. Note that this lands on the same students the equity section said the
  tools were supposed to help.

## Quotes Worth Keeping

> "This arguably makes anything these tools produce a work of mosaic plagiarism."

> "The algorithms that power these tools deliberately conceal the sources they draw from in order
> to make the writing they produce read like original content."

> "The fact that an AI is technically incapable of having an ideology of its own can often lead
> readers to assume that AI-written text is more likely to be objective or unbiased, which can
> make them less likely to scrutinize facts and ideas espoused by the AI."

## Key Takeaways

- Four axes to hang any AI policy on: **IP, equity, privacy, bias.** Useful as a checklist for
  Wednesday's policy-writing session.
- Equity is the double-edged one. The same tool is an accessibility win and a pay-to-win
  advantage, and which it is depends entirely on price and on what the assignment rewards.
- The cruelest loop in the reading: non-native English writers are the group AI editing tools are
  supposed to help, *and* the group AI detectors falsely accuse.
- Concealment of sources is described as a design goal, not a limitation — which is a stronger
  claim than "the model can't cite."
- Instructors are data subjects too. Prompts are IP, and demoing an assignment trains the tool to
  answer it better.

## Open Questions

- "Mosaic plagiarism" is doing a lot of work here and the page hedges it with "arguably." Does the
  analogy hold, given that the model stores weights rather than text? What would decide it?
- The page's studied neutrality is itself a position. Who does a refusal to draw lines serve when
  students are being graded against lines that exist anyway?
- For our own class AI policy (Wed): which of these four does a *course-level* policy actually
  have leverage over? Privacy and IP are set by the vendor; bias is set by the training data. That
  leaves equity and disclosure.
