## Anchoring Bias

The tendency to put excessive weight on an initial assessment and fail to adjust estimates sufficiently when new information arrives.

---

## How It Works

Once an "anchor" number is established in someone's mind, subsequent estimates tend to stay close to that anchor even when the anchor is arbitrary or outdated.

---

## Example

An investor buys a stock at $100 per share. The company then reports declining revenues and cuts guidance. The stock falls to $70. Rather than re-evaluating the company's fair value from scratch, the investor remains anchored to their $100 purchase price and thinks the stock is "cheap" because it is 30% below what they paid. They buy more shares. However, if they had just encountered this company today, fundamental analysis might show fair value is only $50. The purchase price anchor distorts their valuation.

---

## Another Example

An analyst sets a price target of $150 for a stock. When the company acquires a competitor that significantly changes its business model, the analyst revises the target to $160, a mere 7% adjustment. A fresh analysis without the prior anchor might have produced a target of $200 or $100, depending on the acquisition's true impact.
