## Behavioral Finance

The field challenging [[Archive/Finance/Investments/Efficient Market Hypothesis/Efficient Market Hypothesis|EMH]] by examining how psychological biases affect investor decision-making and market prices.

---

## Core Premise

Conventional finance assumes investors are rational, but behavioral finance recognizes that:

- Investors do not always process information correctly
- People make inconsistent or systematically suboptimal decisions
- [[Arbitrage]] has limits: arbitrageurs have limited resources and cannot fully override irrational behavior to bring prices to intrinsic values

This explains why successful trading strategies identifying undervalued stocks may persist, not because the market is efficient, but because behavioral inefficiencies are difficult to arbitrage away.

---

## Information Processing Biases

How investors misinterpret or selectively use information:

- [[Overconfidence Bias]] - Overestimating abilities and forecast precision
- [[Confirmation Bias]] - Seeking information that confirms existing beliefs
- [[Representativeness Bias]] - Inferring patterns from small samples
- [[Gamblers Fallacy]] - Expecting short-term reversions to the mean
- [[Recency Bias]] - Overweighting recent events
- [[Anchoring Bias]] - Insufficient adjustment from initial estimates
- [[Attention and Heuristics]] - Relying on mental shortcuts due to limited cognitive resources

---

## Behavioral Preferences

How psychological factors shape risk attitudes and decisions:

- [[Prospect Theory]] - Asymmetric valuation of gains versus losses
- [[Loss Aversion]] - Losses hurt more than equivalent gains feel good
- [[Framing Effect]] - How presentation affects risk preferences
- [[House Money Effect]] - Reduced risk aversion after prior gains
