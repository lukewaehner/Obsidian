

**Derivatives** are financial assets derived from existing traded assets, rather than securities originally issued by businesses or governments to raise capital. They are contracts whose value depends on the performance of an underlying asset.

## What Are Derivatives?

### Primary Assets
Securities originally sold by a business or government to raise money:
- **Equities** (stocks)
- **Fixed-income securities** (bonds)

### Derivative Assets
Financial assets derived from existing traded assets:
- Not issued to raise capital
- Value depends on underlying asset
- Used for hedging, speculation, and leverage

## Types of Derivatives

### [[Futures Contracts]]
Agreements made today regarding trades that will take place later:
- Fixed expiration and delivery date
- Obligation to buy/sell at contract price
- Examples: S&P 500 futures, crude oil futures, currency futures

### [[Options|Options]]
Contracts giving the **right, but not the obligation**, to buy or sell:
- [[Call Options|Call Options]] - Right to buy
- [[Put Options|Put Options]] - Right to sell
- Buyer pays premium for this right
- More flexible than futures

## Key Difference: Options vs Futures

| Feature | Futures | Options |
|---------|---------|---------|
| **Obligation** | Must execute | Can choose not to execute |
| **Upfront cost** | No payment today | Pay premium today |
| **Risk** | Unlimited both ways | Buyer: Limited loss, seller: varies |
| **Flexibility** | Locked in | Right without obligation |

## Why Use Derivatives?

### Hedging
Protect against price movements:
```
Example: Airline buys oil futures to lock in fuel costs
- If oil prices rise → futures contract saves money
- If oil prices fall → loses on futures, but benefits from cheaper fuel
- Net effect: Predictable costs
```

### Speculation
Bet on price movements:
```
Example: Trader thinks NVDA will rise
- Buy call options instead of stock
- Control more shares with same capital
- Higher potential returns (and losses)
```

### Leverage
Control large positions with less capital:
- Options allow control of 100 shares per contract
- Futures control large commodity quantities
- Amplifies both gains and losses

## Real-World Example: Oil Futures Crisis (2020)

In April 2020, oil futures prices went **negative** (-$37.63/barrel):
- COVID-19 decimated demand
- Storage facilities full
- Futures holders faced physical delivery obligations
- Paid others to take oil off their hands

This illustrates the **obligation** in futures contracts - you must execute, even if unfavorable.

## Market Structure

### Exchange-Traded
- Standardized contracts
- Clearinghouse guarantees
- Examples: CBOE (Chicago Board Options Exchange), CME

### Over-the-Counter (OTC)
- Customized contracts
- Direct between parties
- Higher counterparty risk

## Important Concepts

### Contract Specifications
- **Underlying asset**: What the derivative is based on
- **Contract size**: Standard quantity (e.g., 100 shares for stock options)
- **Expiration date**: When the contract ends
- **Strike price** (options): Price at which you can buy/sell
- **Premium** (options): Price paid for the option contract

### Exercise and Settlement
- **Exercise**: Using your option right
- **Physical delivery**: Receive/deliver actual asset
- **Cash settlement**: Settle difference in cash

## Key Takeaways

1. **Derivatives are contracts**, not ownership stakes
2. **Futures obligate**, options provide flexibility
3. **Used for hedging and speculation**
4. **Leverage amplifies outcomes** - both gains and losses
5. **Understanding obligations is critical** - buyer vs seller dynamics
