## Core Idea

- Corporate strategy decides *where* a firm competes (its **scope**); business strategy decides *how*. Scope has three dimensions — vertical, product, geographic — all analyzed with one toolkit: economies of scope, transaction costs, and costs of corporate complexity.
- A firm's boundary is set by **relative cost**. Internalize an activity when the **transaction costs** of buying it in the market exceed the **administrative costs** of running it inside; otherwise buy. Both sides have costs — the test is always *relative*.
- The force *toward* integration is **transaction-specific investment + uncertainty** (which makes complete contracts impossible). The forces *against* it are scale mismatch, capability development, strategic dissimilarity, weak internal incentives, inflexibility, and compounded risk.
- Make-or-buy is **not binary** — between spot markets and full ownership sits a continuum of hybrids (long-term contracts, franchising, vendor partnerships, alliances, JVs).
- The answer is always "it depends," and it depends on *firm-specific* resources and strategy, not just industry conditions — two firms in the same industry can rationally integrate to opposite degrees.
- **This topic spans two exercises:** Exercise 1 (Grant) is about the **administrative costs** of integrating; Exercise 2 (Stuckey & White) is about **vertical market failure** and the other rationales. The exam asks you to apply these core ideas to the assigned company cases.

# Exercise 1 — Administrative Costs of Vertical Integration (Grant, Ch. 13)

## Key Terms & Definitions

### Transaction Costs (p. 342)
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Market mechanism** | Coordination by independent decisions guided by prices — Adam Smith's *invisible hand*; needs no conscious planning. |
| **Administrative mechanism** | Coordination by managerial decision through a hierarchy — Chandler's *visible hand*; depends on active planning. |
| **Transaction costs** | The costs of *using the market* (not the purchase price): **search**, **negotiation/contracting**, **monitoring**, **enforcement**. |
| **Administrative costs** | The costs of organizing the same activity inside a hierarchy — the counterweight to transaction costs. |
| **Relative cost rule (Coase)** | Internalize an activity when its market transaction costs exceed its internal administrative costs; otherwise buy. |
| **External vs. internal transaction costs** | External = costs of dealing outside (largely set by the environment); internal = costs of organizing inside (rise with firm size). |

### Vertical Integration & Its Forms
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Vertical integration (VI)** | Ownership of vertically related activities; measured by value added ÷ sales revenue. |
| **Backward / forward integration** | Taking ownership upstream (own your inputs) / downstream (activities previously done by customers). |
| **Full / partial integration** | All of a stage's output transferred internally / stages not internally self-sufficient (net buyer or seller of the intermediate good). |
| **Technical economies** | Cost savings from physically integrating processes at one site — justify **co-location**, *not* common ownership. |
| **Minimum efficient scale (MES)** | The output level at which an activity reaches full scale efficiency; if your need is far below it, in-house production is inefficient. |
| **Taper integration** | Deliberately mixing in-house supply with outside suppliers/distributors — keeps internal units exposed to competition, adds flexibility. |
| **Virtual / hollow corporation** | A firm whose main role is coordinating a supplier network / the failure state where it outsources so much it can no longer adapt. |
| **Architectural vs. component capabilities** | Ability to design/coordinate the whole system vs. deep expertise in one part; the test for whether a heavy outsourcer has hollowed out. |

### The Transaction-Cost Trigger
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Transaction-specific investment** | An asset built for one counterparty, with little value elsewhere — the culprit that turns a competitive market into a dependent one. |
| **Bilateral monopoly** | One buyer, one seller — no equilibrium price; the outcome turns purely on relative bargaining power. |
| **Opportunism / hold-up** | Exploiting a counterparty's lock-in to extract better terms after the fact, once their assets are worthless outside the relationship. |
| **Incomplete contracts** | Contracts that cannot anticipate every future circumstance because the future is uncertain — why "just write a better contract" fails. |
| **Information asymmetry** | One party (usually the seller) holds private information the other lacks. |

### The Six Administrative Costs (the heart of Exercise 1)
| Term                                    | Definition                                                                                                                                                             |
| --------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Differences in optimal scale**        | If internal need is far below the adjacent activity's MES, in-house production is inefficient and the transaction costs avoided are trivial next to the scale penalty. |
| **Developing distinctive capabilities** | A specialist serving many customers learns faster than a captive internal unit — *unless* the capabilities are linked, when integration helps.                         |
| **Strategic dissimilarity**             | Vertically linked stages that need different systems, planning, control, HR, and top-management skills are costly to run together.                                     |
| **High- vs. low-powered incentives**    | Market interfaces create sharp profit incentives; internal transfer relationships mute them (consequences slow and undramatic).                                        |
| **Shared-service organization**         | An internal supplier forced to compete with outside providers, to restore incentive intensity inside an integrated firm.                                               |
| **Competitive effects**                 | Integration makes independent suppliers/customers less willing to deal with a firm they now see as a competitor (the realistic risk; monopoly-extension is rare).      |
| **Compounding risk**                    | A problem at any owned stage threatens production and profit at every other stage.                                                                                     |

### Designing Vertical Relationships
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Spot / long-term contract** | One-off market purchase / a specified series of transactions setting terms and responsibilities. |
| **Vendor partnership / relational contract** | Close, trust-based supplier relationship giving security + flexibility, sometimes with nothing written. |
| **Franchising** | Contract letting a franchisee produce/market under the franchiser's brand and system — corporate brand + local entrepreneurship + high-powered incentives. |
| **Allocation of risk / incentive structure** | The two design variables beyond make-or-buy: who bears downside, and how performance is made self-interested (often the promise of future business). |

### Related Corporate-Strategy Frameworks (invoked by the cases)
| Term                                        | Definition                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Economies of scope**                      | Cost advantage from applying one resource/capability across several products or markets (vs. economies of scale = volume of the *same* thing).                                                                                                                                                  |
| **Rumelt's four types**                     | Diversification by % revenue from the primary business + competency sharing: single (>95%) → dominant (70–95%) → related-constrained/linked (<70%, share competencies) → unrelated conglomerate.                                                                                                |
| **Diversification–performance inverted-U**  | Performance rises single → dominant → **peaks at related**, then falls into unrelated. Used to read Johnson Controls/Tyco and UPS.                                                                                                                                                              |
| **Build-Borrow-or-Buy (Capron & Mitchell)** | Close a resource gap: **Build** if internal resources are relevant (similar + superior); **Borrow** (contract/license) if tradable; Borrow-with-equity (alliance/JV) if closeness need is low; **Buy** (acquire) only under low relevance + low tradability + high closeness — the last resort. |
| **Porter's Diamond**                        | Why nations breed world-leading firms: factor conditions, demand conditions, **related/supporting industries (clusters)**, competitive intensity. Guardian is this "in the negative" (no domestic parts cluster).                                                                               |
| **CAGE distance**                           | Cultural, Administrative/political, Geographic, Economic distance — the costs/risks of foreign expansion; the Irish domicile (Johnson Controls) is an administrative-distance play.                                                                                                             |

## The Make-or-Buy Logic (section notes)

**The one rule.** Boundaries are set by relative cost: internalize when market transaction costs exceed internal administrative costs. Both sides have costs — never argue "markets have costs, so integrate." Markets *can* coordinate complex work (a builder subcontracting a plumber, electrician, joiner), so "firms exist because coordination is needed" is wrong; firms exist wherever coordinating through contracts costs more than through hierarchy.

**Why integration is triggered — the causal chain to memorize.** Technical economies force plants next to each other (steel → steel strip: hot-roll while still molten) → purpose-built, co-located assets → **transaction-specific investment** (worthless outside the relationship) → **bilateral monopoly** (no equilibrium price, only bargaining power) → **opportunism / hold-up** → market contracting becomes inefficient → integrate. Two traps: (1) technical economies justify **co-location, not common ownership** — ownership is answered only by transaction costs; (2) "just write a better contract" fails because the future is uncertain, so **contracts are inevitably incomplete**. Commodity inputs with thick markets (gold for jewelers, wheat for millers, steel strip for can-makers like Crown/Ball) get bought, not made.

**But integration has its own costs — the six administrative costs (pp. 348–353).** Integration avoids transaction costs but *creates* costs of administration. This table is the core of Exercise 1:

| Administrative cost | Mechanism | Anchor example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Differences in optimal scale** | Producing below MES costs more than the transaction costs avoided | FedEx buys 40,000 vans/yr vs. a 200,000 assembly MES → don't build. It's the *gap*, not size: a huge firm can still fail this test in one stage |
| **2. Developing distinctive capabilities** | Specialists serving many customers learn faster than captive units | Xerox/Kodak/Philips can't match EDS/IBM/Accenture in IT. **Counter-case (linked capabilities):** IBM mainframes (in-house semis/software), Wal-Mart (in-house IT/logistics) |
| **3. Managing strategically different businesses** | Different systems, planning, control, HR, top-management skills | Manufacturing+retailing integration is rare (Zara/Gucci vs. Wal-Mart, Gap, Carrefour); drove de-integration at Marriott, Whitbread, Scottish & Newcastle |
| **4. The incentive problem** | Internal transfers are low-powered; consequences slow and undramatic | An inefficient in-house fleet only slowly costs bonuses. Partial fix: **shared-service organizations** competing with outside suppliers |
| **5. Competitive effects** | Counterparties won't deal with a perceived competitor (monopoly-extension is rare — Standard Oil, Alcoa) | After Disney bought ABC, rival studios (Dreamworks) cooled on developing shows with ABC |
| **6. Flexibility** (both ways) | Markets win for demand uncertainty + new technical combinations; VI wins for system-wide adjustment | Markets: construction; contract-made iPod/Xbox/Dell. VI: American Apparel, Zara |
| **7. Compounding risk** | One owned stage's failure halts profit at all stages | 1998 GM brake-plant strike halted all 24 US assembly plants |

**The decision criteria (Table 13.1).** Favors **VI**: few firms in the adjacent stage; specific investments needed; large information asymmetries; taxed/regulated intermediate transactions; high uncertainty over the relationship's life; strategically *similar* stages. Favors **market/outsourcing**: dissimilar optimal scale; high need for continual capability investment; high need for entrepreneurial flexibility; highly uncertain demand.

**The single most testable point.** Even in the *same* industry, firms rationally integrate to *opposite* degrees, because they hold different resources and pursue different strategies — Zara far more than H&M/Gap; Armani more than Donna Karan; Starbucks owns its outlets while peers franchise; Capital One keeps IT in-house while most banks outsource. Any question implying one "correct" level of integration for an industry is testing whether you know industry conditions **underdetermine** the answer.

**Zara — the one case where integration wins despite paying every administrative cost.** 68% of Inditex sales; 2000–06: 30% sales growth, 11% margin, 29% ROAE. Design→store in **3 weeks** vs. a 6-month industry lag; ~40,000 designs/yr, ~¼ produced, ~half made in-house; Comditex supplies 40% of fabric mostly undyed so color changes late; stores order/receive twice weekly, dispatched in 8h; items ≤2 weeks in store. Integration wins because the coordination benefit (speed) outweighs the scale/dissimilarity/incentive/risk penalties — *in this industry, for this strategy*.

**Designing the relationship.** A continuum by commitment × formalization: spot → long-term contract → agency → franchise → vendor/relational partnership → JV → full integration. Spot works only with many players and no specific investment; long-term contracts protect specific investments but risk being too rigid *or* too loose; vendor partnerships (Toyota, M&S) trade on trust; franchising marries a big firm's brand/systems with local entrepreneurship + high-powered incentives. Two design variables beyond make-or-buy: **allocation of risk** (franchising loads it on the franchisee; oil-services shifting to driller equity stakes) and **incentive structure** (over-specification is costly — the $400 Navy toilet seat; the best incentive is often just the promise of future business).

## Articles Applied (Part 1 — identify which administrative cost the firm overcomes)

### 1. Amazon — Retail Ad Service
- **What happened:** Amazon opened the ad tech behind its own ~$50B ad business (3rd in US behind Google/Meta) for other retailers to run on their own sites, using models trained on two decades of shopping signals. It captures retail-media profit off its own property *and* gains rivals' data (flagged as FTC risk) — explicitly the AWS pattern (internal infrastructure later sold as a service).
- **VI connections:** This is **economies of scope / capability externalization**, not vertical integration — one resource applied to a second market at near-zero marginal cost. It's the **shared-service logic** taken a step further: the internal supplier becomes a standalone external business. The **competitive effect** appears — adopting retailers hand a competitor visibility into their demand (the Disney–ABC mechanism).
- **Follow-up questions:** Is calling this "forward integration" a category error, and why? If the same models are the shared resource, what stops rivals from refusing to adopt (the competitive-effects deterrent)? *Trap:* Amazon isn't buying retailers or a value-chain stage — the correct label is scope expansion, not integration.

### 2. Apple — Anthropic/OpenAI for Siri
- **What happened:** Apple is in talks to license LLMs for a rebuilt Siri, sidelining its in-house Foundation Models; it wants custom models trained to run on its Private Cloud Compute (for privacy). Anthropic was judged the best fit but talks stalled on price (a multibillion fee escalating yearly). The ~100-person foundation-models team reacted badly, with departures. Build/borrow/buy are all visible at once.
- **VI connections:** A **make-or-buy on a core input**. Build-Borrow-Buy predicts **borrow** — the resource is tradable and AI-lab integration capability is doubtful. Fails the **relevance** test (own models judged inferior). **Developing distinctive capabilities**: specialists (Anthropic) learn faster than a captive team. **Architectural vs. component**: keep the architecture (device integration, on-device models, privacy) while buying the component (the frontier model).
- **Follow-up questions:** Does "AI is strategically important" argue for ownership or only for control? Which Table 13.1 criterion (continual capability investment) actually points *away* from building here? *Trap:* strategic importance argues for control, not necessarily ownership.

### 3. Johnson Controls / Tyco
- **What happened:** A $14B merger ($30B+ sales) combining JC's HVAC with Tyco's security/fire lines; JC holders take ~56%; the firm keeps Tyco's Irish domicile (an **inversion**). Both had shrunk from conglomerates first (Tyco $41B→$10B); synergies were mostly tax + cost (effective rate 18–19% vs. JC's 29%).
- **VI connections:** **Rumelt** trajectory: unrelated → **related-constrained** (shared building-systems customer/channel). The **inverted-U traversed deliberately** — retreat from the unrelated end toward the related peak, then combine within it. **Economies of scope**; **taxes/regulations as a cost of contracting** driving the *legal* boundary (the inversion); activist investors = the principal–agent discipline.
- **Follow-up questions:** Is this firm more or less diversified after the deal — and why is "bigger" the wrong axis? What does the inversion tell you about which "boundary" (operational vs. legal) is being optimized? *Trap:* it's bigger **but more focused** — reading it as increasing diversification inverts the case.

### 4. Meta / Scale AI
- **What happened:** Meta took a 49% stake for $14.8B, with the cash going to Scale's shareholders (not the company); CEO Wang and staff moved to a Meta "superintelligence" lab. The sub-50% structure is attributed to regulatory caution after the Instagram/WhatsApp antitrust trial. Scale's customers include Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon, Nvidia, OpenAI.
- **VI connections:** An **equity alliance** on the continuum (partial ownership, "try before you buy"; cf. Coca-Cola/Monster 18.5%). **Why not acquire?** — the binding constraint is **regulatory**, not integration capability (a variant of the usual "acquisition is the last resort"). **Quasi-backward integration** into a training input. **Competitive effects (Disney–ABC)**: rival labs may cut Scale spend — the open question. **Acqui-hire**: the real asset is Wang + team (low tradability → equity).
- **Follow-up questions:** Why is calling this an "acquisition" wrong on the facts? Which framework (Build-Borrow-Buy) *fails* to predict this outcome, and what variable is it missing? *Trap:* 49% is deliberately below control; regulation isn't the only motive (talent + guaranteed supply stand alone).

### 5. Nvidia — DGX Cloud Lepton
- **What happened:** Nvidia launched a marketplace renting GPU servers directly from Nvidia, aggregating a dozen+ providers' inventory plus its own. Providers see it as competitive (it gives Nvidia direct customer relationships) but fear losing business to rivals who join if they abstain. **Parasail refused**; renting runs through an **Nvidia Cloud Account** (which owns the relationship). The DOJ has shown interest.
- **VI connections:** **Forward integration** — a supplier moving into its customers' stage. **Mutual encroachment**: clouds backward-integrate into chips while Nvidia forward-integrates into cloud — integration as strategic response, not just cost. **Competitive effects mostly *overridden*** by supplier power (only Parasail refused). **Taper integration** (own inventory + aggregate partners').
- **Follow-up questions:** Under what condition does the competitive-effects deterrent bite — and why does it *fail* here? Is Nvidia's "we merely connect partners to customers" a fact or a claim to evaluate against the account structure? *Trap:* it isn't Standard-Oil foreclosure — participation is voluntary.

### 6. Walmart / Dean Foods
- **What happened:** Grocers (Kroger, Walmart, Albertsons) built their own milk-bottling plants; Dean (the largest US processor) and Borden went bankrupt. Dean's Louisville plant sent 70% of output to 130 Walmart stores and prioritized Walmart; when prices fell, Dean held its DairyPure brand pricing and lost. Upstream, 8 co-ops marketed 54% of US milk; Walmart's Fort Wayne plant serves 600+ stores. Per-capita milk fell ~40% over 40 years.
- **VI connections:** **Backward integration** (definitional). **Transaction-specific investment + hold-up** running in Walmart's favor. **FedEx test — passing this time**: one plant serves 600+ stores, clearing MES (Scozzafava's "poor use of resources" argument failed on the numbers). **Low differentiation** as the enabling condition (shoppers stopped distinguishing store from premium milk; Dean's branding bet lost). **Loss leader**; **compounding risk** (Dean's plant-closure cascade → rerouting logjams). **Kroger** is the completed case (processes ~90% of its milk, even supplies Food Lion).
- **Follow-up questions:** Why does Walmart pass the FedEx test where FedEx itself fails it? Give the *two independent* causes of Dean's collapse. *Trap:* citing only backward integration *or* only secular demand decline is incomplete — and Dean's branded-price bet was its own error.

# Exercise 2 — Market Failure & Other Rationales (Stuckey & White, SMR 1993)

## Key Terms & Definitions

### Core Framing
| Term                                       | Definition                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Vertical integration (Stuckey & White)** | A means of coordinating adjacent stages of an industry chain, used only when ordinary bilateral trading doesn't work well.                                                                                                                                                           |
| **Bilateral trading**                      | Ordinary arm's-length buying/selling between two adjacent stages; integration is warranted only when this *isn't* good enough.                                                                                                                                                       |
| **The Table 1 trade-off**                  | Four conflicting buckets: **setup costs** (capital, systems, training), **transaction costs** (info, legal, sales/purchasing), **risk** (adverse prices, foreclosure, lost market signals), **coordination effectiveness** (run lengths, inventory, utilization, delivery, quality). |
| **Governing rule**                         | Do not vertically integrate unless it is absolutely necessary to create or protect value — the burden of proof is on integration.                                                                                                                                                    |

### Vertical Market Failure (VMF)
| Term                                                | Definition                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Vertical market failure**                         | A market "fails" when transactions are too risky *and* the contracts that would manage the risk are too costly or impossible to write.                                                               |
| **Three structural preconditions**                  | (1) small numbers of buyers/sellers, (2) high asset specificity/durability/intensity, (3) frequent transactions — no one alone is sufficient; together they signal VMF.                              |
| **Bilateral monopoly / oligopoly**                  | One (or a few) buyers facing one (or a few) sellers — price is set by unstable bargaining power, not supply/demand.                                                                                  |
| **Asset specificity**                               | Value that depends on one relationship: **site** (co-located), **technical** (equipment usable only by one counterparty), **human-capital** (skills tied to one relationship).                       |
| **Asset intensity / durability**                    | Intensity magnifies the *cost* of any disruption; durability extends the *time horizon* over which lock-in risk applies.                                                                             |
| **Pre- vs. post-investment flip**                   | Before assets are committed, many parties bid competitively; after, specific assets lock the pair into a bilateral monopoly. **VMF is created by the investment itself**, not the initial structure. |
| **Uncertainty / bounded rationality / opportunism** | The three "soft" amplifiers: can't foresee conditions (Simon) → can't write complete contracts → parties cheat when it pays (Williamson).                                                            |
| **R&D / information market failure**                | Markets for information fail — the buyer can't value it before seeing it, the seller can't reveal it without giving it away.                                                                         |

### Market Power, Life Cycle & Quasi-Integration
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Economic surplus** | Return *above full costs including a fair return on capital* — the true target of integration, not value added or customer proximity. |
| **Value added** | Stage cost + surplus, less bought-in inputs; a stage can add huge value and still earn a poor return (transport in the cement chain). |
| **Barriers to entry (via integration)** | When incumbents are integrated, entrants must enter *every* stage, raising required capital and MES. |
| **Price discrimination (anti-resale)** | Forward-integrate into the price-sensitive segment so you sell to yourself there, killing the resale that would collapse two-tier pricing. |
| **Market-development integration** | Forward-integrating to build a young market that doesn't exist yet — a special case of VMF (authors call it *overrated*). |
| **Free rider** | A competitor who captures the benefit of another firm's market-development spending without paying for it. |
| **Quasi-integration** | Long-term contracts, JVs/alliances, licensing, asset ownership, franchising — most of the protection at a fraction of the capital/rigidity. |
| **Market for corporate control** | The takeover/raider market, which pressures over-integrated firms to restructure. |

## The Logic (section notes)

**Default = don't integrate.** VI is expensive, hard to reverse, and management's track record is poor. There are exactly **four legitimate reasons**, and reason 1 dominates:
1. **Vertical market failure** — trading is too risky and contracts can't fix it. *The big one.*
2. **Defending against market power** in an adjacent stage.
3. **Creating/exploiting market power** — raising entry barriers or enabling price discrimination.
4. **Industry life cycle** — build a young market, or fill gaps as independents exit a declining one.

**Reason 1 — VMF (the priority concept).** Three structural preconditions (small numbers; high asset specificity/durability/intensity; frequent transactions) amplified by three soft factors (uncertainty, bounded rationality, opportunism).
- In **small-numbers** markets, price/volume are set by the unstable **balance of bargaining power**, not supply and demand. Bilateral oligopoly is uniquely hard: 3×3 players compete the surplus away to customers; horizontal fixes are illegal, so players **merge vertically** (6→3) to restore a game small enough to behave rationally.
- **The pre/post-investment flip (key mechanism):** the bauxite market looks competitive *before* investment (many miners/refiners bid); *after* the mine and refinery are built, asset specificity (bauxite costly to ship, refining strips 60–70% of volume, refineries tuned to their own bauxite's chemistry) locks the pair together → ~90% of bauxite moves under VI or JVs. **VMF is created by the investment, not the initial structure.**
- **Figure 2 read-off:** integration is warranted in exactly one cell — **high asset specificity × frequent transactions** (bauxite, specialized auto components). Everywhere else a contract does the job (frequent + non-specific = groceries; infrequent + specific = a one-off construction contract).
- **Information/R&D markets fail** because the good *is* information — **EMI/CAT scanner:** EMI invented it but lacked distribution/service assets; GE and Siemens, already integrated, reverse-engineered it, supported it better, and took the market.

**Figure 1 — market structure (sellers × buyers).** The high-trading-risk zone is the bilateral-monopoly/oligopoly corner; the edges are power-asymmetry problems (reason 2), not market failure (reason 1):

| | One buyer | Few buyers | Many buyers |
|---|---|---|---|
| **One seller** | High trading risk | High trading risk | Sellers dominate |
| **Few sellers** | High trading risk | High trading risk | Sellers dominate |
| **Many sellers** | Buyers dominate | Buyers dominate | No one dominates |

**Figure 2 — the coordination-mechanism map (transaction frequency × asset specificity).** Integration is warranted in exactly one cell:

| | Low specificity/durability/intensity | High specificity/durability/intensity |
|---|---|---|
| **Seldom transacted** | Standardized contracts (office lease, credit sale) | Detailed one-off contract (major public construction) |
| **Often transacted** | Standardized transactions (groceries) | **Vertical integration** (bauxite, specialized auto components) |

**Reason 2 — Defending against market power.** If an adjacent stage earns abnormal returns, entering it is tempting — but two traps: **paying away the surplus** in the acquisition premium (one concrete maker paid ~20× cash flow against a ~10% cost of capital — value destroyed), and **assuming insider advantage** (skills differ sharply across stages, so outsiders often make better entrants). Test: can you integrate at a cost *below* the value captured? Usually not.

**Reason 3 — Creating/exploiting market power.** *Entry barriers:* when incumbents are integrated, entrants must enter every stage (upstream aluminum: 6 integrated majors, thin intermediate markets, ~$2B efficient-scale entry). *Price discrimination:* forward-integrate into the price-sensitive segment to kill resale (aluminum into can stock, cable, auto castings). Both are expensive and rarely permanent.

**Reason 4 — Industry life cycle.** *Young industry:* forward-integrate to build a market — works only if you block free-riders (proprietary tech/brand), capture surplus for years, and beat substitutes. *Declining industry:* integrate to fill gaps as independents exit and survivors concentrate (Culbro, cigars).

**When NOT to integrate — spurious reasons.** Over-integration comes from bad justifications and ignoring quasi-integration.
- *"It smooths earnings"* — invalid: contiguous stages are **positively correlated** (same end-demand — zinc mining + smelting), and shareholders can diversify themselves; smoothing benefits managers, not shareholders.
- *"It assures supply/outlets"* — valid **only** where foreclosure or unfair prices are symptoms of real VMF or structural power. In an efficient market you can always trade at the market price; owning it just tempts you to mis-set transfer prices. Test: are **both sides** competitively structured? If yes, integration adds nothing.
- *"Capture more value / get closer to the customer"* — the correlation of profitability with value added or customer proximity is **weak and inconsistent**. What matters is **economic surplus**, not value added: in the Australian cement/concrete chain, transport is the biggest value-added stage and doesn't cover its cost of capital, while fly ash is the smallest and earns high surplus. **Prescription: integrate into the highest-*surplus* stages** — knowing those are protected by entry barriers you must scale for less than the surplus is worth.

**Quasi-integration — usually the right answer.** JVs/alliances preserve identity and cut antitrust exposure (most upstream aluminum plants are now JVs); **asset ownership** — own only the critical specific assets (tools, dies, jigs, molds), contract out the rest; **franchising** — control distribution without the capital, the right to cancel enforces quality; **licensing** — the default for technology unless the innovation needs specialized complementary assets whose owners have power → then integrate (Figure 4, after Teece; the EMI mistake).

**Strategy changes when structure changes** — chiefly the number of buyers/sellers and the importance of specific assets. **Oil 1966→1985:** seller concentration fell (top-4 59%→26%), refineries went crude-specific → flexible, so coordination shifted from ~all VI/10-year contracts to ~50% spot with VI declining. The late-1980s disintegration trend came from unwinding spurious integration, the market for corporate control, and structural changes making trading safer — *but* the authors warn against fashion: don't disintegrate genuinely VMF-critical activities, and "cooperative" sole suppliers rediscover their muscle once rivals are gone. **Decisions must be analytical, not fashionable.**

**The decision tree (Figure 6), built so full integration is the last box:** Is the stage attractive to you (attractive industry + skills match)? If no → is it subject to VMF? If no VMF → does owning it help defend/build/exploit market power? If no → **exit the stage**. In every "yes" branch, first ask **would quasi-integration suffice?** — adopt that if so; only otherwise become/remain at least partially integrated.

## Articles Applied (Part 2)

### 1. Amazon — custom AI chips (Trainium)
- **What happened:** Amazon is building silicon to loosen Nvidia's grip on a $100B+ market and cut supplier power (Nvidia was rationing supply). It acquired **Annapurna (~$350M)**, then built Nitro→Graviton→Inferentia→Trainium; Trainium2 = 4× perf, 3× memory, chips/box cut 8→2. The binding constraint is **software** (Neuron SDK immature vs. Nvidia's toolchain). Anthropic committed to Trainium after ~$8B from Amazon. Design is in-house; **fabrication is outsourced to TSMC (fabless)**.
- **Market-failure / VI connections:** **Backward integration to reduce supplier power** (the cleanest stated motive in either set). **Fabless = MES applied per stage**: integrate design (clears scale), outsource fabrication (fab MES is far beyond even Amazon's volume). **Architectural vs. component**: value sits in the software layer where Nvidia leads. **Buy-then-build** (Annapurna). **Equity alliance flowing downstream** (Anthropic as a guaranteed anchor customer). **Mutual encroachment** (mirror of the Part-1 Nvidia case).
- **Follow-up questions:** Why does the make-or-buy answer differ between chip *design* and *fabrication*? Is this a bid for competitive advantage or just cost + supply security? *Trap:* "Amazon is integrating into chips" is only half true — design, not manufacture.

### 2. Apple / Globalstar — escalation along the continuum
- **What happened:** iPhone 14 Emergency SOS runs on satellites; **Apple funds 95% of Globalstar's capex** while Globalstar **commits 85% of current and future capacity**; Apple then invested **~$1.5B for a ~20% stake**. Both sides make purpose-built specific investments.
- **VI connections:** The clearest "relationship *moving*" case — supply contract → funded capex + capacity commitment → minority equity, each step rising as the specific investment grows. **Transaction-specific investment both directions**; **bilateral monopoly** (no market price). **Why stop at 20%?** — owning a regulated satellite operator is a strategically dissimilar business. **Hold-up → equity as the answer** (shared upside; cf. Commonwealth Bank/EDS). **Alternatives (Starlink, Iridium)** are Apple's bargaining power.
- **Follow-up questions:** Why is escalating commitment (not spot, not acquisition) the theory-predicted response to two-sided specific investment? What does the 20% ceiling tell you about strategic dissimilarity? *Trap:* the 19% stock drop on a Musk post measures substitution risk, not deal quality.

### 3. Apple — component sourcing
- **What happened:** Apple Watch modems are moving Intel → **MediaTek** (supplier switching, nothing crosses Apple's boundary); separately Apple is building an **in-house iPhone modem** (real backward integration) but *not* for the Watch. The satellite feature exists to restore an upgrade reason and ladder prices (SE $249 / Series $399 / Ultra $799).
- **VI connections:** **Supplier switching ≠ integration** — the cheapest answer to supplier power is a credible second source, not ownership. **Selective backward integration**, decided per product line, not per firm.
- **Follow-up questions:** Which of these two moves actually crosses Apple's boundary, and which doesn't? Why integrate the iPhone modem but buy the Watch's? *Trap:* MediaTek replacing Intel is not VI; only the in-house modem is.

### 4. CoreWeave / Core Scientific
- **What happened:** A **long-term contract converting into ownership**: a 12-year, $3.5B lease for 200MW of power → a rejected $5.75/share bid → acquisition talks (analysts saw $30+). **Microsoft = 62% of CoreWeave's 2024 revenue.** Core Scientific's bitcoin-mining infrastructure redeploys to AI (general-purpose power/cooling/land).
- **VI connections:** **LT contract → ownership**, exactly Grant's point that 12-year contracts can't anticipate everything in a volatile market. **Transaction-specific investment** (site/tenant/term). **Bargaining priced** ($5.75 vs. $30+ for the same assets a year apart). **Customer concentration** (62% Microsoft) is the mirror exposure. **Asset redeployment / economies of scope** on the *infrastructure* (why Canaan's chip pivot failed while this succeeds).
- **Follow-up questions:** Why does extreme uncertainty over a 12-year horizon push toward ownership (Table 13.1)? Is the $30+ figure a fact or a forecast? *Trap:* it stacks two *alternative* valuation methods and is a sell-side estimate, not a market price.

### 5. UPS Healthcare / Labport
- **What happened:** UPS is building a health-care logistics business ($10B, ~1/10 of revenue, target 2× by 2026; high-teens margin vs. ~10%). **Labport** offers 100k sq ft of labs by the Louisville hub, pre-leased 10+ years; anchor **HealthTrackRx relocated its labs adjacent** to UPS (which moves ~90% of its samples). Kit assembly (now vendor-split) would be the genuinely vertical step. The unit runs separate branding, leadership, even uniform color.
- **VI connections:** **Related-constrained diversification** + **economies of scope** (the hub monetized twice). **Co-location WITHOUT common ownership — Grant's exact point**: HealthTrackRx moved next door on a *lease, not a merger* (contrast steel/strip, where co-location *did* force integration — differing on asset specificity and number of counterparties). **Build-Borrow-Buy → buy** (target unreachable organically → European acquisitions). Brand/unit separation **mitigates** strategic dissimilarity.
- **Follow-up questions:** Why does co-location lead to integration in steel/strip but only a lease here? Is UPS entering health care, or an adjacent service market? *Trap:* it's diversification into an adjacent *service market* on a shared asset base — only the (hypothetical) kit assembly is vertical.

### 6. American Giant / Walmart
- **What happened:** Walmart sells US-made $12.98 tees; the enabler is **Walmart's scale + guaranteed orders**, confirmed as **noncancelable purchase orders** — without them, suppliers wouldn't have invested in the automation that cut costs. American Giant owns one sewing facility, part-owns an LA one built for this order, and contracts the rest; Walmart shared **demand forecasts by price point**.
- **VI connections:** **The hold-up problem solved by contract design** — the noncancelable PO is the security device (Grant's rationale for long-term contracts, stated by a practitioner). **The Walmart pair:** the *same* buyer power *expropriated* Dean (Walmart wanted a commodity it could make itself) but *underwrote* American Giant (a capability it won't build). Buyer power expropriates or guarantees depending on **whether the buyer intends to integrate**. **Taper integration** on the supplier side; differentiation via **enforced certification** (Walmart bars "American Made"/flag on imports).
- **Follow-up questions:** Why does Walmart use identical buyer power to destroy one supplier and underwrite another? What made the noncancelable PO necessary (which administrative/transaction problem)? *Trap:* the cause named by the participant is contractual commitment, not tariffs (the article rejects the tariff explanation).

### 7. Starbucks — pared hedging
- **What happened:** Starbucks cut fixed-price coffee contracts to <$200M (from $1B in 2019) as prices hit a 13-year high. It buys **~3% of world coffee** for 40,000 cafés and **still owns no farms**; it runs procurement through the wholly owned **Starbucks Coffee Trading Company** with 56 tier-one suppliers, using **price-to-be-fixed** contracts (quantity/delivery set, price later) plus derivatives. Fixed-price contracts, derivatives, and inventory are all at multi-year lows at once.
- **VI connections:** **Governance by contract where the market is thick** — many suppliers, transparent prices, low switching costs; **volume alone doesn't justify backward integration** (like flour millers and wheat). **Allocation of risk** across three instruments; **spot vs. LT** (price-to-be-fixed is a hybrid — long-term on volume, spot-like on price). **Backward integration into the *function* (trading), not the *stage* (farming).** **Uncertainty's limits:** this is *price* uncertainty in a liquid market → financial instruments beat ownership; not all uncertainty pushes toward integration.
- **Follow-up questions:** Why doesn't buying 3% of world output justify owning farms? Which *kind* of uncertainty pushes toward integration and which toward contracts/derivatives? *Trap:* the "approach unchanged" claim sits against three simultaneous multi-year lows — contradicted by the article's own figures.

### 8. Guardian Bikes
- **What happened:** Guardian moved children's-bike production to Indiana in **2022 for quality control, not cost** — mass-retail bikes ship unassembled, leaving no oversight of final assembly. Chinese parts are ~90% of cost, target ~20%; it's making frames in-house but there's **no domestic supplier cluster** for hubs/cranks. It asked its Brazilian rim supplier to build nearby; MES splits the industry (Prevelo can't justify domestic factories, Giant at 4M bikes/yr can); the learning curve ran 100 → 2,700 bikes/day.
- **VI connections:** **VI for quality assurance** — the *incomplete-contracting / monitoring-cost* branch of TCE (distinct from the specific-investment branch). **Reshoring is activity-specific** (the 90%→20% target measures the *chain*, not the *product*). **Absent supporting cluster** = Porter's Diamond in the negative. **Co-location invitation, not acquisition** (the rim supplier — cf. HealthTrackRx). **MES** decides who reshores. **Learning-curve cost** of internalizing a never-performed activity.
- **Follow-up questions:** Which branch of transaction-cost economics (monitoring vs. specific investment) explains a *quality*-driven integration? Why can Giant reshore where Prevelo can't? *Trap:* tariffs didn't cause it — the 2022 factory opened for quality; tariffs are only something Riley *hopes* will help.

## Cross-Case Contrasts (highest-value synthesis)

- **Same buyer, opposite governance (Walmart pair):** Walmart *expropriated* Dean (wanted a commodity it could make itself) but *underwrote* American Giant (wanted a capability it won't build). Buyer power expropriates or guarantees depending on intent to integrate.
- **Co-location ≠ ownership:** steel/strip co-located *and* integrated (specific assets, one counterparty); HealthTrackRx/UPS and Guardian/rim-supplier co-locate *without* ownership (general-purpose assets, many counterparties). The ownership question is always answered separately, by transaction costs.
- **MES decides how far up the chain you go:** Amazon integrates chip *design* but not *fabrication*; Walmart clears MES for bottling; Prevelo can't reshore, Giant can. The recurring binding constraint is *scale*, not strategic importance.
- **The competitive-effects deterrent is conditional:** it bites only when the counterparty has alternatives — overridden for Nvidia (holds the scarce input) and Walmart (holds the scarce demand), an open question for Meta/Scale.
- **Escalation up the continuum is triggered by specific investment:** Apple/Globalstar and CoreWeave/Core Scientific both climb from contract → deeper commitment → equity/ownership once one party must build something worthless outside the relationship; whether they stop at equity or go to ownership depends on strategic dissimilarity.
- **"Not vertical integration" traps:** Amazon Retail Ad (scope expansion), Meta/Scale (equity alliance, not acquisition), Apple/MediaTek (supplier switching), UPS (adjacent-service diversification). Naming the *wrong* concept — right-sounding but incorrect — is the classic exam error.
- **Uncertainty is not one variable:** *price* uncertainty in a liquid market (Starbucks) is handled by contracts/derivatives; *technology/demand* uncertainty over a long horizon (CoreWeave's 12 years) is what actually pushes toward ownership.
